### Detailed Interpretation

Bernadette Park and Charles Judd's 1990 paper, "Measures and models of perceived group variability," is a foundational work in social cognition that systematically investigates how people perceive the diversity within social groups. The paper's primary contribution is the identification and empirical distinction of two separate ways people conceptualize group variability.

**Two Forms of Perceived Variability**

According to analyses of the paper, Park and Judd proposed and tested two distinct forms of perceived variability:

1.  **Perceived Dispersion:** This refers to how much individual group members are seen to deviate or spread out from the group's central tendency (the average or typical member). A group perceived as having low dispersion is one where members are seen as being "tightly bunched" around the mean.
2.  **Stereotypicality:** This measures the extent to which a group is perceived as conforming to its stereotype. It reflects how well the group as a whole fits the perceiver's pre-existing stereotypical beliefs.

The research demonstrated that these two concepts are empirically different, and both showed evidence of the "out-group homogeneity effect," where people tend to see out-groups as less variable than their own in-group.

**Models of Variability Judgments**

A key part of the analysis involved exploring what cognitive information people draw upon when making these variability judgments. The authors found that judgments of a group's variability were not predicted by the variance of specific examples of group members that a person might recall. Instead, the process differed for in-groups versus out-groups:

*   **In-Group Variability:** When judging their own group, people's perception of variability was predicted by how much a retrieved set of members differed from the overall group mean. Furthermore, an individual's own perceived difference from the in-group mean was also a significant predictor of their judgment of the group's overall variability.
*   **Out-Group Variability:** These predictors did not apply when individuals judged out-groups.

A second study reported in the paper used "talk-aloud protocols" to further probe these cognitive processes. The results showed that when thinking about their in-group's variability, participants were more likely to mention themselves and various sub-groups. Conversely, when judging an out-group, they were more likely to discuss specific instances or examples of the group. This suggests that in-group perception is more complex and structured, involving the self and subgroups, while out-group perception may rely on simpler, exemplar-based representations.

In summary, Park and Judd's work provided a more nuanced understanding of perceived group variability by defining its distinct components and revealing the different cognitive models people use when assessing their own groups versus others. It established that complex group representations are associated with higher perceived variability and that the perception of the in-group is more diversified than that of the out-group.
