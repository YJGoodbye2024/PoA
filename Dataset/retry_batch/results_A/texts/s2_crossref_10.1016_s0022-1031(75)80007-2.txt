## 1. Official Abstract
"Two experiments are reported which test predictions from social facilitation and objective self-awareness theories of audience effects upon individual performance. In the first experiment, subjects performed a learning task (mirror drawing) either alone or before a non-evaluative audience, and either with or without the presence of a mirror. The presence of the mirror inhibited learning, while the presence of the audience had no effect. In the second experiment, subjects performed the same task either alone, or before an evaluative audience, or before a non-evaluative audience. The evaluative audience inhibited learning, while the non-evaluative audience had no significant effect. Motivational mechanisms are postulated to account for these findings."

### **Detailed Interpretation**

The 1975 paper by John M. Innes and Roger F. Young is a significant contribution to the fields of social facilitation and self-awareness. Its primary goal was to experimentally distinguish between three potential causes of performance changes in the presence of others: the mere presence of an audience, the fear of being judged (evaluation apprehension), and a heightened state of objective self-awareness. The study is frequently cited for its finding that the *potential for evaluation* is a more critical factor in altering performance on a learning task than the mere presence of passive observers.

The research comprised two main experiments using a mirror-drawing task, which is a complex learning activity.

*   **Experiment 1: Objective Self-Awareness vs. Mere Presence:** This experiment tested the theory of objective self-awareness, which posits that stimuli like mirrors or audiences make individuals focus attention inward, comparing their actual self to an ideal standard. This self-evaluation can be disruptive to complex tasks. The results showed that the presence of a mirror inhibited learning, but a non-evaluative audience did not produce a significant effect. This supported the idea that objective self-awareness, induced by the mirror, was a source of performance disruption.

*   **Experiment 2: Evaluation Apprehension:** This experiment introduced the variable of an *evaluative* audience. The findings were clear: an audience that was explicitly evaluating the participant significantly inhibited learning, whereas a non-evaluative audience had no such impact. This result strongly supported the theory that evaluation apprehension, rather than just the presence of others, is a key driver of social inhibition on novel tasks.

In essence, the paper argues that when individuals perform a task, the presence of others who are perceived as evaluators creates a state of anxiety and heightened self-consciousness that impairs the learning process. Later research builds on this foundation, noting that an audience stimulates a "drive state" in a performer, which increases energy and self-awareness. This heightened self-awareness can create a feedback loop where individuals become more sensitive to the gap between their current actions and desired performance, which can be either helpful or, as in this case, disruptive. The paper's findings helped refine social facilitation theory by demonstrating that not all audiences are equal; their perceived role as evaluators is crucial.
