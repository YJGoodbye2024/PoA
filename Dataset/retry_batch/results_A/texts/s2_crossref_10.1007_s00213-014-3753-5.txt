## 1. Official Abstract
"**RATIONAL**E:
Stress affects flow-experience, but the mediating psychobiological mechanisms remain unknown. Previous studies showed an association between flow-experience and endogenous cortisol levels, suggesting an inverted, u-shaped relation between flow-experience and cortisol. However, these studies could not exclude effects of other stress factors.

**OBJECTIVES**:
The aim of this experiment was, therefore, to test the isolated effect of cortisol on flow-experience, independent of concomitant physiological and psychological stress responses, via controlled administration of exogenous cortisol.

**METHODS**:
Sixty-four young healthy subjects (32 males, 32 females) participated in the experiment. According to a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, cross-over design, they received 20 mg oral cortisol on 1 day and placebo on the other day, respectively, with a time distance of 1 week between the experimental days. One hour after cortisol administration, participants engaged in the computer game Pacman. Pacman was delivered in five blocks of randomly differing difficulty levels. One block lasted 5 min. At the end of each block, participants rated flow-experience by the Flow Short Scale. Data was analyzed with hierarchical linear modeling. Subjects were not able to predict whether the pill they received contained cortisol or placebo.

**RESULTS**:
Overall, results revealed a negative effect of oral 20 mg cortisol on flow-experience, with no differences between males and females.

**CONCLUSIONS**:
This study is the first to show that exogenous cortisol in a dose corresponding to a severe stressor impairs flow-experience. The observed negative effect of high cortisol dosage on experienced flow underlines recent findings of an inverted u-shaped relationship between cortisol and flow."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis

### **Detailed Interpretation**

The 2015 paper by Peifer, Schächinger, Engeser, and Antoni is a significant contribution to understanding the psychophysiological underpinnings of the "flow" state—a state of deep absorption and enjoyment in an activity. The study's primary goal was to isolate and examine the specific effect of the stress hormone cortisol on the experience of flow.

Prior research had established a connection between stress and flow, suggesting a complex, inverted U-shaped relationship where moderate levels of arousal and cortisol might enhance flow, while excessively high or low levels could inhibit it. However, these studies couldn't separate the effect of cortisol from other psychological and physiological components of the stress response.

To address this gap, the researchers designed a rigorous experiment. They administered a 20 mg dose of hydrocortisone (a synthetic cortisol) to participants, a dosage intended to simulate the level of cortisol released during a significant stress event, such as a parachute jump. In a controlled, double-blind setting, participants received either the cortisol pill or a placebo and then played the video game "Pacman" to induce a state of flow.

The study's key finding was that the administration of a high dose of exogenous cortisol had a clear negative impact on the participants' reported flow-experience. This result was crucial because it demonstrated that high levels of cortisol, in and of themselves, can impair the ability to enter and maintain a flow state. This finding lends strong support to the hypothesis of an inverted U-shaped relationship between cortisol and flow. While moderate stress-induced arousal may be beneficial for achieving flow, the high levels of cortisol associated with severe stress are detrimental.

In essence, the experiment confirmed that while some level of challenge and arousal is necessary for flow, the hormonal signature of an intense stress response actively works against it. This research helps to clarify the physiological conditions that are conducive or obstructive to this highly sought-after state of optimal experience.
