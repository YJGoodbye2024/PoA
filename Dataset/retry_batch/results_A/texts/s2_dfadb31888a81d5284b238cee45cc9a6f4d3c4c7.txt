## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible, allowing for a detailed synopsis organized by its original sections.

### Introduction
The authors introduce the concept of self-monitoring, which describes how much individuals control the image they present in social situations. High self-monitors are adept at adjusting their behavior to fit social cues, while low self-monitors tend to act more consistently with their internal dispositions. The paper also discusses nonconscious mimicry, often called the "chameleon effect," where individuals unintentionally copy the mannerisms of their interaction partners, a behavior linked to fostering rapport and liking. The central research question is whether the strategic social behavior of high self-monitors can occur nonconsciously. The authors propose that high self-monitors, who are chronically motivated to affiliate, might use mimicry as an automatic, nonconscious strategy to build rapport when situational cues suggest an affiliation is desirable.

### Study 1: Mimicry of a Peer Versus a Nonpeer
Study 1 aimed to test whether high self-monitors would nonconsciously mimic a peer more than a nonpeer, as a peer represents a more relevant target for affiliation.

*   **Method:** Participants were pre-screened as either high or low self-monitors. They were led to believe they would interact with another participant (who was actually a confederate) of a different status: a high school student, a fellow undergraduate (peer), or a graduate student. During a session where they described photos to each other, the confederate consistently shook her foot. The interaction was videotaped, and coders measured the amount of time the participant spent shaking their own foot. After the interaction, participants were probed for any suspicion or awareness of the confederate's repetitive mannerisms or their own mimicry.

*   **Results:** The results showed that high self-monitors significantly increased their foot-shaking behavior when interacting with a peer compared to a nonpeer (high school or graduate student). Low self-monitors, in contrast, showed no significant difference in their mimicry across the three conditions. Post-experiment questionnaires confirmed that participants were not consciously aware of the confederate's mannerisms or their own mimicking behavior, supporting the nonconscious nature of the effect.

### Study 2: Mimicry of a Leader Versus a Worker
Study 2 was designed to further explore the strategic nature of nonconscious mimicry by placing participants in a situation with a clear power dynamic.

*   **Method:** High and low self-monitors were told they would be working on a task with a partner (a confederate). They were assigned roles as either the "leader" or the "worker," with the leader having the authority to direct the task and evaluate the worker. This created a situation where the worker had a clear motive to affiliate with the leader. During a preliminary interaction, the confederate consistently touched her face. The amount of face-touching by the participant was measured.

*   **Results:** High self-monitors who were assigned the "worker" role mimicked the confederate's face-touching significantly more than high self-monitors assigned the "leader" role. This suggests they nonconsciously used mimicry to build rapport with someone who had power over them. Again, low self-monitors mimicked to the same degree regardless of whether they were in the leader or worker role.

### General Discussion
The authors conclude that the two studies provide strong evidence that self-monitoring can operate outside of conscious awareness. High self-monitors appear to automatically use nonconscious mimicry as an affiliation strategy when situational cues—such as interacting with a peer or someone in a superior role—trigger their chronic goal to get along with others. This extends the traditional understanding of self-monitoring, which has largely focused on conscious and deliberate self-presentation. The findings demonstrate that high self-monitors have a nonconscious "tool" for facilitating social interactions. The research integrates the literature on automaticity and self-monitoring, showing that a personality trait can predict who will nonconsciously pursue a goal in specific, goal-relevant situations.
