## 1. Official Abstract
> In this article processes by which causal arguments effect change in established beliefs were explored. The hypothesis that explanation availability mediates belief change in response to causal arguments was tested in 2 experiments. Persuasive communications used causal evidence, noncausal (statistical) evidence, or both to argue that AIDS is not transmissible by casual contact. Results supported the authors' hypothesis. Causal arguments produced the greatest belief change, with the effect mediated by explanation availability. Causal arguments were also less subject to evaluation bias, consistent with the view that they are difficult to refute without possessing or creating an alternative causal explanation. The results also suggest that causal arguments may be especially useful in teaching new information.

### Detailed Interpretation

The 1996 paper by M. P. Slusher and C. A. Anderson is a significant work in the psychology of persuasion that investigates *why* certain arguments are more effective than others at changing deeply held beliefs. The central thesis is that arguments explaining the **cause** of a phenomenon are more persuasive than arguments that merely present **statistical** evidence. The authors posited that the effectiveness of causal arguments is mediated by "explanation availability"—the ease with which an individual can generate and understand a causal story for why something is true.

To test this, the researchers conducted two experiments centered on the belief that AIDS could be transmitted through casual contact. Participants were presented with different types of persuasive messages:
*   **Causal Evidence:** Arguments that explained the biological mechanisms of the HIV virus and why it could not survive or be transmitted through casual contact.
*   **Non-causal (Statistical) Evidence:** Arguments that presented statistical information, such as studies showing a lack of transmission among family members living with an AIDS patient.
*   **Combined Evidence:** A message containing both causal and non-causal arguments.

The study yielded two primary findings:

1.  **Causal Arguments Are More Persuasive:** The results clearly showed that causal arguments produced the greatest change in participants' beliefs. People were more convinced by explanations of *why* AIDS was not spread by casual contact than by statistics showing it was not. This effect was found to persist even weeks after the initial message. The authors concluded that this is because causal arguments directly influence a person's underlying theories about how the world works.

2.  **The Role of Explanation Availability and Evaluation Bias:** The study confirmed that the power of causal arguments lies in their ability to make an explanation readily available to the individual. When a person understands the mechanism behind a fact, it becomes easier to accept. Furthermore, Slusher and Anderson found that causal arguments are less vulnerable to "evaluation bias." This means that individuals who hold a contrary belief find it more difficult to refute a causal explanation compared to statistical data, which can be more easily dismissed as flawed, unrepresentative, or simply an exception to their personally held rule.

In conclusion, Slusher and Anderson's research demonstrates that to effectively change beliefs or teach new information, it is more powerful to explain the "why" (causality) than to simply present the "what" (statistics). This principle has been influential in subsequent research on health communication, risk messaging, and the psychology of persuasion.
