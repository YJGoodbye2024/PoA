## 1. Official Abstract
"Despite an abundance of organizational research on how contextual and individual difference factors impact well-being, little research has examined whether individuals themselves can take an active role in enhancing their own well-being. The current study assessed the effectiveness of two simple, self-guided workplace interventions (‘‘gratitude’’ and ‘‘social connectedness’’) in impacting well-being. Sixty-seven university employees participated in one of the two self-guided interventions for 2 weeks and completed self-report measures prior to the intervention, immediately following the intervention, and one-month post-intervention. Partially supporting hypotheses, the gratitude intervention resulted in significant increases in positive affective well-being and self-reported gratitude but not did significantly impact negative affective well-being or self-reported social connectedness. The social connectedness exercise did not significantly impact any of those four outcomes. However, both interventions related to a reduction in workplace absence due to illness. The study suggests that self-guided, positive psychology interventions (particularly gratitude) hold potential for enhancing employee well-being."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on the abstract and analysis from related academic sources.

**Introduction & Purpose**

The study addresses a gap in organizational research, which has historically focused on "top-down" factors influencing employee well-being, such as contextual and individual differences. The authors aimed to investigate whether employees could proactively improve their own well-being through simple, self-guided positive psychology interventions. The research evaluated two specific interventions: a "gratitude" exercise and a "social connectedness" exercise, assessing their impact on various well-being metrics. This approach is significant as it explores low-cost, easily implementable strategies for organizations to support employee wellness.

**Methods**

The study involved 67 university employees who were divided into two groups, each participating in one of the self-guided interventions for a period of two weeks. To measure the effects of the interventions, the researchers collected self-reported data at three points in time: before the intervention began, immediately after the two-week period, and at a one-month follow-up. The outcome measures included positive and negative affective well-being, self-reported gratitude, and social connectedness. Additionally, the study tracked workplace absence due to illness.

**Results**

The findings indicated that the two interventions had different levels of effectiveness:

*   **Gratitude Intervention:** This intervention led to a statistically significant increase in participants' positive affective well-being and their self-reported feelings of gratitude. However, it did not have a significant effect on reducing negative affective well-being or on increasing feelings of social connectedness.
*   **Social Connectedness Intervention:** This exercise did not produce any significant changes in the four primary well-being outcomes measured (positive affect, negative affect, gratitude, or social connectedness).
*   **Illness-Related Absence:** Notably, both the gratitude and the social connectedness interventions were associated with a reduction in workplace absences due to illness.

**Discussion & Conclusion**

The authors conclude that simple, self-guided positive psychology interventions, particularly those focused on gratitude, show promise for enhancing employee well-being. The success of the gratitude intervention in boosting positive affect is consistent with other research in the field of positive psychology. The lack of impact from the social connectedness intervention suggests that not all such exercises are equally effective, or that this particular intervention may require a different design to yield significant results.

A key and novel finding of this study was the observed reduction in illness-related work absence for participants in both intervention groups. This suggests that even if some self-reported well-being metrics do not change, these interventions may have tangible health benefits. Given that the interventions are "short, simple, and self-guided," they represent a low-cost and practical component for workplace wellness programs. The study is noted as one of the first to examine a social connectedness intervention in a workplace setting and to demonstrate an effect on absenteeism.
