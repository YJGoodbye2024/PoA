## 1. Official Abstract
This chapter describes the naive explanation of human actions, theory of correspondent inferences, personal involvement and correspondence, and the recent research concerning phenomenal causality and the attribution of intentions. The cognitive task of establishing sufficient reason for an action involves processing the available information about, or making assumptions about, the links between stable individual dispositions and observed action. The dispositional attributes are inferred from the effects of action, but not every effect is equally salient in the inference process. The perceiver's fundamental task is to interpret the action, and this interpretation is a joint function of the effects he perceives the action to have, and his assumptions about the actor's knowledge and ability. The perceiver must have some basis for judging what effects the actor could have anticipated and been able to produce.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible. The following is a synopsis of the paper's core arguments, structured according to its original sections.

### Introduction
The paper begins by establishing its focus: the process by which an observer infers another person's stable dispositions from their actions. The authors propose a framework that moves from an observed "act" to an inferred "disposition." This process is mediated by an inference about the actor's "intention." The central argument is that an observer's cognitive task is to find a "sufficient reason" for an action, which often concludes when a plausible intention is assigned. The authors introduce a key concept, "correspondent inferences," to describe the extent to which an act and an underlying disposition are seen as directly reflecting one another. An inference is correspondent when it confidently links a specific behavior to a distinct personal trait (e.g., inferring someone is genuinely dominant from a single dominant act).

### I. A Theory of Correspondent Inferences
This section details the formal theory. The authors argue that a perceiver analyzes the unique consequences of a chosen action compared to the consequences of actions that were not chosen. The "correspondence" of an inference—the degree of confidence that a behavior reflects a true disposition—is highest when the chosen action has few "non-common effects" compared to the alternatives, and when these effects are low in "social desirability."

*   **Analysis of Non-Common Effects**: To infer an intention, a perceiver considers the effects of a chosen action. If an action leads to multiple desirable outcomes, it is difficult to know which one was intended. However, if a chosen course of action has a unique effect that other choices would not have produced, that unique (or non-common) effect is likely the intended one. The fewer the non-common effects, the clearer the actor's intention becomes.
*   **The Role of Social Desirability**: The authors argue that socially desirable behavior is not very informative about a person's disposition. If someone acts in a conventional, expected way, an observer learns little about their individual personality because they might just be conforming to social norms. Conversely, behavior that defies social norms or expectations is highly informative. For example, if a person chooses an action with negative consequences (like being ostracized) to achieve a particular effect, the observer can be much more certain that the actor is strongly motivated by a corresponding disposition.

### II. Personal Involvement and Correspondence
The theory is extended to situations where the perceiver is not a neutral observer but is personally affected by the actor's behavior. The authors distinguish between two types of personal involvement:

*   **Hedonic Relevance**: An action has hedonic relevance if it directly pleases or displeases the perceiver (i.e., promotes or obstructs their goals). The authors hypothesize that high hedonic relevance increases the likelihood that the perceiver will make a correspondent inference. In essence, when someone's actions affect us directly, we are more likely to see those actions as a direct reflection of their personality and intentions toward us.
*   **Personalism**: Personalism is the perceiver's belief that the actor's behavior was specifically directed *at them*. It is a step beyond hedonic relevance. An action might be hedonically relevant by chance, but personalism implies the actor intended to benefit or harm the perceiver personally. The authors propose that high personalism leads to an even greater tendency to make correspondent attributions. For example, if someone's action has negative consequences for us, we are more likely to attribute it to a negative disposition (like malice) if we believe the action was intentionally targeted at us.

### III. Recent Research Concerning Phenomenal Causality and the Attribution of Intentions
In the final section, the authors review empirical studies that support their theoretical framework. They discuss experiments showing how observers make attributions under different conditions. One study highlighted is Jones, Davis, & Gergen (1961), which found that observers were more confident in their dispositional inferences when an actor behaved in a way that was "out-of-role" (e.g., acting introverted during an interview for an outgoing astronaut position) compared to when they behaved "in-role." This supports the core idea that behavior departing from expectations is more informative. The paper concludes by reiterating that the inference process is a sequence: the observer starts with an action, infers an intention by analyzing the action's effects, and from that intention, attributes a more stable, underlying disposition to the actor.
