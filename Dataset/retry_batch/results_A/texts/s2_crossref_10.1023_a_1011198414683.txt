## 1. Official Abstract
> Subadditive time discounting means that discounting over a delay is greater when the delay is divided into subintervals than when it is left undivided. This may produce the most important result usually attributed to hyperbolic discounting: declining impatience, or the inverse relationship between the discount rate and the magnitude of the delay. Three choice experiments were conducted to test for subadditive discounting, and to determine whether it is sufficient to explain declining impatience. All three experiments showed strong evidence of subadditive discounting, but there was no evidence of declining impatience. I conclude by questioning whether hyperbolic discounting is a plausible account of time preference.

### Detailed Interpretation

Daniel Read's 2001 paper, "Is Time-Discounting Hyperbolic or Subadditive?", challenges the prevailing hyperbolic discounting model as the primary explanation for time-inconsistent preferences. The central argument is that a phenomenon called "subadditive discounting" can account for behaviors typically attributed to hyperbolic discounting, specifically the tendency for people to be less patient over shorter time intervals.

**The Concept of Subadditive Discounting**

Subadditivity, in this context, means that the total discount applied to a period of time is greater if that period is broken into smaller segments. For example, the perceived cost of waiting a year is less than the sum of the perceived costs of waiting twelve individual months. This happens because people are less patient (i.e., they apply a higher per-time-unit discount rate) over shorter intervals, regardless of whether the interval is in the near or distant future. This contrasts with hyperbolic discounting, which posits that impatience specifically declines as the entire delay period moves further into the future.

**Experimental Findings**

The paper details three choice-based experiments designed to isolate and test for subadditive discounting and its ability to explain "declining impatience"—the key evidence often cited for hyperbolic models. The results across all three experiments provided strong and consistent evidence for subadditive discounting. However, the experiments found no evidence of declining impatience once subadditivity was accounted for. In other words, when comparing intervals of the same length, it didn't matter whether they occurred sooner or later; what mattered was how they were divided.

**Conclusion and Impact**

Read concludes by questioning the plausibility and necessity of the hyperbolic discounting model. The paper suggests that the observations of changing patience that gave rise to the hyperbolic model may be better explained by the simpler, more fundamental principle of subadditivity. This work proposes that subadditive discounting could be an alternative, or at least a complementary, explanation for many observed intertemporal choice anomalies. Subsequent research by Read and others has continued to build on this idea, arguing that subadditivity may be sufficient to account for experimental results without needing to invoke hyperbolic functions.
