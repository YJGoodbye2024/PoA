### Detailed Interpretation

**Theoretical Foundation: The Strength Model of Self-Control**
The paper's central argument is built upon the "strength model" of self-control, which posits that self-regulation is a limited, general-purpose resource. Much like a muscle, this resource can be fatigued or depleted through exertion. The authors apply this model to consumer behavior, proposing that acts of self-control in any domain (e.g., regulating attention, controlling emotions) draw from this common resource pool. When this resource is depleted—a state often called "ego depletion"—an individual's ability to resist subsequent temptations, such as the urge to make an impulse purchase, is significantly weakened. The research, therefore, frames impulse buying not just as a personality trait but as a temporary state of vulnerability caused by the prior expenditure of self-regulatory resources.

**Experimental Investigations and Key Findings**
The authors conducted three experiments to test their hypothesis:

*   **Experiment 1:** The first study aimed to establish a direct link between resource depletion and spending desire. Participants had their self-regulatory resources depleted through an attention-control task. Afterward, this group demonstrated a greater willingness to pay for various products compared to a control group whose resources were not depleted.

*   **Experiment 2:** This experiment sought to replicate and extend the initial findings. It used a different depletion task (requiring mental control) and integrated the concept of trait impulsivity. The results showed that resource depletion again led to increased impulsive spending. Crucially, it also revealed an interaction: individuals with a pre-existing disposition for impulse buying were especially vulnerable to the effects of resource depletion, suggesting that a temporary lack of self-control can dramatically increase the likelihood of unplanned spending for those already prone to it.

*   **Experiment 3:** The final investigation moved from hypothetical willingness to spend to actual behavior. The abstract indicates that participants with depleted resources "actually did spend more money in unanticipated buying situations." This confirmed that the effect of ego depletion translates into real-world purchasing decisions.

A notable and somewhat unexpected finding across the studies was how resource depletion affected decision-making processes. Participants with depleted resources reported being equally influenced by both affective (emotional) and cognitive (rational) considerations. This suggests that when self-control is low, the typical conflict between desire and reason may be bypassed, leaving the individual susceptible to a wider range of purchasing triggers.

**Conclusions and Implications**
The core conclusion of the paper is that self-regulatory resource availability is a strong predictor of one's ability to resist impulse buying temptations. When self-control resources are spent, consumers experience stronger urges to buy and are willing to spend more money. This research was significant in shifting the understanding of impulse buying from a purely dispositional trait to a behavior heavily influenced by a temporary psychological state. It demonstrates that everyday activities requiring self-control can leave consumers more susceptible to unplanned purchases later on.
