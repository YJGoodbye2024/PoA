## 1. Official Abstract
A laboratory experiment was conducted to examine the effects of a favor and of liking on compliance with a request for assistance from a confederate. Liking for the confederate was manipulated, and male subjects then received a soft drink from the confederate, from the experimenter, or received no favor. Compliance with the confederate's request to purchase some raffle tickets was measured, as was liking for the confederate. The results showed that the favor increased liking for the confederate and compliance with his request, but the effect of manipulated liking was weak.

### Introduction & Background
The study was designed to investigate the relationship between receiving a favor, liking someone, and the likelihood of complying with a subsequent request from that person. The core questions were whether a favor increases compliance, if it also increases liking, and whether the increased compliance is *because* of increased liking or due to a feeling of obligation. This builds on the "norm of reciprocity," a social rule that suggests people should repay others for acts of kindness they have received.

### Methods
The experiment was presented to participants as a study on art appreciation. Participants, who were all male, were paired with a confederate (an assistant of the experimenter, "Joe," posing as another participant). The experiment had two key manipulations:

1.  **Liking Manipulation:** In half of the cases, the confederate, Joe, behaved in a pleasant and reasonable manner. In the other half, he acted in an unpleasant and nasty way (e.g., being rude during a phone call) to decrease the participant's liking of him.
2.  **Favor Manipulation:** During a break, the experiment was divided into three conditions:
    *   **Favor Condition:** The confederate left and returned with two soft drinks, giving one to the participant as an unsolicited favor.
    *   **No Favor (Control) Condition:** The confederate left and returned empty-handed.
    *   **External Favor Condition:** The experimenter, not the confederate, gave the participant a soft drink.

Later in the experiment, the confederate asked the participant to do him a favor: to buy some raffle tickets from him. The number of raffle tickets the participant purchased served as the primary measure of compliance. Finally, a questionnaire was used to measure how much the participants liked the confederate.

### Results
The study's findings demonstrated the powerful effect of the reciprocity norm:

*   **Effect of Favor on Compliance:** Participants who received a soft drink from the confederate bought significantly more raffle tickets than those who received no favor. This effect was strong regardless of whether they initially liked the confederate or not. In essence, the obligation to reciprocate the favor overrode personal feelings of dislike.
*   **Effect of Favor on Liking:** Receiving a favor did increase the participants' reported liking for the confederate.
*   **Effect of Liking on Compliance:** The initial manipulation of liking (pleasant vs. unpleasant behavior) had a weak and not statistically reliable effect on compliance. While participants in the "no favor" condition bought more tickets if they liked Joe, when a favor was introduced, even those who disliked him bought twice as many tickets.

### Discussion/Conclusion
The simplest interpretation of the results is that receiving a favor creates a powerful feeling of obligation to reciprocate, which is the primary driver of compliance in this context. While doing a favor can also make the favor-doer better liked, this increase in liking is not the main reason for the subsequent compliance. The study powerfully demonstrates that the social rule of reciprocity can be so influential that it compels people to comply with requests from individuals they do not even like.
