## 1. Official Abstract
"Associations between personality and two aspects of meaning in life are investigated: meaningfulness and sources of meaning. Three hypotheses are formulated: The first hypothesis (A) states a moderate predictability of meaningfulness by personality factors; the second (B) suggests that personality factors predict meaningfulness via sources of meaning. The third hypothesis (C) assumes that it should be possible to predict specific sources of meaning by personality factors. A sample of 354 adults completed the NEO-FFI and the Sources of Meaning and Meaningfulness Questionnaire (SoMe). Results confirm all three hypotheses. Forty-two percent of the variance in meaningfulness was predicted by personality factors and sources of meaning. Self-transcendence (a source of meaning) proved to be the strongest predictor. The relationship between personality and meaningfulness was partly mediated by sources of meaning. Specific sources of meaning were well predicted by personality factors. It is concluded that personality is a powerful predictor of meaning in life."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### Introduction

The authors begin by noting that while meaning in life is a central concept in existential and humanistic psychology, its relationship with personality traits has been underexplored. They distinguish between two key facets of meaning: "meaningfulness," which is the subjective experience of life as coherent, significant, and purposeful, and "sources of meaning," which are the specific life domains or values (e.g., relationships, work, spirituality) that provide this sense of meaning. The study was designed to investigate the links between the Big Five personality traits (Neuroticism, Extraversion, Openness to Experience, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness) and these two aspects of meaning.

Three main hypotheses were proposed:
*   **(A)** Personality factors can moderately predict an individual's level of meaningfulness.
*   **(B)** The relationship between personality and meaningfulness is mediated by the sources of meaning an individual pursues. In other words, personality influences which sources of meaning a person values, and these sources in turn determine their overall sense of meaningfulness.
*   **(C)** Specific personality factors can predict specific sources of meaning.

### Methods

*   **Participants:** The study involved a sample of 354 German-speaking adults (210 women, 144 men) with an average age of 40.6 years.
*   **Measures:**
    *   **Personality:** The German version of the NEO-Five-Factor Inventory (NEO-FFI) was used to assess the Big Five personality traits.
    *   **Meaning in Life:** The "Sources of Meaning and Meaningfulness Questionnaire" (SoMe) was used. This instrument, developed by the lead author, is unique in that it assesses both the subjective feeling of "meaningfulness" and commitment to 26 distinct "sources of meaning." These 26 sources are grouped into four broader dimensions: Self-Transcendence, Self-Actualization, Order, and Well-being/Communality.

### Results

The study's results confirmed all three hypotheses:

*   **Hypothesis A (Personality predicts Meaningfulness):** The Big Five personality traits accounted for a significant portion of the variance in meaningfulness. Specifically, Neuroticism had a strong negative correlation with meaningfulness, while Extraversion, Openness, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness all had positive correlations. People who are more emotionally stable, outgoing, open to new things, agreeable, and diligent tend to experience their lives as more meaningful.
*   **Hypothesis B (Mediation by Sources of Meaning):** The analysis showed that the influence of personality on meaningfulness was partly mediated by sources of meaning. Self-transcendence (valuing things beyond oneself, like spirituality, social commitment, or nature) emerged as the most powerful predictor of meaningfulness, stronger than any single personality trait. The results indicated two paths to a meaningful life: one direct path from personality (especially Extraversion and Openness) to meaningfulness, and another, indirect path where personality traits guide the adoption of specific sources of meaning (like self-transcendence), which then foster meaningfulness.
*   **Hypothesis C (Personality predicts Sources of Meaning):** The study found strong, specific links between personality traits and the sources of meaning people value. For example:
    *   **Openness to Experience** was a strong predictor of valuing self-transcendence and self-actualization (e.g., creativity, development, knowledge).
    *   **Extraversion** was linked to valuing well-being and communality (e.g., fun, community, love).
    *   **Conscientiousness** was a strong predictor of valuing order (e.g., tradition, practicality, morality).
    *   **Neuroticism** was negatively associated with most sources of meaning but showed a positive correlation with spirituality, which the authors suggest might function as a coping mechanism for those high in neuroticism.

Overall, the combined model of personality traits and sources of meaning was able to predict 42% of the variance in experienced meaningfulness.

### Discussion/Conclusion

The authors conclude that personality is a "powerful predictor of meaning in life." The findings demonstrate that who a person is (their personality) fundamentally shapes what they find meaningful (sources of meaning) and how meaningful their life feels overall (meaningfulness). The study highlights that while personality has a direct impact on meaningfulness, its indirect effect through guiding life goals and values is also crucial. Self-transcendence was identified as a particularly vital source of meaning. The authors suggest that a meaningful life is not just a matter of chance but is deeply intertwined with stable personality dispositions that orient individuals toward certain values and experiences.
