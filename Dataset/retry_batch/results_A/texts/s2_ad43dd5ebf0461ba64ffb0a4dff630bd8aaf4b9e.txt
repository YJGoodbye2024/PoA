## 1. Official Abstract
"The ability to resolve conflict is crucial for maintaining healthy relationships and psychological well-being. However, prior research has often overlooked the potential benefits of neuroticism for conflict resolution styles, due to a reliance on self-report measures and the lack of consideration of healthy neuroticism. This study examined the relationship between neuroticism and conflict resolution styles and highlights the pivotal role of conscientiousness and measurement modalities. Employing both self-reports and observer ratings of the Conflict Resolution Styles Inventory (CRSI), we analyzed data from young adults (N = 259) using structural equation modeling analysis. Self-reports indicated that neuroticism was associated with unconstructive conflict resolution strategies, whereas observer ratings revealed a positive association with constructive strategies. Conscientiousness moderated these relationships differently across measurement modalities, by buffering the negative aspects of neuroticism in self-reports and enhancing its positive aspects in observer ratings. These findings underscore the importance of measurement modalities and conscientiousness in understanding the benefits of neuroticism for conflict resolution."

### **Introduction and Objectives**
The study addresses a gap in personality research, which has historically focused on the negative aspects of neuroticism. The authors argue that potential benefits of neuroticism, particularly in conflict resolution, have been overlooked due to an over-reliance on self-report measures and a failure to consider the concept of "healthy neuroticism". The research had two primary goals: first, to investigate the moderating role of conscientiousness on the relationship between neuroticism and conflict resolution style, and second, to examine how different measurement methods (self-reports vs. observer-reports) affect this relationship. The authors theorize that high conscientiousness can help neurotic individuals manage their conflict-related stress and anxiety more effectively, leading to more positive outcomes.

### **Methodology**
The study involved a sample of 259 young adults from a university, as part of a larger daily diary study. To measure conflict resolution styles, the researchers employed the Conflict Resolution Styles Inventory (CRSI), collecting data through two different methods: self-reports from the participants and ratings from external observers. The data was then analyzed using structural equation modeling. The study's design is framed by the Self-Others Knowledge Asymmetry (SOKA) model, which posits that individuals are better at reporting their internal states (like anxiety), while others are more accurate in assessing observable behaviors. This framework helps explain potential discrepancies between self-perceptions and how one's actions are viewed by others.

### **Key Findings**
The study produced a striking and nuanced set of findings that depended entirely on the measurement modality:
*   **Self-Reports:** When participants rated themselves, higher neuroticism was associated with *unconstructive* conflict resolution strategies.
*   **Observer Ratings:** In contrast, when observers rated the participants, higher neuroticism was linked to *constructive* conflict resolution strategies.

Furthermore, the personality trait of conscientiousness was found to be a crucial moderator. For highly conscientious individuals, the trait acted as a buffer against the negative effects of neuroticism in self-reports. In observer ratings, conscientiousness appeared to enhance the positive aspects of neuroticism, suggesting that conscientious but neurotic individuals behave in ways that others perceive as constructive when resolving conflicts.

### **Conclusion and Implications**
The core conclusion of the paper is that the link between neuroticism and conflict resolution is not straightforward. The findings demonstrate that neuroticism can have beneficial outcomes, but these benefits may be more visible to others than to the individuals themselves, who may remain focused on their internal negative feelings. The study highlights that conscientiousness can channel the anxieties associated with neuroticism into more productive and structured approaches to conflict. Ultimately, the research makes a strong case for the importance of using multiple measurement modalities (self-report and observer-report) to gain a more complete understanding of how personality traits function in social contexts.
