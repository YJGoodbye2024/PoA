## 1. Official Abstract
"Challening the prevailing view that party identification is a 'running tally' of retrospective assessments of the parties, this book argues that partisanship is a social identity. Drawing on insights from social psychology, the authors contend that for most citizens, party identification is a stable, psychologically engaging attachment to a political group. Using a variety of statistical methods and a wealth of survey data, the book demonstrates that the conventional 'running tally' model of partisanship is not supported by the evidence. The book shows that partisan loyalties are remarkably stable, even in the face of dramatic political and economic events. The authors also show that partisanship has a powerful influence on political attitudes and behavior, shaping how citizens view the political world and how they vote. The book concludes by discussing the implications of its findings for the study of American politics."

*(Source: This abstract is a composite summary of the book's description provided by Yale University Press and distributed across various platforms, including Google Books and Apple Books.)*

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. A detailed interpretation based on the book's preface, introduction, and critical summaries follows.

The central thesis of *Partisan Hearts and Minds* is that partisanship should be understood not as a rational, running tally of policy evaluations, but as a deep-seated social identity. Authors Green, Palmquist, and Schickler argue that a voter's affiliation with a political party is akin to a religious identity or allegiance to a sports team—a stable, enduring feature of their self-conception that shapes their perception of politics, rather than being continuously updated by it. This perspective directly challenges the then-dominant "running tally" model, which posited that voters constantly adjust their party affiliation based on party performance and policy offerings.

The book's analysis is structured to build this argument systematically:

*   **Introduction:** The authors introduce their core argument by contrasting two views of partisanship. The first is the rationalist view where voters gravitate toward parties based on policy agreement or assessments of incumbent performance. The second, which they champion, is the view of partisanship as a social identity. They argue that once these identities form, typically in early adulthood, they are highly persistent and structure how individuals engage with politics. They contend that while party organizations may have weakened, the social identities of "Democrat" and "Republican" remain powerful.

*   **Partisan Stability (Chapters 2-5):** A significant portion of the book is dedicated to demonstrating the remarkable stability of party identification. The authors use extensive survey data from the United States and other countries to show that partisan attachments tend to persist over time. They argue that major events like scandals, recessions, or landslide elections have surprisingly little impact on these core identities. Their analysis suggests that previous research showing partisan volatility was often based on methodological misinterpretations.

*   **Mechanisms of Change (Chapters 6-7):** While arguing for stability, the authors do not claim that partisan identities never change. Instead, they propose that significant shifts in party affiliation at a mass level (realignment) are rare and occur only when the fundamental social meaning or "imagery" of a party changes. The primary example analyzed is the party realignment in the American South following the Civil Rights era, where the Democratic Party's identity was transformed by its association with African Americans after the Voting Rights Act.

*   **Consequences of Partisan Identity (Chapter 8):** The final section explores how these powerful partisan attachments structure the political landscape. The authors argue that partisanship is a primary predictor of electoral choice, often overshadowing other social identities. Partisan identity acts as a filter, influencing how voters interpret political information, evaluate candidates, and ultimately cast their votes. When individuals feel a sense of belonging to a party, they tend to adopt the doctrinal positions advocated by that group.

In essence, Green, Palmquist, and Schickler's work re-centered the study of partisanship on its social and psychological foundations, arguing that for most people, party loyalty is a matter of "who they are" rather than a product of "what they think" about specific issues.
