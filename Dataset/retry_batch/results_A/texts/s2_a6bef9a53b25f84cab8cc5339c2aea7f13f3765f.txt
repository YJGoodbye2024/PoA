## 1. Official Abstract
Critics of intelligence tests—writers such as Robert Sternberg, Howard Gardner, and Daniel Goleman—have argued in recent years that these tests neglect important qualities such as emotion, empathy, and interpersonal skills. However, such critiques imply that though intelligence tests may miss certain key noncognitive areas, they encompass most of what is important in the cognitive domain. In this book, Keith E. Stanovich challenges this widely held assumption. Stanovich shows that IQ tests (or their proxies, such as the SAT) are radically incomplete as measures of cognitive functioning. They fail to assess traits that most people associate with “good thinking,” skills such as judgment and decision making. Such cognitive skills are crucial to real-world behavior, affecting the way we plan, evaluate critical evidence, judge risks and probabilities, and make effective decisions. IQ tests fail to assess these skills of rational thought, even though they are measurable cognitive processes. Rational thought is just as important as intelligence, Stanovich argues, and it should be valued as highly as the abilities currently measured on intelligence tests.

### Detailed Interpretation

**Central Thesis: The Great Rationality Debate**

Keith Stanovich's 2009 book, *What Intelligence Tests Miss: The Psychology of Rational Thought*, presents a foundational challenge to the conventional understanding of cognitive assessment. The central argument is that intelligence tests (IQ tests) and their proxies (like the SAT) are fundamentally incomplete because they fail to measure a critical component of cognition: rationality. Stanovich argues that while critics have often pointed out that IQ tests miss non-cognitive skills like emotional intelligence, they have failed to see the large gap within the cognitive domain itself. The book posits that rationality—the ability to think and behave in a way that achieves one's goals and is consistent with available evidence—is a separate and equally important construct from intelligence.

**The Concept of "Dysrationalia"**

To frame his argument, Stanovich introduces the term "dysrationalia": the inability to think and behave rationally despite possessing adequate or even high intelligence. The book uses the surprising and sometimes "foolish" actions of intelligent people as a key piece of evidence. Stanovich contends that our shock at seeing smart people do irrational things stems from a flawed assumption that intelligence and rationality are intrinsically linked. Research, however, shows that the correlation between IQ and rational thinking skills is often low to moderate, allowing for significant discrepancies between the two.

**A Tripartite Model of the Mind**

A core element of the book's theoretical framework is its tripartite model of the mind, which deconstructs cognitive processing into three distinct parts:
1.  **The Autonomous Mind:** This corresponds to what is often called System 1 processing. It is fast, unconscious, and operates on instinct and heuristics. It's responsible for our gut reactions and intuitive responses.
2.  **The Algorithmic Mind:** This is the dimension of cognitive ability that traditional IQ tests measure. It involves the efficiency of cognitive processes, such as abstract reasoning, processing speed, and working memory capacity.
3.  **The Reflective Mind:** This level controls the initiation of higher-level cognitive functions. It determines when to override the autonomous mind's intuitive responses and engage the more effortful algorithmic mind. It involves thinking dispositions, cognitive styles, and the willingness to consider alternative perspectives and evidence.

Stanovich argues that IQ tests primarily assess the algorithmic mind but completely miss the reflective mind, which is the seat of rational thought. A person can have a powerful "engine" (high algorithmic intelligence) but fail to use it effectively because of tendencies of the reflective mind, such as being a "cognitive miser" (avoiding effortful thought), or processing information with a "myside bias" (favoring one's own prior beliefs).

**Mindware: The Software of Rational Thought**

Another critical concept is "mindware," which refers to the rules, knowledge, procedures, and strategies a person has learned that can be used to support rational thinking. Stanovich explains that rational thinking can fail due to "mindware gaps" (not having the necessary knowledge, such as understanding probabilities or logic) or "contaminated mindware" (holding beliefs that are actively irrational, like superstitions or conspiracy theories). Intelligence tests do not assess the quality or presence of this crucial cognitive software.

In conclusion, Stanovich's book is a call to action for both psychology and society to recognize the limits of IQ and to develop systematic ways of assessing and cultivating rationality, which he terms the "RQ" (Rationality Quotient). He argues that the skills of judgment and decision-making, which were recognized with a Nobel Prize for researchers like Daniel Kahneman, are too important to be left out of our primary measures of "good thinking."
