## 1. Official Abstract
The base-rate fallacy is people's tendency to ignore base rates in favor of, e.g., individuating information (when such is available), rather than integrate the two. This tendency has important implications for understanding judgment phenomena in many clinical, legal, and social-psychological settings. An explanation of this phenomenon is offered, according to which people order information by its perceived degree of relevance, and let high-relevance information dominate low-relevance information. Information is deemed more relevant when it relates more specifically to a judged target case. Specificity is achieved either by providing information on a smaller set than the overall population, of which the target case is a member, or when information can be coded, via causality, as information about the specific members of a given population. The base-rate fallacy is thus the result of pitting what seem to be merely coincidental, therefore low-relevance, base rates against more specific, or causal, information. A series of probabilistic inference problems is presented in which relevance was manipulated with the means described above, and the empirical results confirm the above account. In particular, base rates will be combined with other information when the two kinds of information are perceived as being equally relevant to the judged case.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on reliable third-party analyses of the paper.

Maya Bar-Hillel's 1980 paper, "The base-rate fallacy in probability judgments," provides a foundational critique and alternative explanation for why people neglect base-rate information when making probability judgments. At the time, the prevailing explanation, offered by Kahneman and Tversky, was that the "representativeness heuristic"—our tendency to judge probability based on how much a specific case resembles a stereotype—caused the fallacy. Bar-Hillel argued that representativeness alone was insufficient to explain all instances of base-rate neglect.

The core of Bar-Hillel's thesis is the concept of **perceived relevance**. She proposed that before making a judgment, people intuitively categorize the information they receive by its relevance. Information deemed irrelevant is discarded and not factored into the final decision. According to this view, the base-rate fallacy occurs when general, statistical base rates are perceived as less relevant than specific, individuating information.

Bar-Hillel identified two key factors that influence this perception of relevance:

1.  **Specificity**: Information that is more specific to the target case is seen as more relevant. Individuating information, such as a personality sketch, is by its nature highly specific. In contrast, base-rate information, which describes a general population, seems incidental and less pertinent to the unique case being judged. For example, in the classic "Cab Problem," subjects are given the base rate of cab colors in a city (e.g., 85% Green, 15% Blue) and an eyewitness testimony about a specific accident. Many people ignore the base rate and overweight the less reliable eyewitness account because it is specific to the event in question.

2.  **Causality**: Base rates are more likely to be used when they can be causally linked to the judgment task. If a base rate is presented as a mere statistical fact about a population (e.g., the percentage of green cabs), it is often ignored. However, if the base rate is framed in a way that implies a causal relationship (e.g., the Green cab company has more accidents because it has more cabs), it is perceived as more relevant and is more likely to be integrated into the judgment.

In essence, Bar-Hillel's work shifted the focus from a purely cognitive shortcut (representativeness) to a more nuanced process of information filtering based on perceived relevance. She demonstrated through a series of experiments that base rates are not always ignored. Instead, they are used when they are seen as being as relevant as other available information. This occurs when the base rate and the individuating information are equally specific or when the base rate has a clear causal connection to the problem at hand.
