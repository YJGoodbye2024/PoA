## 1. Official Abstract
A growing body of research derived from terror management theory [e.g., Solomon, S., Greenberg, J., & Pyszczynski, T. (1991). A terror management theory of social behavior: The psychological functions of self-esteem and cultural worldviews. In M. P. Zanna (Ed.), Advances in experimental social psychology: vol. 24 (pp. 93–159). New York: Academic Press] suggests that the human struggle with the awareness of inevitable death can greatly impact people's physical and psychological well-being. The current article reviews converging lines of research that investigate the role of terror management processes in physical and mental health. Specifically, we present research that elucidates the role of death concerns in (1) conscious threat-focused defenses, (2) self-esteem striving, (3) depression, (4) anxiety disorders, (5) discomfort with the physicality of the body and (6) neuroticism.

The paper, "The Worm at the Core: A Terror Management Perspective on the Roots of Psychological Dysfunction," published in *Applied and Preventive Psychology*, puts forth the argument that many psychological dysfunctions are rooted in the human struggle to manage the terror of inevitable death. Drawing from Terror Management Theory (TMT), the authors posit that psychological health is largely dependent on an individual's ability to maintain a sense of self-esteem and faith in a cultural worldview, which together serve as a buffer against death-related anxiety.

A critical analysis from a related study highlights the core thesis: when individuals are unable to use self-esteem to protect against the anxiety that stems from an awareness of mortality, they can develop chronic fears and mental illnesses. The paper by Arndt and his colleagues reviews multiple lines of research to connect terror management processes to specific psychological conditions. These include:
*   **Anxiety and Phobias:** The paper suggests that when the symbolic defenses against death anxiety fail, it can lead to the development of anxiety disorders and specific phobias.
*   **Depression and Self-Esteem:** The inability to maintain self-esteem, which TMT identifies as a key measure of one's value within a meaning-providing culture, is linked to depressive symptoms.
*   **Body-Related Concerns:** The authors also explore how discomfort with the physical nature of the human body can be a manifestation of death anxiety, as the body's creatureliness is a stark reminder of its mortal vulnerability.
*   **Neuroticism:** The paper connects the broader trait of neuroticism to the underlying, and often unconscious, efforts to manage the terror of death.

In essence, Arndt et al. propose that a wide range of psychological problems emerge when the standard terror management defenses—cultural worldview and self-esteem—are ineffective, leading to a direct and debilitating confrontation with the existential fear of death.
