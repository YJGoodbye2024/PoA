## 1. Official Abstract
>Emotional-suppression has been associated with generally negative social consequences (Butler et al., 2003; Gross & John, 2003). A cultural perspective suggests, however, that these consequences may be moderated by cultural values. We tested this hypothesis in a two-part study, and found that, for Americans holding Western-European values, habitual suppression was associated with self-protective goals and negative emotion. In addition, experimentally elicited suppression resulted in reduced interpersonal responsiveness during face-to-face interaction, along with negative partner-perceptions and hostile behavior. These deleterious effects were reduced when individuals with more Asian values suppressed, and these reductions were mediated by cultural differences in the responsiveness of the suppressors. These findings suggest that many of suppression's negative social impacts may be moderated by cultural values.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was not directly accessible for a complete section-by-section synopsis. However, based on detailed analyses from academic and secondary sources, the core insights and structure of the paper can be reconstructed as follows.

The study was designed to test the hypothesis that the negative social consequences of emotion suppression, commonly observed in Western contexts, are moderated by cultural values. It was conducted in two parts.

### **Part 1: Habitual Suppression and Cultural Values**

This part of the study examined the relationship between an individual's habitual tendency to suppress emotions, their cultural values, and their social goals.

*   **Methods:** The researchers had female participants self-report their cultural values on a scale ranging from exclusively European-American values to a combination of European-American and Asian values. Participants also reported on their typical use of emotion suppression.
*   **Findings:** A significant correlation was found between cultural values and suppression. Individuals who held both Asian and American values reported a greater tendency to engage in emotion suppression. For participants who primarily held Western-European values, habitual suppression was linked to self-protective goals and experiencing more negative emotions. This suggests that for this group, suppression is associated with a defensive and negative affective style.

### **Part 2: Experimental Suppression and Social Interaction**

This part of the study experimentally induced emotion suppression to observe its direct social consequences during a face-to-face interaction.

*   **Methods:** Female participants were paired up into dyads. They were shown an upsetting film clip to elicit a negative emotional response. One group was explicitly instructed to suppress any emotional expression during the subsequent interaction (the "suppressors"), while the control group received no such instructions. The researchers then observed the interaction between the suppressor and her partner.
*   **Findings:** The results demonstrated a clear cultural moderation effect.
    *   **For suppressors with Western-European values,** the act of suppressing emotions led to negative social outcomes. They showed reduced interpersonal responsiveness, and their partners, in turn, perceived them as more hostile and withdrawn. This created a negative feedback loop, impairing the social connection.
    *   **For suppressors who held more Asian values,** these negative effects were significantly diminished. The study found that the key difference was the "responsiveness of the suppressors." Individuals from cultural backgrounds where suppression is more normative may be more skilled at suppressing their emotions without withdrawing from the social interaction, thus mitigating the negative consequences for their partners.

### **Conclusion**

The authors conclude that the social costs of emotion suppression are not universal but are instead culture-specific. While suppression is associated with adverse social outcomes in individuals who hold Western-European values, these negative effects are less pronounced for individuals with more Asian-oriented values. The findings suggest that cultural norms shape not only the tendency to use certain emotion regulation strategies but also the social consequences of using them.
