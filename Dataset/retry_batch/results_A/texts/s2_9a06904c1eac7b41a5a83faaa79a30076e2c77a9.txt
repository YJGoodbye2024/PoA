## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible and analyzed. The study, which is recognized as a foundational paper on the "illusory truth effect," investigates how repeating a plausible statement affects a person's belief in its truthfulness.

### **Introduction**
The authors begin by distinguishing between "episodic validity" and "referential validity." Information in a memory experiment has episodic validity—it is valid within the context of the experiment, but subjects are not expected to believe it refers to a real-world event. In contrast, referential validity pertains to the truthfulness of a statement in the real world. The experiment was designed to explore how the frequency of hearing a statement might influence its perceived referential validity. The central hypothesis was that repeating a plausible statement, even in an ambiguous context, would increase a person's certainty of its truth.

### **Method**
The experiment involved 40 college students who participated in three sessions, each separated by a two-week interval. In each session, subjects listened to a list of 60 plausible statements drawn from various topics like sports and politics. These statements were designed to be things most students would not specifically know.

For each statement, subjects had to rate their certainty of its truth or falsity on a 7-point scale, where 1 meant "certainly true" and 7 meant "certainly false."

A key manipulation was the repetition of certain statements. Twenty of the initial 60 statements were designated as "critical items" and were repeated in all three sessions. The remaining 40 statements in the second and third sessions were entirely new. This created a comparison between "repeated" and "nonrepeated" items. The actual truth or falsity of the statements was also a variable in the design.

### **Results**
The study's main finding was a significant change in the validity ratings for the repeated statements. For both true and false statements that were repeated across the three sessions, subjects rated them as progressively more true. Specifically, the average rating for repeated items shifted significantly towards "true," while the ratings for the nonrepeated items showed no change. This demonstrates that "repeated statements are more likely to be judged as 'true' than are similar, non-repeated statements."

### **Discussion**
The authors conclude that the frequency of exposure is a powerful mechanism that people use to judge the truth of plausible statements. This phenomenon occurs even when the statements are, in fact, false. The repetition of a statement appears to increase its believability, effectively serving as a "criterion used to establish the referential validity of plausible statements." The paper suggests that this finding aligns with other research showing the human information processing system's high sensitivity to frequency. The authors propose that frequency might be a primary way that plausible information enters our general knowledge base.
