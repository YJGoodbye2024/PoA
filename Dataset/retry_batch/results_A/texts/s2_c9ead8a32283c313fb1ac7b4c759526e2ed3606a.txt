## 1. Official Abstract
> Previous work on the endowment effect has demonstrated that current ownership status affects object valuation and that this effect occurs instantaneously on possession of an object. The current work presents findings from four studies which indicate that the history of past ownership can also affect object valuation. For objects currently in one's possession, we find that valuation increases with duration of ownership. For objects not in one's possession, previous ownership experience increases valuation, and the increase appears to be related to the duration of ownership before loss. In addition, the perceived attractiveness of objects, although not instantly affected by endowment, is found to increase with duration of ownership.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. A detailed interpretation has been compiled from third-party and abstract-level sources.

The paper "The Effect of Ownership History on the Valuation of Objects" investigates how the history of owning an object, not just the current state of ownership, influences how much a person values it. The research extends the well-documented "endowment effect," which shows that people value things more simply because they own them. The authors, Strahilevitz and Loewenstein, propose and test the idea that the *duration* of ownership and even *past* ownership play a significant role in an object's valuation.

The core findings are derived from four distinct studies:
*   **For currently owned objects:** The research demonstrates that the value a person ascribes to an object increases the longer they have owned it. This suggests that the endowment effect is not static but strengthens over time.
*   **For previously owned objects:** The studies also found that having owned an object in the past increases its valuation, even if the person no longer possesses it. The value increase is linked to how long the object was owned before it was lost.
*   **Ownership and Attractiveness:** An additional finding is that while simply being endowed with an object does not make it seem more attractive, the perceived attractiveness does grow as the duration of ownership increases.

In essence, the paper argues that an object's history is a crucial component of its value. This dynamic perspective contrasts with prior research that treated the endowment effect as an instantaneous and binary phenomenon (owning vs. not owning). The authors' work indicates that valuation is influenced by a temporal relationship with the object, adding layers of duration and past experience to the understanding of consumer behavior and decision-making.
