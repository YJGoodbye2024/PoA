## 1. Official Abstract
Based on evolutionary logic, Henrich and Gil-White [Evolution and Human Behavior, 22(3), 165–196] distinguished between two routes to attaining social status in human societies: dominance, based on intimidation, and prestige, based on the possession of skills or expertise. Independently, emotion researchers Tracy and Robins [Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 92(3), 506–525] demonstrated two distinct forms of pride: hubristic and authentic. The present research tested the hypothesis that these two forms of pride are part of the affective-motivational suites of psychological adaptations that evolved to manage the two status-attainment strategies. In two studies, we found that authentic pride is associated with prestige, and hubristic pride is associated with dominance. Moreover, the two facets of pride are part of a larger suite of distinctive psychological traits uniquely associated with dominance or prestige. Specifically, dominance is positively associated with traits such as narcissism, aggression, and disagreeableness, whereas prestige is positively associated with traits such as genuine self-esteem, agreeableness, conscientiousness, achievement, advice-giving, and prosociality. Discussion focuses on the implications of these findings for our understanding of the evolutionary origins of pride and social status, and the interrelations among emotion, personality, and status attainment.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible, allowing for a comprehensive synopsis organized by its original sections.

### **Introduction**
The introduction outlines two distinct paths to achieving social status in human societies, as theorized by Henrich and Gil-White: "dominance," which is achieved through fear and intimidation, and "prestige," which is earned through respect for one's skills and knowledge. Separately, emotion research has identified two facets of the emotion pride: "authentic pride," related to feelings of accomplishment and confidence, and "hubristic pride," characterized by arrogance and conceit. The authors propose an evolutionary hypothesis connecting these two concepts: the two forms of pride may be the psychological mechanisms that evolved to guide behaviors aimed at either dominance or prestige. Specifically, they hypothesize that "hubristic pride may have evolved to motivate behaviors, thoughts, and feelings oriented toward attaining dominance, whereas authentic pride may have evolved to motivate behaviors, thoughts, and feelings oriented toward attaining prestige."

### **Study 1: Pride and Personality Correlates of Dominance and Prestige**
The first study aimed to establish the predicted relationships between the two facets of pride, personality traits, and the two forms of social status.

*   **Methods:** This study involved 104 undergraduate participants who completed a battery of self-report questionnaires. These included scales to measure their tendencies toward dominance and prestige, their experiences of authentic and hubristic pride, and a range of personality traits such as the Big Five (extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism, openness), narcissism, and self-esteem.

*   **Results:** The findings supported the main hypothesis. Hubristic pride was strongly and uniquely associated with dominance. Authentic pride, conversely, was strongly associated with prestige. Furthermore, dominance and prestige showed divergent personality profiles. Dominance was linked to aggression, narcissism, and disagreeableness. Prestige was positively correlated with genuine self-esteem, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and a self-reported tendency to offer advice.

### **Study 2: Experimental Manipulation of Pride**
This study was designed to provide causal evidence for the link between the two facets of pride and the cognitive and behavioral strategies associated with dominance and prestige.

*   **Methods:** Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions where they were asked to recall and write about a time they felt either authentic pride, hubristic pride, or a neutral control event. Following this emotional induction, they completed tasks measuring their desire for dominance versus prestige and their self-perceived status.

*   **Results:** The results from Study 2 further solidified the findings of Study 1. Participants induced to feel hubristic pride reported a greater desire for dominance. In contrast, those induced to feel authentic pride showed a greater preference for prestige-based status. This experimental evidence suggests that the experience of each pride facet can temporarily shift an individual's motivation toward a corresponding status-seeking strategy.

### **General Discussion**
The discussion section synthesizes the findings from both studies, arguing that they provide strong support for the paper's central thesis. The authors conclude that authentic and hubristic pride are distinct affective mechanisms that likely co-evolved with the strategies of prestige and dominance, respectively.

The paper posits that these two routes to status are "part of a larger suite of distinctive psychological traits uniquely associated with dominance or prestige." For instance, prestige is linked with prosocial and achievement-oriented traits like agreeableness, conscientiousness, and genuine self-esteem, which are all promoted by authentic pride. In contrast, dominance is associated with antisocial and aggressive traits like narcissism and disagreeableness, which are fueled by hubristic pride. The authors suggest that this integrated system of emotions and personality traits serves to guide individuals along one of two effective, albeit very different, paths to elevating their social rank. The paper concludes by discussing the broader implications for understanding the evolution of human social hierarchies, emotion, and personality.
