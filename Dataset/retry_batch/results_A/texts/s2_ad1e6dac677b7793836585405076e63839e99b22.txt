## 1. Official Abstract
Male passersby were contacted either on a fear-arousing suspension bridge or a non-fear-arousing bridge by an attractive female interviewer who asked them to fill out questionnaires containing Thematic Apperception Test pictures. Sexual content of stories written by subjects on the fear-arousing bridge and tendency of these subjects to attempt postexperimental contact with the interviewer were both significantly greater. No significant differences between bridges were obtained on either measure for subjects contacted by a male interviewer. A third study manipulated anticipated shock to male subjects and an attractive female confederate independently. Anticipation of own shock but not anticipation of shock to confederate increased sexual imagery scores on the Thematic Apperception Test and attraction to the confederate. Some theoretical implications of these findings are discussed.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. The core of the paper is structured around three distinct experiments designed to test the hypothesis that strong emotion, specifically fear-induced arousal, can be misattributed as sexual attraction.

### Introduction
The authors begin by noting a body of indirect evidence suggesting a link between strong emotions and sexual attraction, citing observations of mixed courting and aggression behaviors in animals and humans. They propose that the theories of Schachter (1964) on emotion could provide an explanation. Schachter's theory posits that when individuals feel a state of physiological arousal for which they have no clear explanation, they will label this arousal based on the cognitive cues available in their environment.

Dutton and Aron extend this idea, hypothesizing that "strong emotions are relabeled as sexual attraction whenever an acceptable object is present, and emotion-producing circumstances do not require the full attention of the individual." The paper outlines three experiments to test the idea that an attractive female will be perceived as more attractive by males who encounter her while they are experiencing a strong emotion (fear) compared to those who are not.

### Experiment 1: Capilano Suspension Bridge
The first and most famous experiment was conducted in a natural setting to test the emotion-sexual attraction link.

*   **Method:** The experiment took place on two bridges over the Capilano River in North Vancouver. The "fear-arousing bridge" was the Capilano Suspension Bridge, a 450-foot long, 5-foot wide structure that sways 230 feet above a rocky canyon. The "control bridge" was a solid, wider bridge further upriver, only 10 feet above a small stream. An attractive female interviewer approached unaccompanied males between 18 and 35 years old on both bridges. She explained she was doing a project on the effects of scenic attractions on creativity, asked them to write a brief, dramatic story for a Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) picture, and then gave them her phone number in case they wanted to learn more about the study later. To control for the possibility that men on the experimental bridge were simply more adventurous, the procedure was repeated with a male interviewer.
*   **Results:** The findings were significant. Men who were approached by the female interviewer on the fear-arousing bridge wrote stories with significantly more sexual content than those on the control bridge. Furthermore, a much higher proportion of the men on the suspension bridge called the female interviewer afterward (9 out of 18) compared to those on the stable bridge (2 out of 16). When the interviewer was male, there were no significant differences in sexual imagery or call-backs between the two bridges. This suggests the effect was specific to the misattribution of arousal onto the attractive female, rather than a general difference in the personalities of the men on each bridge.

### Experiment 2: Follow-up to Experiment 1
This experiment was designed to address potential alternative explanations for the results of Experiment 1, such as the possibility that the female interviewer acted differently on each bridge or that there were pre-existing personality differences between the groups.

*   **Method:** The setup was similar to the first experiment, using only the fear-arousing suspension bridge and the attractive female interviewer. This time, she approached males either immediately after they crossed the bridge (when arousal would still be high) or about 10 minutes after they had crossed and had a chance to rest (when arousal would have subsided).
*   **Results:** The results mirrored those of the first experiment. The group interviewed immediately after crossing the bridge, while still aroused, had higher scores for sexual imagery in their TAT stories and were more likely to call the interviewer than the group that had rested. This provided further evidence that the arousal from the bridge was a key factor.

### Experiment 3: Controlled Laboratory Setting
The final experiment aimed to replicate the effect in a controlled laboratory environment and to distinguish between arousal from personal fear versus empathetic arousal.

*   **Method:** Male subjects were told they would be participating in a study on the effects of electric shock on learning. They were introduced to an attractive female confederate who was also supposedly a subject. In the "arousal" condition, subjects were told they would receive a strong, painful shock. In the "control" condition, they were told the shock would be a mild, tingling sensation. A third condition was added where the subject was told they would receive the mild shock, but they watched the female confederate being assigned to the strong shock condition. This was to test if simply watching someone else in an anxiety-provoking situation would produce the same effect. Afterward, subjects completed a questionnaire that included measures of their attraction to the female confederate.
*   **Results:** The results supported the misattribution hypothesis. Subjects who anticipated receiving a strong shock themselves rated the female confederate as significantly more attractive than subjects in the control group. Interestingly, subjects who merely anticipated the confederate receiving a strong shock did not show heightened attraction. The authors concluded that "a subject must be experiencing the arousal himself for the arousal/attraction effect to occur."

### General Discussion
The authors conclude that the series of experiments provides strong support for their hypothesis. The results from the field and lab settings converge to show that physiological arousal, when its source is ambiguous or can be reinterpreted, can be mislabeled as sexual attraction toward a plausible target. The bridge experiment, in particular, became a classic demonstration of the misattribution of arousal. The findings are consistent with Schachter's two-factor theory of emotion, which emphasizes the interplay of physiological arousal and cognitive labeling in emotional experience.
