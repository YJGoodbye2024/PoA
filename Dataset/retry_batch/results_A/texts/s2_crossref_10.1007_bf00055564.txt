## 1. Official Abstract
"Most real decisions, unlike those of economics texts, have a status quo alternative—that is, doing nothing or maintaining one's current or previous decision. A series of decision-making experiments shows that individuals disproportionately stick with the status quo. Data on the selections of health plans and retirement programs by faculty members reveal that the status quo bias is substantial in important real decisions. Economics, psychology, and decision theory provide possible explanations for this bias. Applications are discussed ranging from marketing techniques, to industrial organization, to the advance of science."

### Introduction to Status Quo Bias

William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser introduced the concept of "status quo bias" in their 1988 paper, defining it as a cognitive bias that reflects a preference for maintaining the current state of affairs. This bias leads individuals to resist change, often choosing to keep things as they are even when presented with potentially better alternatives. The core idea can be summarized by the saying, "If it ain't broke, don't fix it." The status quo represents the default option, and people tend to stick with it, a behavior that has implications across various fields, including economics, sociology, and politics.

### Experimental Methodology and Findings

To investigate this bias, Samuelson and Zeckhauser conducted a series of experiments using hypothetical scenarios presented through questionnaires. Participants were placed in decision-making roles relevant to individuals, managers, and government officials.

A key element of their methodology was the use of "status quo framing." In one set of experiments, a control group of participants was presented with a set of choices in a neutral manner. For the test group, one of the options was framed as the existing "status quo." The results consistently showed that the option designated as the status quo was chosen more frequently by the test group than by the neutral group. This demonstrated that the framing, rather than the inherent quality of the option, drove the preference.

The researchers observed a "numbers effect," where the relative bias for the status quo was stronger when the number of alternatives in the choice set increased. The experiments revealed a significant tendency for subjects to adhere to the status quo more often than predicted by standard rational decision-making models.

### Real-World Evidence: Health Plans and Retirement

Samuelson and Zeckhauser extended their analysis to significant real-world decisions by examining the choices of faculty members regarding health insurance and retirement plans. Their analysis of TIAA-CREF retirement plans found that a majority of participants remained in the default asset allocation they were assigned when they were younger. This led to many individuals nearing retirement holding portfolios with an inappropriately high level of risk, demonstrating a powerful real-world consequence of the status quo bias.

### Explanations for the Bias

The authors proposed three main categories of explanations for the status quo bias:

1.  **Rational Decision Making**: In some cases, sticking with the status quo can be a rational strategy. The current choice may have been optimal in the past, and the costs of re-evaluating options (transition costs or uncertainty) might outweigh the potential benefits of switching.

2.  **Cognitive Misperception**: This explanation draws from prospect theory, particularly the concept of loss aversion developed by Kahneman and Tversky. Individuals tend to weigh potential losses associated with change more heavily than potential gains. The disadvantages of leaving the status quo loom larger than the advantages of a new alternative, creating a strong preference to remain.

3.  **Psychological Commitment**: This category includes factors like sunk-cost effects, regret avoidance, and a drive for consistency. Decision-makers may feel a psychological commitment to their past choices and wish to avoid the regret that could follow a change that turns out poorly. Sticking with the status quo is often perceived as the path of least regret and requires less mental effort.

In summary, the paper was foundational in identifying and documenting the status quo bias, demonstrating its power through both controlled experiments and real-world data. It showed that this bias is a significant anomaly in the standard model of rational choice and provided a lasting framework for understanding why individuals often resist beneficial change.
