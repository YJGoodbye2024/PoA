## 1. Official Abstract
"An experiment was conducted to test the hypothesis that persons who undergo an unpleasant initiation to become members of a group increase their liking for the group; that is, they find the group more attractive than do persons who become members without going through a severe initiation."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper, "The effect of severity of initiation on liking for a group," published in *The Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* in 1959, was accessible. This analysis is structured according to the paper's original sections.

### Introduction

The study builds upon Leon Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance, which posits that individuals strive for internal consistency. Aronson and Mills hypothesized that if a person endures a difficult or embarrassing initiation to join a group, and subsequently finds the group to be dull and uninteresting, they will experience cognitive dissonance. This dissonance arises from the conflict between two cognitions: the unpleasantness of the initiation and the negative aspects of the group. To resolve this dissonance, the individual may distort their perception of the group, viewing it more favorably to justify the effort of the initiation. The experiment was designed to test the hypothesis that the severity of an initiation is positively correlated with a person's subsequent liking for the group.

### Methods

The experiment involved 63 female college students who volunteered to participate in what was described as a series of group discussions on the psychology of sex. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions:

*   **Severe Initiation:** In this condition, participants were required to read aloud a list of twelve obscene words and two vivid descriptions of sexual activity to the experimenter, under the pretense of an "embarrassment test" to ensure they could comfortably discuss the topic.
*   **Mild Initiation:** Participants in this group read aloud a list of words related to sex that were not obscene.
*   **Control Group:** This group was admitted to the group without any form of initiation.

After the initiation (or lack thereof), each participant was allowed to "listen in" on a pre-recorded discussion of the group they had just joined. The recording was intentionally designed to be as "dull and banal as possible." The discussion involved group members talking in a disjointed and uninteresting manner about the secondary sexual behavior of lower animals.

Finally, participants were asked to rate the group discussion and the group members on various scales, such as how interesting, intelligent, and likable they found them to be.

### Results

The findings supported the researchers' hypothesis. The participants in the Severe Initiation condition rated both the discussion and the group members significantly more favorably than those in the Mild Initiation and Control conditions. There was no significant difference in the ratings between the Mild and Control groups, suggesting that the mild initiation was not unpleasant enough to create a notable level of dissonance. The results indicate that undergoing a more severe initiation led to an increased liking for the group.

### Discussion

Aronson and Mills concluded that the results were consistent with the theory of cognitive dissonance. Individuals who expended significant effort or endured embarrassment to gain group membership (the Severe Initiation group) resolved the resulting dissonance by convincing themselves that the group was, in fact, interesting and worthwhile. This self-justification made their effort seem worthwhile.

The authors acknowledged that after the experiment, the participants were debriefed, and none expressed resentment about being misled. In fact, most were intrigued by the study's design. The study's clever use of deception and a standardized procedure were noted as strengths, though the artificiality of the experimental setup could be seen as a limitation. The random assignment of participants to conditions was a key strength, minimizing the influence of individual variables.
