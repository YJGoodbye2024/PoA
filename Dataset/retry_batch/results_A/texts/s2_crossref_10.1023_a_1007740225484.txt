## 1. Official Abstract
(**Requirement:** Quote the official abstract verbatim. **Requirement:** Cite the source URL.)

"It is widely believed that people are willing to expend greater resources to save the lives of identified victims than to save equal numbers of unidentified or statistical victims. There are many possible causes of this disparity which have not been enumerated previously or tested empirically. We discuss four possible causes of the “identifiable victim effect” and present the results of two studies which indicate that the most important cause of the disparity in treatment of identifiable and statistical lives is that, for identifiable victims, a high proportion of those at risk can be saved."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis

Full text was accessible. The following is a synopsis organized by the paper's original sections.

### Introduction

The paper begins by establishing the central phenomenon: people are willing to expend far greater resources to save specific, identified individuals than to save a statistically equivalent or greater number of anonymous people. The authors introduce this concept with powerful examples, including the massive public donation to rescue "Baby Jessica" McClure from a well, contrasting it with the general public's apathy towards preventative measures that could save many more lives. They cite Thomas Schelling, who first distinguished between an "individual life" and a "statistical life," and a quote attributed to Joseph Stalin: "The death of a single Russian soldier is a tragedy. A million deaths is a statistic." The authors state that their primary goal is to investigate the psychological underpinnings of this "identifiable victim effect" by empirically testing several potential causes.

### Potential Causes of the Identifiable Victim Effect

Jenni and Loewenstein identify four main factors that could explain the differential treatment between identifiable and statistical victims.

1.  **Vividness and Personal Connection**: Identifiable victims are often presented with vivid, personalizing details (a name, a face, a story), which can evoke a stronger emotional response than abstract statistics. This aligns with research showing that concrete examples have a greater impact than "pallid statistical information."
2.  **Proportion of the Reference Group**: An identifiable victim often constitutes their own reference group. Saving that one person means saving 100% of the people in that specific group. In contrast, saving even a large number of statistical victims may only represent a tiny fraction of the total population at risk. The authors note that people are generally more concerned about risks that are concentrated within a specific group.
3.  **Certainty and Risk-Seeking for Losses**: Identifiable victims are often in certain peril, whereas statistical victims face a probabilistic threat. The authors connect this to the "certainty effect," where people overweight outcomes that are certain. Furthermore, they draw on prospect theory, noting that people tend to be "risk-seeking for losses," which might lead them to prefer a risky option that could save many statistical lives over a certain option that saves fewer, even if the expected value is lower.
4.  **Ex Post vs. Ex Ante Evaluation**: Decisions to help identifiable victims are typically made *ex post* (after the person is already in danger), while decisions to help statistical victims are made *ex ante* (to prevent a future tragedy). The authors suggest that the sense of responsibility is stronger for an actual, current victim than for a potential, future one.

### Study 1: Methods and Results

The first study was designed to disentangle and test these four hypotheses. Participants were presented with scenarios involving a new type of cancer and asked to rate their support for developing a new treatment program. The scenarios were manipulated to isolate each of the four factors.

*   **Methodology**: The study used a within-subject design where participants evaluated multiple scenarios. For example, to test the "proportion" hypothesis, they compared a scenario where a treatment would save 20 out of 20 at-risk individuals (100%) with one where it would save 20 out of 1000 at-risk individuals (2%). To test for "vividness," some scenarios included a picture and name of a victim, while others did not.
*   **Results**: The study found "very strong support" for the proportion-of-the-reference-group hypothesis. Participants showed significantly more support for programs that saved a higher proportion of the at-risk group, even when the absolute number of lives saved was identical. The study found "no support for the hypothesis that the vividness with which a victim is described increases subjects' desire to save that victim." Simply knowing a person was at risk was sufficient. The certainty effect received some support, but the ex post vs. ex ante distinction did not; in fact, subjects slightly favored preventative (ex ante) actions.

### Study 2: Methods and Results

The second study aimed to replicate the findings of the first using a different context and a between-subjects design to ensure the previous results were not an artifact of the methodology.

*   **Methodology**: Participants were presented with one of two scenarios involving lead poisoning in children. In the "identifiable" case, a specific child was hospitalized, and a treatment had a 0.1% chance of saving his life. In the "statistical" case, a program to remove lead-based paint had a 0.1% chance of saving one child's life from a large pool of children at risk. Both scenarios involved the same probability of saving one life.
*   **Results**: The findings from Study 2 were consistent with Study 1. While there was a small, non-significant tendency to support the identifiable victim more, the dominant factor was again the proportion of the reference group. The authors concluded that the identifiable victim effect is largely driven by the fact that the victim represents a high proportion (often 100%) of their specific reference group.

### General Discussion

In their concluding discussion, the authors reiterate that their findings point to the "proportion of the reference group" as the most significant explanation for the identifiable victim effect. They state, "the most important cause of the disparity in treatment of identifiable and statistical lives is that, for identifiable victims, a high proportion of those at risk can be saved."

The hypotheses related to vividness and the ex post/ex ante distinction were not supported by their data. The authors suggest that the vividness of media portrayals (like that of Baby Jessica) may be a consequence of a victim's identifiability, rather than the cause of the public's concern. The core trigger is the knowledge that a specific person is in peril and can be saved. The paper concludes by discussing the policy implications, noting that this cognitive bias can lead to an inefficient allocation of societal resources, favoring dramatic rescues over more effective, large-scale preventative measures.
