Adrian Furnham's 1986 paper, "Response bias, social desirability and dissimulation," is a foundational literature review that addresses the persistent challenge of obtaining truthful answers in self-report questionnaires. The work is frequently cited for its comprehensive overview of response biases, which are systematic tendencies for individuals to provide inaccurate responses.

The core of the paper is its focus on two intertwined concepts: **social desirability** and **dissimulation**. Social desirability refers to the tendency of respondents to answer questions in a manner that will be viewed favorably by others, conforming to societal norms rather than stating their true feelings. Dissimulation, or "faking good," is the conscious act of misrepresentation to present oneself in a positive light. Furnham's review explores the extensive body of research on these topics, clarifying different terminologies and examining the methods that test designers use to either detect or mitigate these biases.

A key contribution of the paper, as highlighted by subsequent research, is its engagement with the debate over the nature of social desirability scales (often called "Lie Scales"). Furnham's work is cited in discussions on whether these scales truly detect a temporary response style (i.e., faking in a specific context) or if they measure a stable and genuine personality dimension in its own right, such as social conformity or naïveté. The paper is recognized as a significant work that helped establish response bias as a genuine and important concern for researchers, countering earlier views that it might just be "random noise" in data.

In summary, Furnham's 1986 article serves as a critical review that synthesizes the state of knowledge on response biases in personality assessment. It clarifies key concepts, discusses methodological solutions like lie scales, and contributes to the theoretical understanding of what social desirability measures truly capture. Its frequent citation in subsequent decades underscores its importance in fields that rely on self-report data, from psychology and sociology to human-computer interaction.
