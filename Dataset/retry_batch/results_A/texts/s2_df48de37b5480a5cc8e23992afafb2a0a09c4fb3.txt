## 1. Official Abstract
> The present research investigated one antecedent and various consequences of pursuing avoidance personal achievement goals over the course of a semester. The authors assessed participants' achievement-relevant goals using the newly devised Achievement Goals Questionnaire. The motive to avoid failure, assessed with self-report and projective measures, was established as an antecedent of avoidance goal pursuit. Avoidance regulation was shown to have deleterious consequences for a host of achievement-relevant and general well-being outcomes at the end of the semester, including longitudinal change in subjective well-being. Perceived competence was validated as a mediator of the direct relationships observed. The results highlight the need to attend to avoidance, as well as approach, forms of self-regulation and the need to consider both motive disposition and goal constructs in accounting for competence-relevant behavior.

### Detailed Interpretation

**1. Introduction & Theoretical Framework**
The 1997 paper by Elliot and Sheldon is a foundational text in motivation psychology that sought to integrate the classic concept of "fear of failure" with the modern achievement goal framework. At the time, achievement goal theory primarily distinguished between "mastery goals" (developing competence) and "performance goals" (demonstrating competence relative to others). Elliot and Sheldon argued for the necessity of incorporating the distinction between "approach" (striving for positive outcomes) and "avoidance" (striving to avoid negative outcomes) motivation. This paper specifically investigates the antecedents and consequences of adopting avoidance-oriented personal goals in an achievement context. The central thesis is that avoidance goals are driven by a fear of failure and lead to a range of negative psychological and performance outcomes.

**2. Methodology**
The study was conducted longitudinally over the course of a university semester. The researchers developed and utilized a new assessment tool, the Achievement Goals Questionnaire, to measure students' goals. To identify the antecedents of these goals, they assessed participants' "motive to avoid failure" using both self-report questionnaires and projective measures. Throughout and at the end of the semester, they measured various consequences, including performance, changes in subjective well-being, and physical symptoms. A key part of their analysis was testing whether "perceived competence" acted as a mediator between avoidance goals and their negative outcomes.

**3. Key Findings**
The research produced several significant findings:
*   **Antecedent of Avoidance Goals:** The study established a clear link between the motive to avoid failure and the subsequent adoption of avoidance achievement goals. This provided empirical evidence that fear of failure is a direct predictor of why individuals regulate their behavior by trying to avoid negative outcomes rather than approaching positive ones.
*   **Consequences of Avoidance Goals:** Pursuing avoidance goals was shown to have "deleterious consequences" for a wide array of outcomes. These negative effects included lower subjective well-being, reduced personal control, and a higher reporting of physical symptoms and illness. Critically, this pattern held even when controlling for neuroticism, suggesting that the effect was specific to the goal type and not just a function of a general negative disposition.
*   **Mediating Role of Perceived Competence:** The analysis validated that the negative impact of avoidance goals was mediated by perceived competence. This suggests that the process by which avoidance goals harm well-being and performance is through their tendency to lower an individual's sense of their own competence.
*   **Performance and Intrinsic Motivation:** Related research building on this paper notes that avoidance goals can trigger worry and anxiety during tasks, which in turn undermines both performance and intrinsic motivation. In contrast, approach goals (both mastery and performance-approach) are generally associated with positive outcomes like higher grades and continued interest.

**4. Contribution & Significance**
This paper was instrumental in revising the prevailing achievement goal model. By reintroducing the approach-avoidance distinction, Elliot and his colleagues proposed a trichotomous framework (mastery, performance-approach, and performance-avoidance goals), which has since become a standard in the field. The study underscored the critical importance of not just *what* goals people pursue, but *how* they pursue them—whether they are moving toward a positive state or away from a negative one. The findings demonstrated that avoidance-based self-regulation is a costly strategy that negatively impacts well-being and achievement, highlighting the need for both researchers and educators to consider the underlying motives behind students' goals.
