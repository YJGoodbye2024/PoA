## 1. Official Abstract
"Openness to experience is the broadest personality domain of the Big Five, including a mix of traits relating to intellectual curiosity, intellectual interests, perceived intelligence, imagination, creativity, artistic and aesthetic interests, emotional and fantasy richness, and unconventionality. Likewise, creative achievement is a broad construct, comprising creativity across the arts and sciences. The aim of this study was to clarify the relationship between openness to experience and creative achievement. Toward this aim, I factor analyzed a battery of tests of cognitive ability, working memory, Intellect, Openness, affect, and intuition among a sample of English Sixth Form students (N = 146). Four factors were revealed: explicit cognitive ability, intellectual engagement, affective engagement, and aesthetic engagement. Intellectual Engagement was related to creative achievement in the sciences, whereas Affective and Aesthetic Engagement were related to creative achievement in the arts. These results are interpreted in the context of dual-process theory, and a new four-factor model of openness to experience is proposed."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. What follows is a synopsis organized by the paper's original sections.

### **Introduction**
The introduction establishes that Openness to Experience is the Big Five personality trait most consistently linked to creativity. However, both "Openness to Experience" and "creative achievement" are broad constructs. The paper notes that creative achievement can be divided into arts and sciences, and recent research has split Openness to Experience into two aspects: Intellect (associated with reasoning and abstract analysis) and Openness (associated with aesthetic and perceptual engagement). The primary goal of this study is to "clarify the relationship between openness to experience and creative achievement" by investigating the sub-traits that constitute Openness and how they relate to creativity in both the arts and sciences. The author proposes that a finer-grained analysis will reveal differential predictions, with Intellect-related traits predicting scientific creativity and Openness-related traits predicting artistic creativity.

### **Method**
**Participants:** The study involved 146 English Sixth Form students (equivalent to 11th and 12th graders in the U.S.) from a coeducational state school, with an average age of 17.11 years.

**Procedure:** Participants completed a series of questionnaires and cognitive tests. Creative achievement was measured using the Creative Achievement Questionnaire (CAQ), which asks individuals to endorse their real-world accomplishments across 10 domains, which were then categorized into "Arts" and "Sciences."

**Measures:** A wide array of measures was used to assess personality and cognitive abilities, including:
*   **Personality:** The Big Five Aspect Scales to measure Intellect and Openness, as well as scales for Need for Cognition, Sensation Seeking, and Positive and Negative Affect.
*   **Cognitive Ability:** Tests included measures of fluid reasoning, verbal reasoning, and working memory.
*   **Intuition:** The Rational-Experiential Inventory was used to measure intuitive and rational thinking styles.

### **Results**
A factor analysis was performed on the results of all the tests. This analysis revealed four distinct factors, which Dr. Kaufman labeled:

1.  **Explicit Cognitive Ability:** This factor was strongly loaded with measures of fluid reasoning, verbal reasoning, and working memory. It represents the traditional notion of general cognitive ability.
2.  **Intellectual Engagement:** This factor was primarily defined by traits like "ideas," "intellect," "need for cognition," and "verbal reasoning." It reflects a drive to engage with and understand complex information.
3.  **Affective Engagement:** This factor was associated with positive affect, emotional engagement, and sensation seeking.
4.  **Aesthetic Engagement:** This factor was loaded with traits like "fantasy," "aesthetics," "feelings," and an intuitive thinking style. It captures a preference for beauty, fantasy, and artistic experiences.

The study then examined how these four factors correlated with creative achievement in the arts and sciences. The key findings were:
*   **Creative achievement in the sciences** was significantly predicted only by **Intellectual Engagement**.
*   **Creative achievement in the arts** was significantly predicted by both **Affective Engagement** and **Aesthetic Engagement**.
*   **Explicit Cognitive Ability** did not significantly predict creative achievement in either the arts or the sciences in this particular sample.

### **Discussion**
The discussion section interprets these findings within the framework of dual-process theories of cognition. The author argues that the results support a more nuanced view of Openness to Experience. Instead of a single trait, it appears to be a composite of different modes of cognitive and emotional engagement.

The study proposes a new four-factor model of Openness to Experience based on the results: Explicit Cognitive Ability, Intellectual Engagement, Affective Engagement, and Aesthetic Engagement. The author concludes that "Intellectual Engagement is clearly most relevant to creative achievement in the sciences, whereas Affective and Aesthetic Engagement are clearly most relevant to creative achievement in the arts." This supports the central hypothesis that different components of the broader Openness to Experience trait relate to different domains of creative accomplishment. The findings help to explain why previous research has found inconsistent links between general intelligence and creativity; the drive for *intellectual engagement* may be more critical than raw cognitive horsepower for scientific creativity. Similarly, artistic creativity is tied more to emotional and aesthetic ways of experiencing the world.
