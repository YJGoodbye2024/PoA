## 1. Official Abstract
"Here we show that if an adult demonstrates a new way to execute a task to a group of infants aged 14 months, the children will use this action to achieve the same goal only if they consider it to be the most rational alternative. Our results indicate that imitation of goal-directed action by preverbal infants is a selective, interpretative process, rather than a simple re-enactment of the means used by a demonstrator, as was previously thought."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not directly accessible. The following synopsis is constructed from detailed summaries and analyses from multiple academic sources.

### Introduction & Background
The paper builds upon earlier imitation research, particularly studies by Meltzoff, which showed that 14-month-old infants would re-enact an unusual action (e.g., turning on a light-box with their forehead) a week after seeing it demonstrated. The authors, Gergely, Bekkering, and Király, aimed to investigate whether this imitation is a simple, automatic re-enactment or a more sophisticated, rational process. They hypothesized that infants' imitation is selective and interpretative, suggesting that infants evaluate the rationality of an observed action in relation to situational constraints. This "teleological reasoning" implies that infants expect agents to act efficiently towards a goal.

### Methods
The experiment involved 14-month-old infants who watched an adult model perform an unusual action: illuminating a light-box by touching it with her forehead. The infants were divided into two groups, each observing a different condition:

1.  **Hands-Occupied Condition:** The adult model pretended to be cold and wrapped a blanket around her shoulders, occupying her hands by holding the blanket. She then leaned forward and touched the light-box with her forehead to turn it on. In this scenario, using her head was the most rational action because her hands were unavailable.
2.  **Hands-Free Condition:** The adult model also wore a blanket, but her hands were placed visibly and freely on the table next to the light-box. She still chose to lean forward and use her forehead to illuminate the device. In this context, the choice to use her head over her readily available hands could be interpreted as having a specific, unstated reason, implying it was a necessary or better way to perform the action.

One week after observing one of these two conditions, the infants were presented with the light-box to see how they would attempt to turn it on.

### Results
The findings revealed a significant difference in the imitative behavior between the two groups.

*   In the **Hands-Free condition**, 69% of the infants re-enacted the novel head action to turn on the light.
*   In contrast, in the **Hands-Occupied condition**, only 21% of the infants imitated the head action, with the majority opting for the more direct and efficient method of using their hands.

This difference demonstrated that the infants did not automatically copy the observed action. Instead, their choice to imitate was modulated by the context.

### Discussion & Conclusion
The authors concluded that 14-month-old infants engage in a process of "rational imitation." They appear to analyze the efficiency of the model's action in relation to the situational constraints. When the model's hands were occupied, the infants inferred that using the head was a necessary, context-bound solution and that, in their own unconstrained situation, using their hands was the more rational choice. However, when the model freely chose the unusual head action despite her hands being available, the infants inferred that there must be a good reason for this specific action, leading them to copy it.

This study provides strong evidence that imitation in preverbal infants is not merely a motor mimicry process. Instead, it is a selective and inferential process where infants evaluate the rationality of an action. This suggests an early-developing "teleological stance" or an intuitive theory of rational action, where infants understand that actions are chosen to achieve goals in the most efficient manner possible.
