## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### Introduction

The authors begin by highlighting a gap in the research on prosocial behaviors, which has often overlooked the multidimensional nature of such actions. They argue that existing measures tend to be "global," assessing general tendencies to help without distinguishing between different types of prosocial behavior across various contexts and motivations. The paper asserts that "there are different types of prosocial behaviors and that these types are related differently to theoretically related constructs." To address this, the primary goal of the study was to develop and validate a new multidimensional instrument, the Prosocial Tendencies Measure (PTM), specifically for late adolescents. The PTM was designed to assess six distinct types of prosocial behavior:

*   **Public:** Helping in front of others to gain approval.
*   **Anonymous:** Helping without the recipient knowing who provided the assistance.
*   **Dire:** Assisting in crisis or emergency situations.
*   **Emotional:** Providing aid in emotionally evocative circumstances.
*   **Compliant:** Helping when requested to do so.
*   **Altruistic:** Assisting out of a genuine concern for others, often at a personal cost.

The authors hypothesized that these different forms of prosocial behavior would show unique relationships with various socio-cognitive and emotional variables, such as sympathy, perspective-taking, and moral reasoning. They also predicted gender differences, expecting females to score higher on emotional, altruistic, and compliant prosocial behaviors, and males to score higher on public prosocial behaviors.

### Methods

The research was conducted in two parts.

**Study 1:**
The primary validation study involved 249 undergraduate students (145 female, 104 male) from a Midwestern university, with a mean age of 19.89 years. Participants completed a battery of self-report questionnaires, including:
*   **The Prosocial Tendencies Measure (PTM):** The newly developed scale to measure the six proposed types of prosocial behavior.
*   **Additional Measures:** Instruments to assess theoretically related concepts, such as sympathy, perspective-taking, personal distress, social desirability, and prosocial moral reasoning (using the PROM-R).

**Study 2:**
A second study was conducted to establish the test-retest reliability of the PTM. This involved a smaller sample of 40 college students (28 female, 12 male) with a mean age of 22.9 years, who completed the PTM on two separate occasions.

### Results

The findings from both studies provided strong support for the PTM's reliability and validity.

**Study 1:**
*   **Factor Analysis:** A confirmatory factor analysis supported the proposed six-factor structure of the PTM (Public, Anonymous, Dire, Emotional, Compliant, and Altruistic), demonstrating that these are distinct, though related, constructs.
*   **Correlations:** The different PTM subscales showed unique patterns of correlation with other variables. For instance, altruistic prosocial behavior was positively related to sympathy, perspective-taking, and internalized moral reasoning. In contrast, public prosocial behavior was positively associated with hedonistic and approval-oriented moral reasoning but negatively related to perspective-taking. These distinct correlational patterns supported the multidimensionality of prosocial behavior.
*   **Gender Differences:** The results confirmed the researchers' hypotheses regarding gender. Females reported significantly higher levels of anonymous, altruistic, compliant, and emotional prosocial behaviors.

**Study 2:**
*   **Reliability:** The test-retest results demonstrated that the PTM subscales were stable over time, further establishing the measure's reliability.

### Discussion/Conclusion

The authors conclude that the studies provide substantial evidence for the PTM as a reliable and valid instrument for measuring different types of prosocial behaviors in late adolescents. The key takeaway is the confirmation that prosocial behavior is not a single, uniform construct but is instead multifaceted. The distinct relationships between the six types of prosocial tendencies and various socio-cognitive and emotional factors underscore the importance of moving beyond global assessments of helping behavior.

The development of the PTM offers researchers a more nuanced tool to explore the motivations and individual differences underlying why, when, and how late adolescents engage in helping behaviors. The authors suggest that this multidimensional approach is crucial for advancing the understanding of prosocial development.
