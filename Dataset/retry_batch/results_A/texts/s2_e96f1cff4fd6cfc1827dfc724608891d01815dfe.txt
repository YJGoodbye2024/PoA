## 1. Official Abstract
"Time is an important dimension when individuals make decisions. Specifically, the time until a beneficial outcome can be received is viewed as a cost and is weighed against the benefits of the outcome. We propose that impulsive individuals experience time differently, that is with a higher cost. Impulsive subjects, therefore, overestimate the duration of time intervals and, as a consequence, discount the value of delayed rewards more strongly than do self-controlled individuals. The literature on time perception and impulsivity, however, is not clear cut and needs a better theoretical foundation. Here, we develop the theoretical background on concepts of time perception, which could lead to an empirically based notion of the association between an altered sense of time and impulsivity."

The 2008 paper by Wittmann and Paulus in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* puts forth a significant hypothesis linking three cognitive functions: decision-making, impulsivity, and the subjective perception of time. The central argument is that an individual's degree of impulsivity is directly related to how they experience the passage of time, which in turn governs their decision-making processes, particularly in choices involving delayed rewards.

**Core Hypothesis: Impulsivity and the Subjective Cost of Time**

The authors propose that impulsive individuals perceive time with a "higher cost". This means that for a given objective duration (e.g., waiting five minutes), an impulsive person experiences the wait as subjectively longer and more burdensome than a less impulsive person. This subjective overestimation of time intervals is the core mechanism the authors suggest to explain impulsive behavior. When faced with a choice between a small, immediate reward and a larger, delayed reward, the impulsive individual's distorted time perception inflates the "cost" of waiting, leading them to devalue, or "discount," the future reward more steeply. Consequently, they are more likely to opt for the immediate, smaller reward.

**Theoretical Framework: The Internal Clock Model**

To provide a theoretical basis for this phenomenon, the paper draws on established cognitive models of time perception, often referred to as the "internal clock" model. This model posits a pacemaker-accumulator mechanism:
*   A **pacemaker** generates neural pulses at a certain rate.
*   An **attentional gate** opens when an individual pays attention to time, allowing these pulses to flow to an **accumulator**.
*   The number of pulses collected in the accumulator represents the perceived duration of an interval.

Wittmann and Paulus argue that factors like increased arousal, which is often associated with impulsivity, could speed up this internal pacemaker. A faster pacemaker would cause more pulses to be accumulated in a given objective time, leading to a systematic overestimation of duration. This provides a neurocognitive explanation for why impulsive individuals might feel that "time passes too slowly" and find delays intolerable.

**Impact and Critical Analysis from Related Research**

The paper serves as a theoretical touchstone for subsequent empirical work. Researchers have tested and supported its predictions, noting that highly impulsive individuals do indeed tend to overestimate and under-produce time intervals. For example, when asked to reproduce a specific duration, impulsive subjects often produce a shorter interval, indicating that their internal clock ran faster. This framework has been applied to understand behaviors in drug-dependent individuals, who exhibit both high impulsivity and a less-extended future time perspective. Other studies confirm the correlation between temporal discounting and time perception, reinforcing the paper's central thesis. The theory effectively bridges the gap between the subjective experience of time and observable economic behaviors like intertemporal choice.

In summary, the paper provides a crucial theoretical model suggesting that impulsive decision-making is not merely a failure of self-control, but is potentially rooted in a fundamental difference in the subjective perception of time itself.
