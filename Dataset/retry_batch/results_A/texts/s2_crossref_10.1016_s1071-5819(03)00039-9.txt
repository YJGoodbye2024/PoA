## 1. Official Abstract
"The concept of trust is believed by some to compensate for feelings of uncertainty. Therefore, trust is considered to be crucial in people's decision to rely on a complex automated system to perform tasks for them. This experiment aimed to study the effects of errors on control allocation, and the mediating role of trust and self-confidence in the domain of route planning. Using a computer-based route planner, participants completed 10 route-planning trials in manual mode, and 10 in automatic mode, allowing participants to become equally experienced in operating both modes. During these so-called fixed trials, the numbers of errors in automatic as well as manual mode were systematically varied. Our results showed that high automation error rates (AERs) decreased levels of system trust compared to low AERs. Conversely, high manual error rates (MERs) resulted in lower levels of self-confidence compared to low MERs, although to a lesser extent. Moreover, the difference between measures of trust and self-confidence proved to be highly predictive of the number of times automatic mode was selected during the six free trials. Additionally, results suggest a fundamental bias to trust one's own abilities over those of the system. Finally, evidence indicating a relationship between trust and self-confidence is discussed."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on abstracts and citations from related academic works.

This study investigates how errors made by an automated system and by a human user affect the user's trust in the system, their self-confidence, and their decision to use automation. The experiment centered on a route-planning task where participants used a computer-based route planner.

**Experimental Design**
The experiment was designed to give participants equal experience with both manual and automated control. Participants performed 10 trials in a manual route-planning mode and 10 trials in an automatic mode. During this phase, the researchers systematically manipulated the rate of errors, both for the automated system (Automation Error Rate - AER) and for the user in manual mode (Manual Error Rate - MER). Following these "fixed trials," participants were given six "free trials" where they could choose whether to use the manual or automatic mode.

**Key Findings**
The study produced several significant findings regarding the interplay between errors, trust, and control allocation:
*   **System Errors Erode Trust:** A high rate of errors from the automated system significantly decreased the participants' level of trust in that system. This finding is consistent with other research showing that trust in automated aids declines when they are imperfect.
*   **Personal Errors Erode Self-Confidence:** A high rate of errors made by the user in manual mode led to a decrease in their self-confidence. However, this reduction in self-confidence was less pronounced than the drop in system trust caused by automation errors.
*   **Trust vs. Self-Confidence Predicts Reliance:** The decision to use the automated system was strongly predicted by the difference between the user's trust in the system and their self-confidence. This dynamic between trust and self-confidence appears to be a critical factor in how users adapt to and rely on automation.
*   **Inherent Self-Bias:** The results suggest a "fundamental bias" where users tend to trust their own abilities more than those of the system. This inherent preference can lead to the disuse of automation, especially after system errors are observed.

In conclusion, the paper demonstrates that system performance is a powerful driver of user trust, while personal performance impacts self-confidence. The decision to delegate control to an automated system is not based on trust alone but on a comparative judgment between trust in the system and confidence in one's own capabilities.
