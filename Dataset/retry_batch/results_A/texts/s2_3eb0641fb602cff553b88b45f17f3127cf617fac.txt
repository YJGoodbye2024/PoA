## 1. Official Abstract
> Many negotiations provide opportunities for integrative agreements in which parties can maximize joint gains without competing for resources in a direct win-lose fashion. However, negotiators often settle for suboptimal compromise agreements rather than search for mutually beneficial, or integrative, agreements. We hypothesized that misperceptions of the other party's interests are a primary cause of suboptimal outcomes. Two studies examined the role of social perception in negotiation and the relationship between judgment accuracy and negotiation performance. Results indicated that: most negotiators enter negotiation expecting the other party's interests to be completely opposed to their own; negotiators learn about the potential for joint gain during negotiation; most learning occurs within the first few minutes of interaction; accurate perception of the other party's interests leads to better negotiation performance; negotiators who learn about the other party's interests in the early stages of negotiation earn higher payoffs than do those who learn during the later stages of negotiation; a substantial number of negotiators fail to realize when they have interests that are completely compatible with those of the other party and settle for suboptimal agreements; and the two types of judgment error, Fixed Sum Error and Incompatibility Error, appear to be unrelated, distinct judgment errors. We discuss the role of social judgment in negotiation and the generalizability of the results to real world negotiations.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation and critical analysis of the paper's core findings based on its citation and discussion in subsequent academic research.

The 1990 paper "Social perception in negotiation" by Leigh Thompson and Reid Hastie is a foundational work in negotiation research that empirically investigates how negotiators' perceptions of their counterparts' interests affect negotiation outcomes. The paper's central argument is that inaccurate social perceptions are a primary driver of suboptimal agreements. The research identified and provided evidence for several key cognitive biases in negotiation, most notably the "fixed-pie perception."

**Key Findings and Concepts:**

*   **Fixed-Pie Perception:** The most significant contribution of the paper is the empirical identification of the "fixed-pie perception" or "fixed-sum error". This is the erroneous belief that the other party's interests are directly and completely opposed to one's own. Thompson and Hastie were the first to measure this bias empirically by asking negotiators to fill out their counterpart's payoff schedule after a negotiation. They found that a majority of negotiators enter discussions with this assumption, which hinders the search for integrative, win-win solutions. This bias leads negotiators to focus on claiming value rather than creating it, as they incorrectly assume a purely distributive (win-lose) situation.

*   **Incompatibility Bias:** The research also identified a related but distinct error called the "incompatibility bias." This is the failure of negotiators to recognize when their interests are perfectly compatible with the other party's. Thompson and Hastie found that a significant portion of negotiators failed to identify these compatible interests, leading them to settle for less-than-optimal agreements on issues where both parties wanted the same thing.

*   **The Role of Learning in Negotiation:** The study demonstrated that while many negotiators start with a fixed-pie mindset, they can and do learn about the potential for joint gain during the negotiation process. Crucially, the research showed that most of this learning happens within the first few minutes of interaction. The speed of learning was directly correlated with outcomes; negotiators who achieved accurate judgments about their counterpart's priorities early in the process secured better joint outcomes.

*   **Judgment Accuracy and Outcomes:** A core conclusion of the paper is that judgment accuracy is strongly linked to negotiation performance. Accurate perceptions allow negotiators to identify opportunities for mutually beneficial trade-offs and to recognize compatible interests, leading to higher joint gains. Conversely, errors in social perception are a direct cause of inefficient agreements. The research established that effective negotiation is not just about argumentation but is fundamentally a process of learning and correcting social judgments.

In summary, Thompson and Hastie's paper shifted the focus of negotiation research toward the cognitive and perceptual processes of the individual negotiator. It provided empirical evidence that negotiators' mental models and biases are critical determinants of whether they achieve integrative, value-creating agreements or settle for suboptimal compromises. The concepts of the fixed-pie perception and incompatibility bias have become cornerstones of negotiation theory and training, highlighting the importance of overcoming these innate perceptual errors through active information exchange and learning.
