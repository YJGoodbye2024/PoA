## 1. Official Abstract
"What happens to the share of choices each item receives when the choice set boundaries are extended by adding a new item that is extremely good on one dimension but poor on the others? First, there is a substitution effect whereby the new item takes choice share mainly from similar items in the set. Second, there is an attraction effect resulting in a general shift of preference toward the added item. Experimental studies show that choice patterns conflict with current theoretical and common-sense ideas about the effect of added alternatives on choice."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. The following is a synopsis of its core arguments and findings, organized by the paper's original sections.

### **Introduction**
The paper investigates how adding a new alternative to a choice set affects the market shares of existing items. The authors challenge two prevailing classes of models. The first, based on Luce's (1959) choice axiom, posits a "proportionality" effect, where a new product draws share from existing products in proportion to their original shares. The second, exemplified by Tversky's (1972) "elimination-by-aspects" model, posits a "similarity" effect, where a new product draws share primarily from the most similar existing products. The authors propose and test two additional effects: the **substitution effect** and the **attraction effect**. The substitution effect is similar to the similarity effect, stating a new item takes share from those most like it. The attraction effect, however, is a counterintuitive phenomenon where adding a new, asymmetrically dominated or relatively inferior alternative can *increase* the choice share of the item it most resembles.

### **The Attraction and Substitution Effects**
This section details the theoretical framework. The authors define the **substitution effect** as the tendency for a new alternative to take the most share from its closest neighbors in a perceptual map. They contrast this with the **attraction effect**, which suggests that "adding a new alternative to a choice set can increase the probability of choosing an item from the original set." This violates the principle of "regularity," a fundamental assumption in many choice models which states that the market share of an alternative cannot be increased by adding another alternative to the choice set. The authors hypothesize that this attraction effect occurs when the added item is positioned to be clearly inferior to one of the existing options (asymmetrically dominated) but not all, thereby enhancing the perceived attractiveness of the dominating option.

### **Method**
The research was conducted through three separate experimental studies involving choices across different product categories: cars, restaurants, beers, lotteries, film, and television sets. In each study, participants were presented with choice sets. A "core set" of two or three alternatives was established first. Then, one or more "expanded sets" were created by adding a new alternative that was specifically designed to be either relatively inferior or asymmetrically dominated. For example, a new beer might be added that is priced higher than a target beer but has a lower quality rating. Participants were asked to make a choice from the presented set, assuming all unmentioned attributes were identical. The researchers then analyzed the changes in choice shares between the core and expanded sets to measure the direction and magnitude of the substitution and attraction effects.

### **Results**
The results from the three studies consistently demonstrated the existence of both the substitution and attraction effects, often in direct violation of the proportionality and regularity principles.

*   **Study 1:** Showed a strong attraction effect. For example, in a choice between two cars, adding a third car that was clearly inferior to one of the original two (but not the other) increased the share of the now-dominating car. This effect was significant and demonstrated that the "regularity condition is not a universal principle of choice behavior."
*   **Study 2:** Replicated the findings with different product categories (e.g., restaurants, beer, lotteries) and further strengthened the evidence for the attraction effect. The addition of a relatively inferior option consistently pulled share toward the most similar, superior option.
*   **Study 3:** Tested the boundary conditions of the effects. It found that the attraction effect was strongest when the added item was "relatively inferior" to a target item, rather than being "absolutely inferior" on all dimensions. The study confirmed that both substitution and attraction effects are necessary to "account for patterns of choice across different choice sets."

Across the studies, the attraction effect was powerful enough to reverse the choice shares of the original items in some cases.

### **Discussion**
The authors conclude that simple models of choice based on proportionality (like the Luce model) or similarity alone are insufficient to explain consumer behavior. The findings show that the context of a choice, specifically the composition of the choice set, has a significant and predictable impact on preferences. The attraction effect, in particular, demonstrates a violation of the regularity principle, a cornerstone of many rational choice models. This implies that the relative positions of alternatives can be manipulated to enhance the appeal of a target product. The paper suggests that these effects have practical implications for product positioning and market-entry strategies, as a firm can potentially increase the market share of a product by introducing a specific type of "decoy" competitor.
