## 1. Official Abstract
"Subjects were less willing to pay for government medical insurance for diseases when the number of people who could not be cured was higher, holding constant the number who could be cured. In a second experiment, willingness to pay (from a hypothetical government windfall) for risk reduction was unaffected by whether the risk was described in terms of percentage or number of lives saved, even though subjects knew that the risks in question differed in prevalence. These results are consistent with the findings of Fetherstonhaugh et al., Jenni and Loewenstein, and others. I suggest that these results can beE explained in terms of a general tendency to confuse proportions and differences, a confusion that is analogous to other confusions of quantitative dimensions in children, adults, the news media, and perhaps even the epidemiological literature."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. A detailed interpretation based on the abstract and related literature is provided below.

The paper investigates a cognitive bias where individuals confuse absolute and relative risk when making valuation judgments, particularly concerning lifesaving or risk-reducing measures. The core argument, as presented in the abstract and supported by two key experiments, is that people's valuation of an intervention is disproportionately influenced by the proportion of people saved (relative risk) rather than the absolute number of people saved.

**Experiment 1: Valuation of Medical Insurance**
In the first experiment, subjects were asked about their willingness to pay for government medical insurance. The key finding was that willingness to pay decreased when the number of people who *could not* be cured was higher, even when the absolute number of people who *could* be cured by the insurance remained constant. This suggests that the perceived value of the intervention was diminished by the larger context of uncured individuals, indicating that the *proportion* of people saved was more influential than the absolute number. This effect is a form of "proportion dominance."

**Experiment 2: Risk Reduction Framing**
The second experiment explored how willingness to pay for risk reduction was affected by its description. Subjects were presented with scenarios where a risk was described either in terms of the "percentage of lives saved" or the "number of lives saved." The experiment found that willingness to pay was not significantly different between these two frames, despite subjects being aware that the total populations at risk (and therefore the absolute numbers of lives saved) were different. This result further supports the idea that people anchor on the numerical value presented (whether it's a percentage or a count) without adequately integrating the base rate or denominator, leading to a confusion between relative and absolute measures of risk.

**General Discussion and Conclusion**
The author posits that these findings are explained by a general cognitive tendency to confuse proportions and differences. This confusion is not isolated to these experiments but is analogous to similar errors in quantitative reasoning observed in various groups, from children to adults and even in media reporting. The paper connects these results to a broader literature on valuation and risk perception, citing similar findings by researchers like Fetherstonhaugh, Jenni, and Loewenstein, which collectively highlight a bias where the proportion of a reference group saved heavily influences valuation, often more than the absolute number of lives at stake.
