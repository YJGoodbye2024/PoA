## 1. Official Abstract
In order to examine the effects of possible changes in the social organization of prisons, a comparison was made of three experimental prison regimes, using 60 non-convict volunteer subjects. A baseline regime was modelled closely on existing medium-high security prisons. The two other regimes differed in the degree of individuality and participation accorded the prisoners. In an independent groups design with one replication, each group (six prisoners and four officers) spent four days in the experimental prison. The three regimes differed significantly in general atmosphere, and the character of the relations that developed between oficers and prisoners. The results were interpreted as further evidence that hostile affiontive relations in prisons are a function of the social organization of prisons rather than the personal characteristics of the participants.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on an analysis of third-party sources.

The 1979 study by S. H. Lovibond, Mithiran, and W. G. Adams is a significant piece of research in social psychology, primarily known for being a conceptual replication and extension of the famous 1971 Stanford Prison Experiment (SPE). While the SPE concluded that situational factors could lead ordinary individuals to behave in tyrannical or pathological ways, the Lovibond et al. study introduced crucial variations that challenged this deterministic conclusion. The Australian study is often cited in critiques of the SPE to argue that the behavior of participants is not an inevitable result of the prison environment itself, but is heavily influenced by the leadership, instructions, and organizational structure provided.

The core of the experiment was its comparison of three distinct prison regimes over a four-day period, using non-convict volunteers as "prisoners" and "officers". This design allowed for a direct test of how altering the social organization of the prison could affect behavior.

The three experimental conditions were:
1.  **Standard Custodial Regime:** This was the baseline condition, designed to closely model the environment of a typical medium-to-high security prison of the time. It served as a parallel to the setup of the Stanford Prison Experiment.
2.  **Individuality-Focused Regime:** In this condition, the prison environment was modified to afford prisoners a greater degree of self-respect and individuality.
3.  **Participatory Regime:** This regime went a step further by treating prisoners as individuals and actively including them in certain decision-making processes within the simulated prison.

The findings demonstrated significant differences in atmosphere and interpersonal relationships across the three conditions. Unlike the uniform hostility and abuse that emerged in the Stanford Prison Experiment, the Lovibond study found that such negative outcomes were not guaranteed. In the conditions where prisoners were treated with more dignity and allowed participation, the hostile relations did not develop to the same extent as in the standard custodial regime.

The study's primary conclusion was that hostile and confrontational relationships in prisons are a direct function of the prison's social organization, rather than an automatic consequence of the personal traits of the guards and prisoners. This suggests that the extreme behaviors seen in the SPE may have been heavily influenced by the specific instructions and expectations communicated by the researchers (i.e., Zimbardo's role as "prison superintendent"). The Lovibond et al. experiment is therefore critically important as it provides evidence that when the social structure of the institution is changed to be less deindividuating and more participatory, the "tyrannical" behavior is not a necessary outcome.
