## 1. Official Abstract
The choice overload hypothesis states that an increase in the number of options to choose from may lead to adverse consequences such as a decrease in the motivation to choose or the satisfaction with the finally chosen option. A number of studies found strong instances of choice overload in the lab and in the field, but others found no such effects or found that more choices may instead facilitate choice and increase satisfaction. In a meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments (N = 5,036), we found a mean effect size of virtually zero but considerable variance between studies. While further analyses indicated several potentially important preconditions for choice overload, no sufficient conditions could be identified. However, some idiosyncratic moderators proposed in single studies may still explain when and why choice overload reliably occurs; we review these studies and identify possible directions for future research.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. A detailed interpretation based on the abstract and other available information is provided below.

The target paper, "Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload," published in the *Journal of Consumer Research*, presents a significant challenge to the widely held "choice overload" hypothesis. This hypothesis posits that while a greater number of options can initially seem appealing, an excessive number can lead to negative outcomes, such as diminished motivation to make a choice and reduced satisfaction with the chosen option.

**Detailed Interpretation**

The core of the paper is a meta-analysis that synthesizes the results of 50 different experiments, encompassing 63 unique conditions and a total of 5,036 participants. The authors' primary finding is that, when all the studies are aggregated, the average effect of having more choices is "virtually zero." This suggests that, contrary to the popular narrative, increasing the number of options does not reliably lead to negative consequences for the decision-maker.

However, the analysis did not end there. The researchers also found "considerable variance between studies," indicating that while the average effect was null, the outcomes in individual studies varied significantly. This led them to investigate potential moderating variables—specific conditions that might trigger choice overload.

The paper explores several "potentially important preconditions" for choice overload to occur, but it was unable to identify any single factor or set of factors that were sufficient to reliably cause the effect. In other words, even when certain conditions thought to cause choice overload were present, the negative effects did not consistently appear.

The authors conclude that the choice overload effect is not as robust or universal as previously believed. They suggest that the phenomenon may depend on "idiosyncratic moderators" that were specific to individual studies. The paper calls for future research to focus on identifying these more nuanced conditions under which having too many options might genuinely become a problem. This work reframes the "paradox of choice" not as a general rule, but as a more specific phenomenon that occurs only under a particular, yet-to-be-fully-defined, set of circumstances.

Another meta-analysis from 2015, which included the studies from Scheibehenne et al.'s 2010 paper, identified four key factors that moderate choice overload: the complexity of the choice set, the difficulty of the decision task, the uncertainty of the chooser's preferences, and the decision goal. That analysis found that when these moderators are taken into account, the overall effect of assortment size becomes significant.
