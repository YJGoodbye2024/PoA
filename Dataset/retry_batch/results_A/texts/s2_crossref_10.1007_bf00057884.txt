## 1. Official Abstract
"We investigate the relation between judgments of probability and preferences between bets. A series of experiments provides support for the competence hypothesis that people prefer betting on their own judgment over an equiprobable chance event when they consider themselves knowledgeable, but not otherwise. They even pay a significant premium to bet on their judgments. These data cannot be explained by aversion to ambiguity, because judgmental probabilities are more ambiguous than chance events. We interpret the results in terms of the attribution of credit and blame. The possibility of inferring beliefs from preferences is questioned."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible, and the following is a synopsis organized by the paper's original sections.

### **Introduction**
The paper challenges the standard model of decision-making, which derives subjective probabilities from preferences between bets. It questions whether preferences under uncertainty are solely governed by the perceived likelihood of events or if other psychological factors play a role. The authors introduce an alternative, the "competence hypothesis." This hypothesis posits that people's willingness to bet on an uncertain event depends not just on its probability, but also on their perceived knowledge or competence regarding that event.

The authors contrast this with the well-known "ambiguity aversion" described by the Ellsberg paradox, where people prefer to bet on known probabilities over unknown or vague ones. They argue that ambiguity aversion cannot explain all phenomena. The competence hypothesis predicts that if people feel knowledgeable in a particular domain, they will prefer to bet on their own judgments, even if those judgments are inherently more ambiguous than a matched-probability chance event (like a lottery). This preference stems from the idea that people derive greater satisfaction or credit from a win based on their skill or knowledge, and less blame for a loss, compared to an outcome determined by pure chance.

### **Experiment 1: General Knowledge**
*   **Methods:** Participants answered general knowledge multiple-choice questions and stated their confidence (subjective probability) that their answer was correct. They were then presented with two options: (1) a bet that their answer was correct, or (2) a chance-based bet with a probability of winning equal to their stated confidence level. For example, if a participant was 75% confident in their answer, they could either bet on their answer or on a lottery with a 75% chance of winning. Participants were randomly assigned to a "paid" group, where choices had real monetary consequences, and a "nonpaid" group.
*   **Results:** A significant majority of participants, in both paid and nonpaid groups, preferred to bet on their own judgment rather than on the matched-chance lottery, especially when their confidence was high (above 50%). Those in the paid group were even willing to pay a premium to bet on their answers.
*   **Conclusion:** The results strongly support the competence hypothesis. The preference to bet on one's own judgment cannot be explained by ambiguity aversion, as the judgmental probabilities are vaguer than the explicit lottery probabilities. Instead, the feeling of competence in the domain of general knowledge drove the preference.

### **Experiment 2: Chance Events**
*   **Methods:** This experiment was designed to test the competence hypothesis in a domain where participants could not feel knowledgeable: predicting the outcomes of random chance devices. Participants were shown two bags of colored chips, one with a known distribution (e.g., 50 red, 50 white) and one with an unknown distribution. They were asked to judge the probability of drawing a specific color from each. They then chose whether to bet on their judgment about the known bag, their judgment about the unknown bag, or a chance lottery matched to their probability judgment.
*   **Results:** When betting on chance events about which they had no special knowledge, the pattern reversed. Participants showed a clear preference for betting on the known-probability chance event over their own (more ambiguous) judgments about the contents of the unknown bag. This is consistent with classic ambiguity aversion.
*   **Conclusion:** This experiment demonstrates the boundary conditions of the competence effect. When individuals feel they are in a domain of pure chance with no basis for expertise, they prefer clear, unambiguous probabilities over vague ones, as predicted by ambiguity aversion theories. This shows that the competence effect is not universal but is specific to domains where a person feels knowledgeable.

### **Experiment 3: Football**
*   **Methods:** This experiment extended the investigation to the domain of sports. Participants, who were either football fans or non-fans, made judgments about the outcomes of upcoming professional football games. They assigned probabilities to which of two teams would win and then chose between betting on their judgment or a matched-probability lottery.
*   **Results:** Football fans overwhelmingly preferred to bet on their judgments about the games rather than on the chance lottery. Non-fans, in contrast, were roughly indifferent between the two types of bets.
*   **Conclusion:** This provides further evidence for the competence hypothesis. The fans, who perceived themselves as knowledgeable, demonstrated the competence effect. The non-fans, who lacked this feeling of competence, did not. This highlights that the effect is driven by self-perceived expertise.

### **General Discussion**
The authors conclude that preferences under uncertainty are shaped by more than just the probability and magnitude of potential outcomes. The source of the uncertainty is critical. The "competence hypothesis" successfully explains why people might prefer to bet on their own ambiguous judgments in one context (like general knowledge or sports) but avoid ambiguity in another (like a random lottery).

The key mechanism proposed is attribution. When betting on one's knowledge, a win is credited to one's skill, and a loss can be explained away. In a chance-based game, both success and failure are attributed to luck, which is less psychologically rewarding. This discrepancy between preference and belief—preferring to bet on event A over event B despite judging B to be at least as probable—calls into question the foundational principle of decision theory that beliefs can be directly inferred from preferences.
