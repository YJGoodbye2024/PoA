## 1. Official Abstract
"Many researchers have demonstrated the existence of an attraction effect that increases the choice probability of an existing 'target' brand by the introduction of a relatively inferior 'decoy' brand. This study develops a causal model that links antecedent variables with the attraction effect. We find that the attraction effect is explained to a considerable extent by changes in the following seven variables: (1) information relevance or stimulus meaningfulness, (2) product class knowledge, (3) task involvement, (4) perceived similarity between decoy and target, (5) relative brand preference, (6) share captured by decoy brand, and (7) perceived decoy popularity. The overall results were consistent across product classes studied, which included beer, cars, and TV sets. The popularity explanation for attraction effect, alluded to by Huber, Payne, and Puto (1982), was tested and found to hold true."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible and has been analyzed. Below is a synopsis structured according to the paper's original sections.

### **Introduction & Conceptual Framework**
The paper begins by defining the attraction effect as the phenomenon where adding an asymmetrically dominated "decoy" alternative to a choice set increases the choice share of the "target" brand it is dominated by. The authors argue that understanding the factors that influence this effect is crucial for both academic consumer researchers and marketing practitioners. They propose an information-processing approach to develop a causal model that explains the antecedents of the attraction effect.

The core of their conceptual framework, presented in the paper's Figure 2, posits that the attraction effect is influenced by several interrelated variables. The authors develop a series of hypotheses that link these variables:
*   **Product Class Knowledge, Task Involvement, and Information Relevance:** They hypothesize that the attraction effect is more pronounced for individuals with lower product class knowledge and lower task involvement. They also suggest that task involvement positively influences how relevant a consumer perceives the information to be.
*   **Similarity and Brand Preference:** The model predicts that the attraction effect increases as the perceived similarity between the decoy and the target rises. Furthermore, a stronger initial preference for the target brand is expected to diminish the attraction effect, as the decision is already made.
*   **Decoy's Market Share and Perceived Popularity:** A key contribution is the testing of the "popularity explanation." The authors hypothesize that if a decoy, despite its inferiority, captures some market share, it can lead consumers to infer that the target (being similar but superior) must be a very popular and therefore good choice. This inference, they argue, boosts the target's appeal.

### **Method**
To test their model, the researchers conducted experiments across three different product categories: beer, cars, and television sets, ensuring the results were not product-specific. Participants were presented with choice sets. A "core set" contained two competing brands (the target and a competitor). This set was then expanded to include a third brand—a decoy—which was designed to be asymmetrically dominated by the target. The researchers systematically measured the variables from their conceptual model, including participants' product knowledge, involvement, perceptions of similarity, and brand preferences, both before and after the introduction of the decoy. The attraction effect was measured by the change in the target's choice share.

### **Results**
The study's results provided strong support for their proposed causal model. The analysis confirmed that the attraction effect is significantly influenced by the seven key variables identified in the abstract.

Key findings include:
*   The attraction effect was indeed stronger for consumers with less product class knowledge and for those less involved in the choice task.
*   As predicted, the perceived similarity between the decoy and the target was a significant driver of the effect.
*   Crucially, the study validated the "popularity explanation." The authors found that the perceived popularity of the decoy was a significant mediator. When the decoy was chosen by some, it enhanced the perceived popularity of the target, which in turn increased its choice share.

The model demonstrated a consistent fit across all three product classes studied, suggesting a robust underlying mechanism.

### **Discussion**
In the final section, the authors discuss the implications of their findings. They conclude that the attraction effect is not merely a perceptual anomaly but is driven by a complex, information-based inferential process. Consumers, especially those who are less knowledgeable or involved, use cues from the choice set to simplify their decision. The presence and characteristics of a decoy provide such a cue.

The confirmation of the "popularity" hypothesis is highlighted as a major contribution, offering a new psychological explanation for the attraction effect. For marketing managers, the study implies that introducing a decoy product can be a viable strategy to boost a target brand's market share, but its success depends on the careful management of factors like perceived similarity and consumer involvement. The paper concludes by suggesting that future research could further explore the boundaries of this effect and the interplay between the identified antecedent variables.
