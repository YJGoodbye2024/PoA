## 1. Official Abstract

The following is the abstract for the book, which outlines its primary goals and scope:

> "A biological understanding of social behavior is now part of modern science. We know that previously assumed social or cultural causes of behavior have their origins, at least in part, in human biology. Similarly, we now know that social circumstances (such as stressful working conditions) can have profound effects on our biological functioning. The purpose of this book is to introduce scholars to biological and evolutionary perspectives on organizational behavior (OB). Its specific goals are to: (1) start a dialogue between OB scholars and biologically oriented scientists; (2) create a theoretical and empirical foundation for a biological and evolutionary perspective on OB; (3) show how a biological and evolutionary perspective can be a meta-theoretical lens for OB; (4) articulate the methods and research designs for conducting research on the biology of OB; and (5) show what a biological and evolutionary perspective on OB looks like for a variety of organizational topics."

"The Biological Foundations of Organizational Behavior" serves as a foundational text advocating for the integration of biology—specifically evolutionary psychology and behavioral genetics—into the field of organizational behavior (OB). The editors, Colarelli and Arvey, argue that while other social sciences have embraced biological perspectives, OB has been slow to do so, thereby missing a significant part of the puzzle of human behavior in the workplace. The book is structured to demonstrate how this integration can provide a more robust, meta-theoretical foundation for understanding and predicting behavior in organizations.

The core of the book is organized into thematic sections, with chapters authored by experts in each domain:

*   **Introduction: Biology and Organizational Behavior:** In the opening chapter, Colarelli and Arvey discuss the historical reluctance of OB scholars to adopt biological viewpoints and argue for why such a perspective is now essential. They posit that because humans are biological creatures, their makeup significantly influences workplace behavior, and understanding this can lead to more effective organizational interventions. The introduction makes the case that adding a biological lens will enhance the explanatory and predictive power of OB research.

*   **Genetics, Individual Differences, and Work Behavior:** This section delves into how genetic predispositions influence employee traits and actions. Chapters explore the implications of molecular genetics for OB research, the heritable components of workplace attitudes and emotions, and the biological underpinnings of entrepreneurship. For instance, research indicates that traits like leadership tendency and risk tolerance have significant heritable components. The central argument is not that biology is destiny, but that genetic factors interact with environmental ones to shape behavior.

*   **Physiology, Health, and the Brain:** This part of the book examines the neurobiological and physiological systems that affect employee behavior and well-being. Topics include how neurobiological systems relate to organizational life, the impact of physiological functioning on employee health, and how chronic stress can impair cognitive performance and decision-making by affecting hormones like cortisol. This section highlights the direct link between workplace conditions and an employee's biological state.

*   **Evolution and Organization:** This section applies principles of evolutionary psychology to understand organizational structures and dynamics. Chapters cover topics such as leader-follower relations (viewed through a "service-for-prestige" theory), the nature of human cooperation, decision-making in groups, and the unique dynamics of family firms through the lens of kinship. A key concept is "evolutionary mismatch," which describes the disconnect between our evolved psychological mechanisms and the novel environments of modern organizations. For example, our minds evolved for small, kin-based groups, which has implications for communication, identity, and cooperation in large, anonymous corporations.

*   **Challenges Ahead:** The concluding chapter provides a critical assessment of the promises and limitations of applying a biological approach to organizational studies. It acknowledges that biology is not a "silver bullet" but rather one essential part of a multi-faceted understanding of human behavior. The book concludes with a call for greater interdisciplinary collaboration between biologists and social scientists to build a more comprehensive science of organizational behavior.

In essence, the book is a manifesto and a roadmap for a new subdiscipline within organizational behavior, one that acknowledges the deep influence of our evolutionary past and biological present on life in the workplace.
