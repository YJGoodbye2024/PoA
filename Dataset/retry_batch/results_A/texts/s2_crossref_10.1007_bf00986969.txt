## 1. Official Abstract
> The tendency for negative information to have more-weight than equally extreme or equally likely positive information appears in a variety of cognitive processing tasks, but has rarely been documented empirically in politics. This paper provides evidence for two types of negativity effects in electoral behavior: negativity in the formation of impressions (of Humphrey and Nixon in 1968, of McGovern and Nixon in 1972, and of Carter and Reagan in 1980), and negativity as a consequence of impressions (in the 1974 and 1978 congressional elections). Both post hoc rationalization and the nonequivalence of the positive and negative information were examined and ruled out as artifactual explanations for these results. Discussion centered around two possible explanations for negativity, a cost-orientation hypothesis (which holds that people are more strongly motivated to avoid costs than to approach gains) and a figure-ground hypothesis (which holds that negative information stands out against a general positive background).

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on analyses in related academic literature.

Richard R. Lau's 1982 paper, "Negativity in political perception," is a foundational work in political psychology that empirically documents the "negativity effect" in the context of voter behavior. The central thesis is that negative information has a greater impact on an individual's evaluation of political candidates than positive information of equal weight. Lau's analysis was among the first to systematically apply this concept, long studied in social psychology, to the political realm.

The paper provides evidence for two distinct types of negativity effects in voting:
1.  **Negativity in Impression Formation**: This refers to the process where negative information is more influential than positive information when voters are forming their overall opinion of a candidate. Lau demonstrated this effect using data from the 1968 (Humphrey vs. Nixon), 1972 (McGovern vs. Nixon), and 1980 (Carter vs. Reagan) presidential elections.
2.  **Negativity as a Consequence of Impressions**: This effect occurs after an impression has been formed. It suggests that a negative opinion of a candidate has a greater impact on voting decisions than a positive opinion. This was documented using data from the 1974 and 1978 midterm congressional elections.

Lau's paper is significant for proposing and testing two primary theoretical explanations for why this negativity bias exists:
*   **The Figure-Ground Hypothesis**: This perceptual theory posits that in the generally positive landscape of political discourse (where candidates try to present themselves in the best light), negative information is rare and therefore stands out, much like a dark object on a light background. This salience gives it greater weight in cognitive processing.
*   **The Cost-Orientation Hypothesis**: This motivational theory suggests that humans are psychologically predisposed to be more sensitive to potential costs and threats than to potential gains. From an evolutionary perspective, avoiding danger is more critical for survival than approaching rewards. In a political context, this translates to voters being more strongly motivated to avoid a "bad" candidate (a potential cost) than to elect a "good" one (a potential gain).

The study also made a point to rule out alternative, artifactual explanations for the observed effects, such as voters simply engaging in post-hoc rationalization of their decisions. Lau's subsequent work, particularly his 1985 paper "Two Explanations for Negativity Effects in Political Behavior," further developed these foundational ideas.
