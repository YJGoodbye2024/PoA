## 1. Official Abstract
An overview of self-related superiority biases in social comparison is presented. Included are false consensus, false uniqueness, pluralistic ignorance, illusory superiority, unrealistic optimism, the sensitive and multifaceted self, the “Barnum” effect and the self-other asymmetry. Important conceptual and theoretical problems characterizing the field are pointed out and a review of cognitive explanations is presented. It is argued that most superiority biases are closely related to each other and that the self-enhancement motive or the pervasive tendency to see oneself in a favourable light offers a more fruitful approach towards their integration than the cognitive point of view. Some theoretical and heuristic implications of the proposed integration are briefly outlined.

The 1993 paper "Self-enhancement and Superiority Biases in Social Comparison" by Vera Hoorens is a foundational review article in the field of social psychology. Its primary contribution is to survey a wide range of seemingly distinct cognitive biases and argue that they can be best understood as manifestations of a single, powerful motivational drive: self-enhancement, or the tendency to maintain a favorable view of oneself.

The paper systematically identifies and connects several key biases, including:
*   **Illusory Superiority:** The tendency for people to overestimate their positive qualities and abilities relative to others, also known as the "better-than-average" effect.
*   **Unrealistic Optimism:** The belief that one is less likely than others to experience negative events and more likely to experience positive events.
*   **False Consensus & False Uniqueness:** The dual tendencies to overestimate how much others share one's own opinions (false consensus) while underestimating how much they share one's positive attributes and successful behaviors (false uniqueness).
*   **The Self-Other Asymmetry:** The phenomenon where comparative judgments differ depending on whether the self or the other person is the standard of comparison.

Hoorens' central thesis is that while purely cognitive explanations (e.g., information-processing shortcuts or egocentrism) have been proposed for each of these biases, they fail to provide a cohesive framework. Instead, she posits that the motivational need to "see oneself in a favourable light" is a more comprehensive and integrative explanation for why people consistently judge themselves as superior to others across so many domains. The paper is widely cited for bringing these various "superiority biases" under a single conceptual umbrella.

Subsequent research builds directly on Hoorens' framework. For example, studies on the "hubris hypothesis" investigate the social consequences of expressing these self-superiority beliefs, finding that while most people harbor such beliefs, they tend to dislike individuals who express them explicitly. Other researchers have used Hoorens' work as a foundational text when exploring how superiority biases function in specific contexts, such as health risks during the COVID-19 pandemic or the spread of misinformation. The paper is recognized for framing self-enhancement as a central motive in social comparison, influencing decades of research into how people evaluate themselves in relation to others.
