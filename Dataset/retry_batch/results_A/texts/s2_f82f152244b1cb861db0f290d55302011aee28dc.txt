## 1. Official Abstract
> " In a recent series of studies of the structure of affect, Watson and Tellegen and their colleagues have consistently found that 2 dominant dimensions, Positive Affect and Negative Affect, emerge in ratings of mood and in the structure of emotion terms. To provide reliable, valid, and brief scales for use in a variety of research settings, we developed 2 10-item mood scales that comprise the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS). The scales are shown to be highly internally consistent, largely uncorrelated, and stable at appropriate levels over a 2-mo time period. Normative data and factorial and external evidence of convergent and discriminant validity for the scales are also presented. "

### Detailed Interpretation

The 1988 paper by Watson, Clark, and Tellegen, "Development and validation of brief measures of positive and negative affect: the PANAS scales," introduced a pivotal instrument in psychological assessment: the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS). The primary goal was to address the need for a brief, reliable, and easy-to-administer tool to measure the two dominant, and largely independent, dimensions of mood: Positive Affect (PA) and Negative Affect (NA). Prior to the PANAS, many existing mood scales were deemed inadequate due to issues like low reliability or poor validity.

**Scale Development and Structure**
The authors developed two 10-item scales to measure PA and NA.
*   **Positive Affect (PA)** reflects the extent to which an individual feels enthusiastic, active, and alert. High PA is characterized by high energy, full concentration, and pleasurable engagement. The PA scale includes words like "interested," "excited," "strong," "enthusiastic," and "proud."
*   **Negative Affect (NA)** represents a general dimension of subjective distress, encompassing aversive mood states such as anger, guilt, fear, and nervousness. Low NA is a state of calmness. The NA scale includes words like "distressed," "upset," "guilty," "scared," and "irritable."

The items for the scales were empirically derived from a larger set of mood-describing adjectives.

**Validation and Psychometric Properties**
A significant portion of the paper is dedicated to demonstrating the scales' psychometric soundness. The authors conducted a series of studies using samples of undergraduate students, university employees, and psychiatric inpatients to establish the scales' properties.

The key findings on validation were:
*   **High Internal Consistency:** The items within both the PA and NA scales were shown to be highly related to each other, indicating that they reliably measure the same underlying construct.
*   **Structural Independence:** A crucial finding was that the PA and NA scales were largely uncorrelated, supporting the theoretical model that positive and negative affect are two distinct, independent dimensions rather than opposite ends of a single continuum. However, some later research has noted a weak correlation between the two factors.
*   **Temporal Stability:** The scales demonstrated appropriate stability over a two-month period, suggesting they can measure both transient mood states and more stable affective traits.
*   **Convergent and Discriminant Validity:** The PANAS was validated against other well-established psychological measures. For instance, the NA scale correlated strongly with measures of distress like the Hopkins Symptom Checklist and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), establishing convergent validity. At the same time, its weaker (negative) correlation with PA demonstrated discriminant validity.

**Impact and Significance**
The PANAS scales became a widely used tool in psychology for both clinical and research purposes. Their brevity and robust psychometric properties made them highly practical. The paper solidified the two-factor model of affect and provided researchers with a standardized method to assess emotional experience, which has been instrumental in studies on personality, well-being, and the differentiation between anxiety and depression. The scale has since been translated into numerous languages and adapted for various populations, including children.
