## 1. Official Abstract
"Research suggests that individuals mentally track the costs and benefits of a consumer transaction for the purpose of reconciling those costs and benefits on completion of the transaction (Prelec and Loewenstein 1998; Thaler 1980, 1985). In transactions where costs precede benefits, this can lead to a systematic and economically irrational attention to sunk costs (Arkes and Blumer 1985; Thaler 1980). In this article, we consider economic exchanges in which costs significantly precede benefits, as with many prepayment types of consumer transactions. We predict a consumer will gradually adapt to a historic cost with the passage of time, thereby decreasing its sunk-cost impact on the consumption of a pending benefit. We label this process of gradual adaptation to costs “payment depreciation.” In a series of experiments, we find evidence of payment depreciation across a range of consumer transactions and offer insight into the behavioral implications of temporally separating costs from benefits."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible. The following is a synopsis structured according to the paper's original sections.

### Introduction & Theoretical Background
The paper introduces the concept of "payment depreciation," which explores how the psychological impact of a cost diminishes over time. Building on Richard Thaler's theory of mental accounting, which posits that individuals create psychological accounts to track the costs and benefits of transactions, the authors investigate what happens when there is a significant time lag between payment (cost) and consumption (benefit). They theorize that consumers "open a mental account on making a payment for a product, but gradually adapt to that payment with the passage of time." This adaptation process is termed "payment depreciation," where the paid cost feels less significant as time passes, thereby reducing its influence on future consumption decisions. This stands in contrast to the traditional sunk-cost effect, where prior investments irrationally drive future decisions. The authors predict that the temporal separation between payment and consumption will weaken the sunk-cost effect.

### Experiment 1: Ski Resort Season Passes
*   **Method:** The first experiment was a field study conducted at a ski resort. The resort offered two types of season passes: a pure season pass and a pass consisting of four one-day lift tickets. Researchers surveyed 68 skiers who had purchased one of these passes, asking them about their purchase date, the price they paid, and their skiing behavior. The core of the study was to see if the time elapsed since the purchase of the pass influenced their decision to ski on a day with poor weather conditions (a measure of their motivation to use the pass).
*   **Results:** The findings supported the payment depreciation hypothesis. Skiers who had purchased their passes earlier in the season were less likely to ski on a given day compared to those who had purchased their passes more recently. This suggests that the psychological "sting" of the payment had depreciated over time for the early purchasers, making them less compelled to "get their money's worth" by skiing, especially in marginal conditions. The effect held even when controlling for skiing frequency and expertise.

### Experiment 2: Health Club Memberships
*   **Method:** This experiment examined the usage patterns of 149 members of a health club over a two-year period. Members paid an annual fee. The researchers tracked the monthly attendance of two groups: "new members" in their first year and "renewing members" in their second year. The hypothesis was that renewing members, having made their payment long ago, would show lower and less variable attendance compared to new members, for whom the payment was more recent.
*   **Results:** New members exhibited a distinct pattern: their attendance was highest in the month immediately following payment and then steadily declined over the year. In contrast, renewing members showed a much flatter and consistently lower attendance pattern throughout the year. This indicates that the sunk-cost effect of the annual fee was strongest for new members right after paying and depreciated over the year. For renewing members, the payment was a distant memory, and their attendance was driven more by routine than by the need to justify the cost.

### Experiment 3: Theater Tickets
*   **Method:** To establish a more direct causal link and control for external variables, the authors conducted a lab experiment. One hundred and twenty university staff members were sold tickets to a local theater production. Participants were divided into two groups: one group purchased their tickets two weeks before the performance, while the other group purchased them just one hour before. All participants were later informed that they had a schedule conflict and were asked if they would still attend the performance.
*   **Results:** A significantly higher percentage of participants who had purchased their tickets only an hour before the show indicated they would still attend, compared to those who had bought their tickets two weeks prior. This demonstrates that the perceived cost of the ticket and the resulting compulsion to attend were much stronger when the payment was recent. When the payment was made weeks in advance, its psychological weight had depreciated, making it easier for individuals to forgo the benefit.

### General Discussion
In the final section, the authors summarize their findings across the three experiments, concluding that there is strong evidence for the phenomenon of payment depreciation. They argue that the temporal separation between paying for and consuming a good or service causes the initial cost to be gradually "written off" in the consumer's mind. This has important implications for understanding the sunk-cost fallacy; the effect is not static but diminishes over time.

The paper discusses the marketing implications of this finding. For instance, businesses that rely on prepayment models (like season passes or annual memberships) should expect a drop-off in usage as the time from payment increases. Conversely, marketers can leverage payment depreciation by timing consumption opportunities long after the payment has been made, as consumers might be more willing to spend additional money (e.g., on concessions at a game for which tickets were bought months ago) because the initial ticket cost is no longer salient. The authors also differentiate their concept from simple memory decay, suggesting it is a more active process of psychological adaptation to a past cost.
