## 1. Official Abstract
The human experience of meaning in life is widely viewed as a cornerstone of well-being and a central human motivation. Self-reports of meaning in life relate to a host of important functional outcomes. Psychologists have portrayed meaning in life as simultaneously chronically lacking in human life as well as playing an important role in survival. Examining the growing literature on meaning in life, we address the question "How meaningful is life, in general?" We review possible answers from various psychological sources, some of which anticipate that meaning in life should be low and others that it should be high. Summaries of epidemiological data and research using two self-report measures of meaning in life suggest that life is pretty meaningful. Diverse samples rate themselves significantly above the midpoint on self-reports of meaning in life. We suggest that if meaning in life plays a role in adaptation, it must be commonplace, as our analysis suggests.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of this paper was accessible for analysis. The paper is a review article that synthesizes a large body of research to argue against the common psychological and philosophical notion that meaning in life is rare or elusive. The authors contend that, for most people, life is experienced as quite meaningful.

### **What Is Meaning in Life?**
The authors begin by defining meaning in life as a subjective experience composed of three primary themes:
*   **Purpose:** The sense that one's life is directed by valued goals.
*   **Significance:** The feeling that one's life matters and has value.
*   **Coherence:** The cognitive sense that one's life is comprehensible and makes sense.

They note that while scholarly definitions focus on these abstract components, laypeople often point to more concrete sources like family and relationships.

### **Is Meaning in Life Chronically Lacking?**
This section explores the prevalent view in psychology and existential philosophy that meaning is absent and a central problem of human existence. The authors cite figures like Frankl and Yalom, who described an "existential vacuum" and "meaninglessness" as fundamental givens of life. This perspective suggests that life should be rated as not very meaningful. However, the authors contrast this with the idea that if meaning is an adaptive quality that promotes survival, it must be common, not rare. This sets up the central question of the paper: which view is correct?

### **On Average, How Meaningful Is Life?**
This is the core empirical section of the paper. To answer their central question, the authors analyze data from a wide range of studies using two primary self-report scales: the Purpose in Life Test (PIL) and the Presence of Meaning subscale of the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ).

*   **Findings:** Their analysis of 73 studies using the PIL and 122 studies using the MLQ reveals that people across diverse samples consistently rate their lives as significantly above the neutral midpoint of these scales. The average scores indicate that "the typical response to these questionnaires is to report a fairly meaningful life."
*   **Epidemiological Data:** The authors supplement this by reviewing large-scale international surveys (e.g., the World Values Survey). This data also shows that a vast majority of respondents worldwide affirm that their lives have a clear sense of meaning or purpose.

From this evidence, the authors conclude that, contrary to the existentialist view, "life is pretty meaningful" for the average person.

### **How Can Life Be So Meaningful?**
The final section addresses *why* meaning is so commonplace, proposing several sources that are readily available in daily life.
*   **Positive Affect:** Good moods serve as information that life is going well, which in turn fosters a sense of meaning.
*   **Social Connection:** Feeling connected to others is a powerful and reliable source of meaning.
*   **Routine and Predictability:** The authors argue that simple routines and predictable patterns in daily life contribute to a sense of coherence, making the world feel less chaotic and more meaningful.
*   **Environmental Coherence:** Extending the idea of routine, the authors cite studies showing that exposure to coherent, organized stimuli (even simple visual patterns) can temporarily boost feelings of meaning. This suggests that the human mind is attuned to finding patterns, and the presence of these patterns fosters a sense of meaning.

The paper concludes by reinforcing its main thesis: if meaning in life is a crucial psychological resource for adaptation and survival, it must be the default state, not a rare achievement. The evidence suggests that it is indeed commonplace, derived from the simple, everyday experiences of mood, relationships, and coherence.
