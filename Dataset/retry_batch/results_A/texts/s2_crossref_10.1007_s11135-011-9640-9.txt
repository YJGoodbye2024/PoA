## 1. Official Abstract
"Survey questions asking about taboo topics such as sexual activities, illegal behaviour such as social fraud, or unsocial attitudes such as racism, often generate inaccurate survey estimates which are distorted by social desirability bias. Due to self-presentation concerns, survey respondents underreport socially undesirable activities and overreport socially desirable ones. This article reviews theoretical explanations of socially motivated misreporting in sensitive surveys and provides an overview of the empirical evidence on the effectiveness of specific survey methods designed to encourage the respondents to answer more honestly. Besides psychological aspects, like a stable need for social approval and the preference for not getting involved into embarrassing social interactions, aspects of the survey design, the interviewer's characteristics and the survey situation determine the occurrence and the degree of social desirability bias. The review shows that survey designers could generate more valid data by selecting appropriate data collection strategies that reduce respondents' discomfort when answering to a sensitive question."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible, allowing for a comprehensive synopsis organized by its original sections.

### 1. Introduction
The paper begins by establishing the central problem: surveys on sensitive topics like illegal acts, sexual behavior, or socially frowned-upon attitudes are often plagued by social desirability bias (SDB). This bias causes respondents to underreport socially undesirable behaviors and overreport desirable ones to present themselves in a positive light. The author states that the goal of the article is to review the theoretical explanations for this behavior and to summarize the empirical evidence on the various factors that influence SDB in surveys. The ultimate aim is to show that careful survey design can lead to more valid and honest data.

### 2. Concepts of ‘sensitivity’ and ‘response bias’
Krumpal defines a "sensitive question" as one where a truthful answer could be seen as a deviation from social norms, leading to potential disapproval from the interviewer or society. The sensitivity of a question is not inherent to the topic itself but depends on whether the respondent's true answer is the socially undesirable one. As the paper notes, "for those for whom the answer is ‘no’ questions about any particular behaviour are not sensitive."

Response bias is defined as a "systematic tendency to respond to a range of questionnaire items on some other basis than the specific item content." This bias is distinct from random measurement errors and represents a directional deviation from the respondent's true score. The paper conceptualizes a survey response as being composed of the true score, a bias component (the systematic tendency to misreport), and a random error component.

### 3. Theoretical explanations of social desirability bias
The review explores several theoretical frameworks to explain why SDB occurs:

*   **Trait-based Models:** This perspective views the tendency to give socially desirable answers as a stable personality trait. Individuals with a high "need for social approval" are more likely to distort their answers to gain affirmation, regardless of the specific survey situation.
*   **Situational Models:** In contrast, this approach sees SDB as a temporary social strategy influenced by the immediate context of the survey. Factors like the presence of an interviewer, the topic of the question, and assurances of confidentiality play a crucial role. Respondents may engage in "impression management" to cope with the discomfort of the interaction.
*   **Rational Choice Theory:** This model posits that respondents perform a cost-benefit analysis when deciding whether to answer truthfully. They weigh the perceived social and material costs of admitting to a sensitive behavior against the benefits of lying (e.g., avoiding embarrassment or legal consequences). If the perceived risk of a truthful answer is high, they are more likely to misreport.

### 4. Determinants of social desirability bias
This section forms the core of the literature review, detailing the specific factors that have been empirically shown to influence SDB.

*   **Mode of Data Collection:** A key determinant is the method used to administer the survey. The presence of an interviewer in face-to-face or telephone interviews significantly increases SDB compared to self-administered questionnaires (SAQs), such as paper-and-pencil or web surveys. Self-administration provides a greater sense of anonymity, which encourages more honest reporting on sensitive topics like illicit drug use, alcohol problems, and sexual behavior.
*   **Question Design and Context:** The way a question is worded and framed can impact responses. The author discusses techniques like "forgiving wording" (e.g., "Did you ever happen to...") or embedding sensitive items within a list of non-sensitive ones to normalize the behavior and reduce the perceived threat.
*   **Specialized Questioning Techniques:** The paper reviews several indirect questioning methods designed to protect respondent privacy and elicit more truthful answers. These include Randomized Response Techniques (RRT), where a randomizing device (like a coin flip) determines whether the respondent answers the sensitive question or an unrelated one, making it impossible for the interviewer to know the individual's true status. Other methods mentioned are the Unmatched Count Technique (UCT) and the Crosswise Model.
*   **Interviewer and Situational Characteristics:** The characteristics of the interviewer (e.g., gender, age, ethnicity) and the survey setting can influence responses, especially in face-to-face interviews. The perceived level of privacy and confidentiality is paramount; respondents who feel their answers are truly anonymous are less likely to provide socially desirable responses.

### 5. Conclusion
Krumpal concludes that social desirability bias is a complex phenomenon driven by a combination of individual personality traits and, more significantly, situational factors within the survey. The review demonstrates that while SDB is a pervasive threat to data validity in sensitive surveys, it is not an insurmountable problem. The author emphasizes that survey designers have a range of effective strategies at their disposal. By carefully selecting the mode of data collection (favoring self-administered methods), thoughtfully designing questions, and employing specialized techniques that enhance anonymity, researchers can significantly reduce the pressure respondents feel to misrepresent themselves and thereby collect more accurate data.
