## 1. Official Abstract
"Al­most uni­ver­sal­ly, re­search and prac­tice sug­gest that a brand that in­creas­es its prod­uct as­sort­ment, or va­ri­ety, should ben­e­fit through in­creased mar­ket share. In this pa­per, we show this is not al­ways the case. We in­tro­duce the con­struct 'as­sort­ment type' and demon­strate that the ef­fect of as­sort­ment size on brand share is sys­tem­at­i­cal­ly mod­er­at­ed by as­sort­ment type. We de­fine an 'align­able' as­sort­ment as a set of brand vari­ants that dif­fer along a sin­gle, com­pen­sato­ry di­men­sion such that choos­ing from that as­sort­ment only re­quires with­in-at­tribute trade-offs. In con­trast, we de­fine a 'non­align­able' as­sort­ment as a set of brand vari­ants that si­mul­ta­ne­ous­ly vary along mul­ti­ple, non­com­pen­sato­ry di­men­sions, de­mand­ing be­tween-at­tribute trade-offs. In turn, we ar­gue that an align­able as­sort­ment can ef­fi­cient­ly meet the di­verse tastes of con­sumers, there­by in­creas­ing brand share, but that a non­align­able as­sort­ment in­creas­es both the cog­ni­tive ef­fort and the po­ten­tial for re­gret faced by a con­sumer, there­by de­creas­ing brand share. We term this ef­fect 'over­choice.' Across three stud­ies, we pro­vide ev­i­dence of over­choice and tie the ef­fect to the ef­fort and re­gret brought about by non­aligna­bil­i­ty. In the process, we demon­strate that sim­pli­fi­ca­tion of in­for­ma­tion pre­sen­ta­tion, re­versibil­i­ty of choice, and a re­duc­tion in un­der­ly­ing non­aligna­bil­i­ty serve to re­duce or elim­i­nate this ef­fect."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Based on a detailed working paper version of the publication, the analysis is structured according to the paper's original sections.

### **Introduction & Literature Review**
The paper begins by challenging the conventional wisdom that increasing product variety is always beneficial for a brand's market share. While traditional economic theory and marketing practice advocate for wider assortments to cater to diverse consumer tastes, the authors point to emerging evidence of "overchoice," where too much variety can be detrimental. They reference studies showing that expanding choice sets can lead to choice deferral or decreased satisfaction. The authors propose that the conflicting findings in the literature can be reconciled by introducing a new construct: "assortment type."

### **The "Assortment Type" Construct**
The core of the paper's theoretical contribution is the distinction between two types of assortments:

*   **Alignable Assortments:** These are defined as sets of products that differ along a single, compensatory dimension, requiring only "within-attribute trade-offs." An example is a product line where items vary by size; a consumer can easily compare the options along this one dimension (e.g., choosing a larger size for a higher price). The authors argue that, consistent with prior research, expanding an alignable assortment should generally increase brand share.
*   **Nonalignable Assortments:** These are assortments where products vary across multiple, noncompensatory dimensions, forcing consumers to make "between-attribute trade-offs." An example would be choosing between different vacation packages or restaurant entrees, where selecting one option (e.g., salmon) means completely forgoing the unique features of another (e.g., lamb).

The central hypothesis is that increasing the size of a nonalignable assortment backfires. It increases the cognitive effort required to make a decision and heightens the potential for the consumer to feel regret, leading to a decrease in the brand's market share—the "overchoice" effect.

### **Studies and Results**
The paper presents three studies designed to test the hypotheses related to assortment type, overchoice, and its underlying psychological drivers (effort and regret).

*   **Study 1: Establishing the Overchoice Effect:** This study aimed to demonstrate that the effect of assortment size on brand choice is moderated by assortment type. Using a factorial design, participants were presented with choices of portable CD players. The results showed that for alignable assortments (varying only on playback time and price), increasing the number of options increased the brand's choice share. However, for nonalignable assortments (varying in features like "anti-skip protection" vs. "bass boost"), increasing the number of options from 2 to 5 significantly *decreased* the brand's choice share, confirming the overchoice hypothesis.

*   **Study 2: Investigating the Role of Effort and Regret:** This study explored the mechanisms behind overchoice. The authors manipulated the "reversibility" of the choice, arguing that if a choice is easily reversible, the potential for regret should be lower. As predicted, when choices were presented as reversible, the negative effect of a large, nonalignable assortment disappeared. This supports the idea that anticipated regret is a key driver of the overchoice effect.

*   **Study 3: The Impact of Information Presentation:** The final study tested whether simplifying the decision-making process could mitigate overchoice. The authors manipulated how information was presented for a nonalignable assortment. In one condition, information was presented in a complex, non-comparable format. In another, it was simplified into a common metric (a "quality index"). The results showed that the overchoice effect was present in the complex condition but was eliminated when the information was simplified, suggesting that cognitive effort is the other primary driver of the phenomenon.

### **Discussion and Conclusion**
The authors conclude that the effect of variety on brand share is not uniform but is instead systematically moderated by the type of assortment. While expanding an alignable assortment is generally a sound strategy, expanding a nonalignable one can backfire by overwhelming consumers, increasing their cognitive burden, and making them anticipate regret. This leads to the paradoxical outcome where less choice can be more effective. The paper demonstrates that this negative "overchoice" effect can be reduced or eliminated by making choices reversible (to reduce regret) or by simplifying the presentation of information (to reduce cognitive effort).
