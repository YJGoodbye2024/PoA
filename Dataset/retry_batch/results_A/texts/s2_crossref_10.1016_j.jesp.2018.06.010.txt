## 1. Official Abstract
>Processing-fluency, the experienced ease of ongoing mental operations, influences judgments such as frequency, monetary value, or truth. Most experiments keep to-be-judged stimuli ambiguous with regards to these judgment dimensions. In real life, however, people usually have declarative information about these stimuli beyond the experiential processing information. Here, we address how experiential fluency information may inform truth judgments in the presence of declarative advice information. Four experiments show that fluency influences judged truth even when advice about the statements' truth is continuously available and labelled as highly valid; the influence follows a linear cue integration pattern for two orthogonal cues (i.e., experiential and declarative information). These data underline the importance of processing fluency as an explanatory construct in real-life judgements and support a cue integration framework to understand fluency effects in judgment and decision making.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on analyses from citing academic sources.

The core contribution of Unkelbach and Greifeneder's 2018 paper is the demonstration that experiential fluency (the subjective ease of processing information) and declarative advice (explicit information about truthfulness) work in tandem to influence an individual's judgment of truth. The research establishes that even when people are given reliable advice about whether a statement is true or false, the feeling of fluency from repetition or familiarity still sways their judgment.

The study's key finding, derived from four experiments, is that these two types of cues—experiential and declarative—are integrated in a linear fashion. This means that the influence of fluency doesn't disappear in the face of explicit advice; rather, both sources of information contribute to the final truth judgment. This finding is significant because it extends the understanding of the "truth effect" (the tendency to believe information as more truthful after repeated exposure) to situations that more closely mirror real life, where individuals often have both their own feelings of familiarity and external information to rely on.

The paper provides robust evidence against the idea that people can simply ignore the feeling of fluency when they have more diagnostic, declarative information available. Even when advice about a statement's validity is present and marked as highly reliable, the influence of processing ease persists. This supports a cue-integration framework for understanding how people make judgments, suggesting that the mind weighs and combines various inputs rather than discarding one in favor of another. The research underscores the pervasive and fundamental nature of processing fluency as a cognitive heuristic that operates even alongside more deliberate, analytical processing.
