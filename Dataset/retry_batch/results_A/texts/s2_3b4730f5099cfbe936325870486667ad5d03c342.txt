## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on the abstract and analysis from related academic sources.

This paper investigates the role of partisans' exaggerated perceptions of their opponents' views in escalating conflict and hindering resolution. The central argument is that people in ideological disputes tend to imagine their adversaries are more extremist and biased than they actually are, and this "imagined difference" is a direct cause of negotiation failure. This phenomenon is part of a broader concept known as "naïve realism," where individuals perceive their own views as objective and assume that those who disagree must be irrational or ideologically biased.

The paper presents findings from three experiments designed to test this hypothesis in face-to-face negotiations:

*   **Experiment 1: The Detriment of Imagined Differences:** This experiment demonstrated that merely attending to supposed ideological differences, even when false, harms negotiations. When negotiating pairs (dyads) were led to believe they had political differences—despite actually holding similar views—they took longer to complete a negotiation task and viewed their partners more negatively than pairs who were not made aware of any political differences. This finding highlights that the perception of ideological opposition itself, rather than substantive disagreement, can be a primary driver of conflict.

*   **Experiments 2 & 3: The Benefit of Acquiring Accurate Information:** These experiments tested a method for reducing the negative impact of imagined differences. The researchers brought together partisans with genuinely opposing views on contentious issues: abortion (Experiment 2) and the death penalty (Experiment 3). The key intervention was having the opponents disclose and explain their actual views to each other before beginning negotiations. The results showed that this process of acquiring accurate information about the other side's beliefs led to more successful outcomes. Specifically, the opposing partisans were able to reach more comprehensive and integrative agreements and developed a more favorable perception of each other.

In essence, the research by Keltner and Robinson shows that partisans consistently overestimate the ideological gap between themselves and their opponents. This "false polarization" effect leads them to see their adversaries as more extreme than they truly are. The paper's key contribution is demonstrating experimentally that correcting these misperceptions by facilitating the exchange of accurate, personal viewpoints can serve as a powerful conflict resolution tool, allowing for the discovery of common ground and fostering more successful, integrative agreements.
