## 1. Official Abstract
Recent studies have suggested that the medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC) contributes both to understanding the mental states of others and to introspecting about one's own mind. This finding has suggested that perceivers might use their own thoughts and feelings as a starting point for making inferences about others, consistent with "simulation" or "self-projection" views of social cognition. However, perceivers cannot simply assume that others think and feel exactly as they do; social cognition also must include processes that adjust for perceived differences between self and other. Recent cognitive work has suggested that such correction occurs through a process of "anchoring-and-adjustment" by which perceivers serially tune their inferences from an initial starting point based on their own introspections. Here, we used functional MRI to test two predictions derived from this anchoring-and-adjustment view. Participants (n = 64) used a Likert scale to judge the preferences of another person and to indicate their own preferences on the same items, allowing us to calculate the discrepancy between the participant's answers for self and other. Whole-brain parametric analyses identified a region in the MPFC in which activity was related linearly to this self-other discrepancy when inferring the mental states of others. These findings suggest both that the self serves as an important starting point from which to understand others and that perceivers customize such inferences by serially adjusting away from this anchor.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis

### Introduction
The paper addresses the neural mechanisms underlying "mentalizing," the process of inferring another person's thoughts and feelings. The authors highlight that the medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC) is consistently activated during both mentalizing about others and introspecting about oneself. This has led to "simulation" or "self-projection" theories, which propose that we use our own minds as a model to understand others.

However, simply projecting our own thoughts onto others is insufficient. The authors introduce the "anchoring-and-adjustment" heuristic as a more refined model. This cognitive model suggests that when judging others, people first "anchor" on their own perspective and then "adjust" this inference to account for perceived differences between themselves and the other person. The study was designed to find neural evidence for this two-stage process. The researchers hypothesized that if people use themselves as an anchor, neural activity during mentalizing should correlate with the degree of difference (discrepancy) between the self and the other. Furthermore, they predicted that the "adjustment" process would manifest as a linear change in neural response, corresponding to the size of this self-other discrepancy. The MPFC was the primary brain region expected to show these effects.

### Methods
The study utilized functional MRI (fMRI) with a sample of 64 participants. The core task required participants to perform two types of judgments using a Likert scale: rating their own preferences on various items and, separately, inferring the preferences of another person on the same items. This design allowed the researchers to calculate a "self-other discrepancy" score for each item, representing the difference between the participant's own rating and their rating for the other person.

In the fMRI analysis, this discrepancy score was used as a parametric modulator. This technique aimed to identify brain regions where activity during the "other" condition (mentalizing about the other person) was systematically predicted by the magnitude of the difference between the self and other ratings.

### Results
The whole-brain analysis yielded a key finding: a region within the medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC) demonstrated a linear relationship between its activity and the self-other discrepancy score. Specifically, as the difference between a participant's preference and their inference of the other's preference increased, so did the activity in this MPFC region.

This result supports both of the study's primary predictions. First, the fact that neural activity correlated with self-other discrepancy at all suggests that the brain spontaneously references the self when thinking about others. If self-knowledge were not used as a starting point, there would be no reason for brain activity to track the difference from it. Second, the linear nature of this relationship is consistent with the "serial adjustment" component of the model, where greater differences require more significant cognitive adjustment away from the initial anchor point.

### Discussion
The authors conclude that their findings provide strong evidence for an anchoring-and-adjustment model of mentalizing. The results demonstrate that the self serves as a fundamental starting point, or anchor, for understanding the minds of others. The linear increase in MPFC activity with greater self-other discrepancy suggests a process of customizing these inferences by serially adjusting away from the self-anchor.

This study augments the growing body of work supporting simulation or self-projection theories of social cognition. The authors note that alternative models, such as those based on abstract, theory-like reasoning, would struggle to explain why neural activity is so tightly coupled to the difference between one's own perspective and an inference about someone else's. The research solidifies the role of the MPFC as a key neural substrate for this process of using the self to navigate the social world.
