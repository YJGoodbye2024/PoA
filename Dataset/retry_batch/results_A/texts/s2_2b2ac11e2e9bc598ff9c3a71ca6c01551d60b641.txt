## 1. Official Abstract
"Psychological ownership, or the feeling that something is mine, has garnered growing attention in marketing. While previous work focuses on the positive aspects of psychological ownership, this research draws attention to the darker side of psychological ownership—territorial behavior. Results of five experimental studies demonstrate that when consumers feel psychological ownership of a target, they are prone to perceptions ofINFRINGEMENT and subsequent territorial responses when they infer that another individual feels ownership of the same target. Potential infringers are held less accountable when they acknowledge ownership prior to engaging in otherwise threatening behaviors, and when they could not be expected to know that a target is owned, as it was not clearly marked. In addition, high narcissists are subject to a psychological ownership metaperception bias, and are thus more apt than low narcissists to perceiveINFRINGEMENT. A multitude of territorial responses are documented for both tangible (coffee, sweater, chair, pizza) and intangible (a design) targets of ownership. Further, consumers infer the psychological ownership of others from signals of the antecedents of psychological ownership: control, investment of self, and intimate knowledge. Theoretical implications for territoriality and psychological ownership are discussed, along with managerial implications and areas for future research."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. The following synopsis is structured according to the paper's original sections.

### **Theoretical Background**
The paper introduces psychological ownership (PO) as the feeling that "it is MINE," a concept gaining traction in marketing for its positive effects. However, the authors pivot to explore its "darker side": territoriality. Territoriality is defined as a behavioral expression of PO aimed at managing and defending a "territory," which can be a place, an object, or even an idea. The core argument is that when a consumer with high PO for an object perceives that someone else also feels ownership over it, this creates a sense of infringement. This perceived infringement triggers territorial responses to defend one's psychological "property lines." The authors propose that this response is a defense mechanism against the "lessening of self" that occurs when a possession, viewed as part of the self, is threatened. The framework suggests that consumers infer others' PO from the same signals that generate their own: control, intimate knowledge, and investment of self.

### **Study 1: Infringement and Territorial Responses**
This study aimed to establish the primary link between a consumer's PO and their territorial reaction to another's perceived ownership.
*   **Method:** Participants were invited to a mock restaurant and encouraged to customize a cup of coffee to induce high PO. A server then signaled their own PO by either slightly moving the cup (a control-oriented signal) or not. Territorial responses were measured through observers' notes on participants' negative expressions (e.g., frowning) and actions.
*   **Results:** When participants felt high PO over the coffee, a server's simple act of moving it triggered significantly more negative expressions and territorial behaviors compared to the no-signal condition. This study provided initial evidence that a perceived ownership claim by another person on one's territory elicits territorial responses.

### **Study 2: The Role of Marking the Territory**
This experiment investigated whether "marking" a territory mitigates infringement perceptions and territorial responses.
*   **Method:** The study used a scenario where a participant imagined having high PO over a favorite chair in a coffee shop. The chair was either marked (e.g., with a jacket) or unmarked. Another customer then occupies the chair.
*   **Results:** Participants felt their territory was more clearly communicated when it was marked. When another person occupied a *marked* chair, participants perceived a stronger infringement and reported a higher likelihood of engaging in territorial behaviors (e.g., asking the person to move) than when the chair was unmarked. This suggests that when ownership is clearly signaled, violations are perceived as more egregious.

### **Study 3: The Infringer's Acknowledgment**
This study explored if an infringer's acknowledgment of the owner's PO could reduce negative territorial responses.
*   **Method:** Using a scenario involving a shared pizza, one person (the infringer) takes the last slice, which the participant psychologically owned. The infringer either acknowledged the participant's claim ("I know you were working on this last slice...") or did not.
*   **Results:** When the infringer acknowledged the participant's psychological ownership before taking the slice, the participant's perception of infringement was significantly lower. This led to a reduced tendency to retaliate. This shows that respecting the "property lines in the mind" can de-escalate conflict.

### **Study 4: The Role of Narcissism**
This study examined how the personality trait of narcissism affects the perception of infringement.
*   **Method:** Participants' narcissism levels were measured. They were then put into a scenario involving ownership of a sweater design. They were told a fellow student also claimed the design.
*   **Results:** Individuals with higher levels of narcissism were more likely to perceive that others were claiming ownership of their territory (a "psychological ownership metaperception bias"). Consequently, high narcissists were more prone to perceiving infringement and reacting with territorial responses, even when the other person's claim was ambiguous.

### **Study 5: Intangible Territories**
This final study extended the research from tangible objects to intangible ones—specifically, an idea for a restaurant logo.
*   **Method:** Participants developed a logo design, thereby investing their "self" and creating PO. They were then led to believe another person was presenting their design as their own.
*   **Results:** The study confirmed that territorial responses are not limited to physical objects. Participants who felt high PO over their design reported significant feelings of infringement and a desire to engage in territorial behaviors (like confronting the other person) when they saw their intangible idea being claimed by someone else.

### **General Discussion**
The authors conclude by summarizing their contributions. They establish that consumers' PO can lead to negative territorial behaviors when they perceive a competing claim on their territory, whether tangible or intangible. The paper provides a framework for how these infringements are perceived and how they can be managed.

*   **Theoretical Implications:** The research formally links the theories of psychological ownership and human territoriality within a consumer context. It demonstrates that territoriality extends beyond physical space to objects and ideas and is triggered by perceived threats to one's extended self.
*   **Managerial Implications:** The findings are highly relevant for businesses, especially in the sharing economy or in service environments like coffee shops. For example, allowing customers to "mark" their space (like a favorite table) can create PO, but companies must be prepared for potential conflicts. Staff training on how to respectfully acknowledge a customer's psychological territory (as shown in Study 3) could be a valuable tool for de-escalation and improving customer experience.
