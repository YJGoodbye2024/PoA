## 1. Official Abstract
"A recent two-factor model of socially desirable responding based on denial and attribution components was reviewed and disputed. A second model distinguishing self-deception and impression management components was reviewed and shown to be related to early factor-analytic work on desirability scales. Two studies were conducted to test the model. A factor analysis of commonly used desirability scales revealed that the two major factors were best interpreted as Self-Deception and Impression Management. A second study employed confirmatory factor analysis to show that the attribution/denial model does not fit the data as well as the self-deception/impression management model. A third study compared scores on desirability scales under anonymous and public conditions. Results showed that those scales that had loaded highest on the Impression Management factor showed the greatest mean increase from anonymous to public conditions. It is recommended that impression management, but not self-deception, be controlled in self-reports of personality."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### Introduction
The paper begins by noting the long history and variety of scales designed to measure socially desirable responding (SDR), the tendency of individuals to present themselves in a favorable light. Paulhus reviews evidence suggesting that the various SDR measures can be understood within a two-factor model. He critiques one such model, which distinguishes between "attribution" (claiming desirable but uncommon traits) and "denial" (disclaiming undesirable but common traits), arguing the evidence for it is weak.

Instead, Paulhus proposes a more robust two-component model distinguishing between **Self-Deception** (an unconscious, honest belief in one's favorable qualities) and **Impression Management** (a conscious, deliberate attempt to mislead others to create a favorable impression). This model builds on the work of Sackeim and Gur (1978), who developed the Self-Deception Questionnaire (SDQ) and the Other-Deception Questionnaire (ODQ). The paper's central aim is to empirically test and validate this Self-Deception versus Impression Management model.

### Study 1: Exploratory Factor Analysis
The first study sought to identify the underlying structure of various social desirability scales through exploratory factor analysis. A set of nine widely used desirability scales, including the Marlowe-Crowne scale, Edwards Social Desirability Scale, and the Self- and Other-Deception Questionnaires (SDQ and ODQ), were administered to 202 university students.

**Results:** The factor analysis revealed two primary, uncorrelated factors.
*   **Factor 1 (Self-Deception):** This factor was most strongly marked by the Self-Deception Questionnaire (SDQ). Scales loading on this factor involve items that are universally true but psychologically threatening to admit. High scorers honestly see themselves in a positive light, even if it involves a degree of self-delusion.
*   **Factor 2 (Impression Management):** This factor was most clearly defined by the Other-Deception Questionnaire (ODQ). Scales loading here involve admitting to socially desirable but statistically rare behaviors. High scores indicate a deliberate effort to present a positive public image.

The analysis showed that some traditional scales, like the Marlowe-Crowne, loaded on both factors, suggesting they measure a mix of both self-deception and impression management.

### Study 2: Confirmatory Factor Analysis
This study directly compared the fit of the attribution/denial model against the self-deception/impression management model using confirmatory factor analysis on the same dataset from Study 1. The goal was to determine which model better explained the relationships among the SDR scales.

**Results:** The self-deception/impression management model provided a significantly better fit to the data than the attribution/denial model. This finding provided strong support for Paulhus's proposed framework, indicating that the distinction between unconscious self-deception and conscious impression management is a more accurate way to conceptualize the two main facets of socially desirable responding.

### Study 3: Experimental Manipulation
The third study was designed to provide experimental validation for the two-component model. The core hypothesis was that scores on impression management scales, but not self-deception scales, would increase when responses were made public. A new group of 114 students completed a subset of the SDR scales under two conditions: an initial anonymous, private condition and a subsequent public condition where they were told their answers would be discussed with a psychologist and peers.

**Results:** As predicted, the scales that loaded highest on the Impression Management factor in Study 1 (like the ODQ) showed a significant increase in scores from the anonymous to the public condition. Conversely, scores on the scales that were pure markers of the Self-Deception factor (like the SDQ) remained stable across both conditions. This result powerfully demonstrates the distinction between the two components: impression management is a conscious strategy that is sensitive to audience effects, whereas self-deception is a more stable, intrapsychic tendency.

### General Discussion
In the final section, Paulhus summarizes the converging evidence from the three studies. The factor analyses (Studies 1 and 2) and the experimental manipulation (Study 3) all support a two-component model of socially desirable responding composed of self-deception and impression management.

He concludes with a significant practical recommendation for psychological assessment: researchers should be primarily concerned with controlling for *impression management*. Because it represents a deliberate response style, it directly contaminates the validity of self-reports. Self-deception, on the other hand, is viewed as a substantive personality trait itself—an honest, albeit biased, self-view that is part of an individual's psychological makeup. Therefore, statistically removing the influence of self-deception from personality measures may be inappropriate, as it would be removing a genuine aspect of personality. This paper established a foundational distinction that has heavily influenced subsequent research on response biases.
