## 1. Official Abstract
"Response bias continues to be the most frequently cited criticism of personality testing for personnel selection. The authors meta-analyzed the social desirability literature, examining whether social desirability functions as a predictor for a variety of criteria, as a suppressor, or as a mediator. Social desirability scales were found not to predict school success, task performance, counterproductive behaviors, and job performance. Correlations with the Big Five personality dimensions, cognitive ability, and years of education are presented along with empirical evidence that (a) social desirability is not as pervasive a problem as has been anticipated by industrial-organizational psychologists, (b) social desirability is in fact related to real individual differences in emotional stability and conscientiousness, and (c) social desirability does not function as a predictor, as a practically useful suppressor, or as a mediator variable for the criterion of job performance. Removing the effects of social desirability from the Big Five dimensions of personality leaves the criterion-related validity of personality constructs for predicting job performance intact."

### Detailed Interpretation

The 1996 paper, "Role of social desirability in personality testing for personnel selection: The red herring," by Ones, Viswesvaran, and Reiss, stands as a landmark meta-analysis in industrial-organizational psychology. Its primary goal was to empirically investigate the long-held belief that social desirability (the tendency of individuals to present themselves favorably) significantly undermines the validity of personality tests in hiring contexts. The authors challenged the assumption that this response bias was a major concern that needed to be corrected.

The core of the paper is a large-scale meta-analysis of the existing literature. The authors examined three potential roles of social desirability in relation to job performance and other criteria:
1.  As a **predictor** of performance.
2.  As a **suppressor** variable that, if removed, would increase the predictive power of personality traits.
3.  As a **mediator** that explains the relationship between personality and performance.

The central and most impactful finding of the study was that social desirability is largely a "red herring"—an issue that is distracting and ultimately insignificant. The meta-analysis revealed that social desirability scores did not predict key outcomes like job performance, task performance, academic success, or counterproductive work behaviors.

Furthermore, the research provided two critical insights into the nature of social desirability itself. First, it demonstrated that scores on social desirability scales are not merely "noise" or faking. Instead, they are systematically related to substantive personality traits, particularly the Big Five dimensions of Emotional Stability and Conscientiousness. This suggests that individuals who score high on social desirability are often genuinely more emotionally stable and conscientious.

Second, and perhaps most practically relevant, the study found that statistically removing or "correcting for" the effects of social desirability from personality measures did not improve their ability to predict job performance. The criterion-related validity of the Big Five personality traits remained intact even when social desirability was factored out. This finding directly contradicted the common practice and recommendation of using social desirability scales to adjust or discard the results of job applicants' personality tests.

In conclusion, the paper argues that the extensive concern among psychologists about response distortion in personality testing for personnel selection was misplaced. Ones, Viswesvaran, and Reiss concluded that social desirability is not a pervasive problem that invalidates personality assessments in hiring, but rather is linked to real, positive personality characteristics and that attempts to control for it are unnecessary and ineffective.
