## 1. Official Abstract
"Feelings of familiarity are not direct products of memory. Although prior experience of a stimulus can produce a feeling of familiarity, that feeling can also be aroused in the absence of prior experience if perceptual processing of the stimulus is fluent (e.g., Whittlesea, Jacoby, & Girard, 1990). This suggests that feelings of familiarity arise through an unconscious inference about the source of processing fluency. The present experiments extend that conclusion. They show that the feeling of familiarity can be produced by manipulating the fluency of conceptual processing. When a target word (e.g., BOAT) was presented in a predictive context (The stormy seas tossed the...), it was processed more fluently and was more likely to be called 'old' than when it appeared in a nonpredictive context (He saved his money and bought a...), whether or not it had been actually seen before. This illusion of familiarity demonstrates that feelings of familiarity are attributions about the quality of processing, made with reference to an expectation."

### Detailed Interpretation

Bruce W. A. Whittlesea's 1993 paper, "Illusions of familiarity," is a highly influential work that challenges the traditional view of familiarity as a direct readout from memory. Instead, it posits that feelings of familiarity are the result of an unconscious attribution process. The core argument is that when we process a stimulus with unexpected ease (fluency), we infer that this ease must be due to a prior encounter, thus generating a feeling of familiarity, even for novel stimuli.

**Core Concepts: Processing Fluency and Attribution**

The paper builds on the idea of "processing fluency," which is the ease with which our minds process information. Whittlesea distinguishes between two types:
*   **Perceptual Fluency:** The ease of processing the physical features of a stimulus (e.g., a clearly written word vs. a blurry one).
*   **Conceptual Fluency:** The ease of processing the meaning or semantic content of a stimulus.

The central thesis is that people use a "fluency heuristic." When processing feels unexpectedly fluent, individuals perform an unconscious attribution, concluding that the stimulus must be familiar from a past experience. This attribution process is what creates the subjective feeling of familiarity.

**Experimental Design and Key Findings**

To test this hypothesis, Whittlesea designed a series of experiments that manipulated conceptual fluency. Participants were presented with target words in sentences. The key manipulation was the context provided by the sentence stem:
*   **Predictive Context:** The sentence strongly suggested the target word. For example, "The stormy seas tossed the BOAT."
*   **Nonpredictive Context:** The sentence was general and did not predict the target word. For example, "She saved her money and bought a LAMP."

The experiments included both "old" words (previously seen by participants) and "new" words (never seen before). The critical finding was that participants were significantly more likely to misidentify a *new* word as "old" when it appeared in a predictive context. The predictive sentence stem "primed" the concept of the target word, making it easier to process (i.e., increasing its conceptual fluency). This enhanced fluency led participants to incorrectly attribute the ease of processing to a prior encounter, thereby creating an "illusion of familiarity."

Measurements showed that target words in predictive contexts were named about 130 ms faster than those in nonpredictive contexts, confirming the increase in processing fluency. Crucially, this fluency boost led to an 18% increase in the false recognition of novel targets.

**Conclusion and Impact**

Whittlesea concluded that feelings of familiarity are not a direct product of memory traces. Instead, they are judgments or attributions about the quality of our cognitive processing. When processing is surprisingly easy, we explain this fluency by inferring a past experience, leading to a feeling of familiarity. This can happen correctly for genuinely old items (which are often processed more fluently) but can also lead to powerful illusions for new items when their fluency is artificially enhanced by context. This work was foundational in establishing the "discrepancy-attribution hypothesis," which suggests that the feeling of familiarity arises when there is a surprising discrepancy between expected and experienced processing ease.
