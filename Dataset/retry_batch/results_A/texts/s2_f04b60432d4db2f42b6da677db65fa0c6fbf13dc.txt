## 1. Official Abstract
"The authors examine self-control problems--modeled as time-inconsistent, present-biased preferences--in a model where a person must do an activity exactly once. They emphasize two distinctions: do activities involve immediate costs or immediate rewards, and are people sophisticated or naive about future self-control problems? Naive people procrastinate immediate-cost activities and preproperate--do too soon--immediate-reward activities. Sophistication mitigates procrastination but exacerbates preproperation. Moreover, with immediate costs, a small present bias can severely harm only naive people, whereas with immediate rewards it can severely harm only sophisticated people. Lessons for savings, addiction, and elsewhere are discussed."

### Introduction
The paper challenges the standard economic model of exponential discounting, where preferences are time-consistent. The authors argue that a wide range of evidence suggests people's preferences are "present-biased," meaning they have a tendency to pursue immediate gratification and avoid immediate costs. This paper explores the implications of these time-inconsistent preferences in a simple model where an individual must complete a task exactly once over a finite period. The analysis focuses on two key distinctions: whether the task involves immediate costs (e.g., doing a tedious chore) or immediate rewards (e.g., watching a good movie from a list), and whether individuals are "sophisticated" (aware of their future self-control problems) or "naive" (unaware of them).

### I. A Model of Time-Inconsistent Preferences
The authors formalize present-biased preferences using the `(β, δ)` model. In this framework, an individual's intertemporal utility from a future period's perspective is the standard sum of discounted utilities. However, from the present moment's perspective, all future utilities are additionally discounted by a factor `β ≤ 1`. When `β < 1`, the person exhibits a present bias, giving extra weight to the current moment.

The paper then defines two types of individuals based on their awareness of this bias:
*   **Sophisticates**: These individuals are fully aware that they will have a present bias in all future periods. They can therefore perfectly predict their future behavior.
*   **Naifs**: These individuals are unaware of their future self-control problems. They (incorrectly) believe that their future selves will behave in a time-consistent manner.

The model considers a scenario where a person must perform an activity once within `T` periods. Each period `t` is associated with a cost `ct` and a reward `vt`.

### II. Immediate Costs
This section analyzes tasks where the costs are immediate and the rewards are delayed (e.g., completing a report, doing taxes). The key findings are:
*   **Procrastination**: Naive individuals with present-biased preferences tend to procrastinate. They repeatedly plan to do the task in the next period, not realizing that when the next period arrives, their preference will again shift to delay. This can lead to severe welfare losses, as they may delay far beyond the optimal time, or even until the final, least desirable moment.
*   **The Role of Sophistication**: Sophistication mitigates procrastination. A sophisticated individual understands that if they delay, their future self is also likely to delay. This pessimism about their future behavior makes them more inclined to complete the task "now" to avoid even worse outcomes later. In some cases, a sophisticated person might even perform the task earlier than someone with time-consistent preferences would.
*   **Welfare Implications**: For tasks with immediate costs, even a small present bias (`β` slightly less than 1) can cause significant harm to a naive person. In contrast, a sophisticated person with a small present bias will behave almost optimally.

### III. Immediate Rewards
This section flips the scenario to tasks with immediate rewards and delayed costs (e.g., consuming an unhealthy treat, going to a movie). The authors term the tendency to do these activities too soon as "preproperation."
*   **Preproperation**: Naive individuals tend to preproperate. They seize a reward that is good enough for "today" without fully appreciating that waiting might yield a much better reward in the future. They wrongly believe they will have the patience to wait tomorrow, so they indulge today.
*   **The Role of Sophistication**: In this context, sophistication exacerbates preproperation. A sophisticated person knows they will likely give in to temptation in the future. If they believe their future self will seize a suboptimal reward, they may reason it's better to just take the reward available now. This can create a "race to the bottom" where the individual acts too early because they don't trust their future selves to wait for the best option.
*   **Welfare Implications**: For tasks with immediate rewards, a small present bias can cause significant harm to a sophisticated person, as their awareness of future failings drives them to preproperate. A naive person with a small bias, however, suffers only minor welfare loss.

### IV. Discussion and Conclusion
The authors conclude that the distinction between naivete and sophistication is crucial for understanding the behavioral and welfare consequences of self-control problems. The behavior of naive individuals directly reflects their present bias, leading them to procrastinate on costly tasks and preproperate on rewarding ones. Sophisticated individuals, however, behave in more complex ways as they strategically react to their predicted future behavior. This can lead to counterintuitive outcomes, such as a sophisticated person with a self-control problem saving *more* than a time-consistent individual, simply to prevent their future self from over-spending. The paper suggests these insights are applicable to a wide range of economic behaviors, including savings, addiction, and the design of incentive schemes like deadlines.
