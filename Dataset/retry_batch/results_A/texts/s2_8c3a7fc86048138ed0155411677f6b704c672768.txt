## 1. Official Abstract
> Although Janis's concept of groupthink is influential, experimental investigations have provided only weak support for the theory. Experiment 1 produced the poor decision quality associated with groupthink by manipulating group cohesion (using group labels) and threat to group members' self-esteem. Self-reports of some groupthink and defective decision-making symptoms were independently, but not interactively, affected by cohesion and threat. Experiment 2 confirmed the success of the cohesion manipulation.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on an analysis of reliable third-party and citation sources.

The 1992 paper, "Threat, Cohesion, and Group Effectiveness," presents a significant experimental test of Irving Janis's groupthink theory, proposing a "Social Identity Maintenance" model as an alternative explanation for the phenomenon. Rather than viewing groupthink as a simple result of high cohesion, the authors reframe it as a collective effort by group members to maintain a positive view of their group, especially when that shared social identity is under threat.

The core of the research lies in two experiments designed to test this perspective:

*   **Methodology and Setup**: The researchers manipulated two key variables. First, **cohesion** was manipulated not as interpersonal attraction, but from a social identity perspective, by simply giving groups distinct labels to foster a sense of "us." Second, **threat** was introduced by telling certain groups that their performance would be evaluated and that tapes of poorly performing groups would be used as examples of dysfunctional decision-making in campus and corporate training sessions. The groups were tasked with a complex problem-solving exercise (the Parasol Subassembly Task).

*   **Key Findings**: The study successfully produced the poor decision-making outcomes characteristic of groupthink. The combination of a threatened group identity and high cohesion led to lower quality decisions. However, the symptoms of groupthink, such as self-censorship and rationalization, were affected independently by threat and cohesion, not by their interaction. A notable finding, highlighted by other analyses, is that groups operating under these groupthink-inducing conditions could produce higher-quality decisions if they were given an excuse for potential poor performance.

*   **Contribution and Interpretation**: This study is significant for providing experimental evidence for groupthink-like outcomes where previous studies had been weak. The authors interpreted their findings as support for their Social Identity Maintenance model. This model posits that when a group's identity is threatened, its members become more concerned with protecting the group's image than with making a high-quality decision. This leads to the classic symptoms of groupthink, such as rationalizing away negative feedback and pressuring dissenters. The paper effectively argues that the drive to maintain a positive collective identity is a primary motivator for the behaviors associated with groupthink.
