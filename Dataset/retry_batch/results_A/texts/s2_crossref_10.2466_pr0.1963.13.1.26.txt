## 1. Official Abstract
"A methodological problem in research on cognitive complexity (CC) has been the comparability of using a judge's own construct dimensions in assessing CC in contrast to using constructs provided for the judge. This study compared CC scores obtained with Ss' own constructs with scores obtained with a standard set of constructs provided for S. While the use of own constructs on the Repertory Test is useful in clinical work, these results indicate that for research purposes provided constructs are comparable to own constructs in measuring cognitive complexity."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on reliable third-party academic sources.

A central methodological question in the study of cognitive complexity has been whether the constructs used to measure it should be elicited directly from the individual (own constructs) or can be supplied by the researcher (provided constructs). The 1963 paper by Tony Tripodi and James Bieri addresses this issue directly. Their research is situated within the framework of George Kelly's Personal Construct Theory, which posits that individuals perceive their world through a unique system of bipolar constructs.

The study's primary objective was to compare cognitive complexity scores derived from these two different types of constructs. In the experiment, the researchers utilized a modified version of the Role Construct Repertory Test (often called the "Bieri grid"). They compared the cognitive complexity scores that were generated using five constructs elicited from the subjects themselves against scores from five constructs that were provided to them.

The key finding of the study was that there was no significant difference in the cognitive complexity scores between the two conditions. Furthermore, they found a rank-order correlation of .50 between the scores from the "own" and "provided" construct lists.

Based on these results, Tripodi and Bieri concluded that for research applications, using a standardized set of provided constructs is a comparable and valid method for measuring cognitive complexity when compared to using an individual's personally elicited constructs. This finding has practical implications for the design of studies on cognitive complexity, suggesting that the more convenient method of using a standard set of constructs does not compromise the measurement's validity for research purposes. While the use of a person's own constructs remains valuable in clinical settings for understanding an individual's unique worldview, the study supports the utility of provided constructs in broader research contexts.
