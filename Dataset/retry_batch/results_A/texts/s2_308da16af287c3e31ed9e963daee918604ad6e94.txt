## 1. Official Abstract
We propose that visceral states can influence beliefs through “visceral fit”: People will judge states of the world associated with their current visceral experience as more likely. We found that warmth influenced belief in global warming (Studies 1–3) and that thirst impacted forecasts of drought and desertification (Study 5). These effects emerged in a naturalistic setting (Study 1) and in experimental lab settings (Studies 2, 3, and 5). Studies 2–6 distinguished between 3 mechanistic accounts: temperature as information (Studies 2 and 3), conceptual accessibility (Studies 4 and 5), and fluency of simulation (Studies 6a and 6b). Studies 6a and 6b used a causal chain design and found support for a “simulational fluency” account. Participants experiencing the visceral state of warmth constructed more fluent mental representations of hot (vs. cold) outdoor images, and those who were led to construe the same hot outdoor images more fluently believed more in global warming. Together, the results suggest that visceral states can influence one's beliefs by making matching states of the world easier to simulate and therefore seem more likely.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### **Introduction**
The authors introduce the concept of "visceral fit," proposing that when a person is experiencing a strong bodily or "visceral" state (like warmth, thirst, or hunger), they are more likely to believe in future world states that are congruent with that feeling. The paper distinguishes this idea from previous research by focusing on how the direct physical experience itself—not just memories of recent weather or cognitive information—can shape beliefs about broad, complex issues. The authors predict, for example, that "warm participants will believe that global warming is more likely to become a reality and that thirsty participants will believe that drought is more likely to occur." They outline a plan to test this hypothesis and explore three potential psychological mechanisms: that the visceral state is used as a piece of information, that it increases the accessibility of related concepts, or that it makes it easier to mentally simulate a matching world (simulational fluency).

### **Study 1: A Naturalistic Study**
*   **Method:** Researchers surveyed 193 people on a university campus on two different days—one warm (78°F) and one cool (63°F). Participants were asked to rate their belief in global warming.
*   **Results:** Individuals surveyed on the warmer day reported a stronger belief in global warming than those surveyed on the cooler day. This provided initial, real-world evidence for the visceral fit hypothesis.

### **Study 2: Experimental Manipulation of Warmth**
*   **Method:** To establish causality, 89 participants were brought into a lab and randomly assigned to one of two rooms: a warm room (81°F) or a neutral room (74°F). They then completed a survey on their global warming beliefs.
*   **Results:** Participants in the warm room expressed significantly stronger belief in global warming than those in the neutral room. This supported the idea that the visceral experience of warmth directly influences belief. The study also ruled out the "temperature as information" explanation, as participants did not mistakenly believe the lab's temperature was indicative of the world's temperature.

### **Study 3: Distinguishing Visceral Fit From Temperature as Information**
*   **Method:** This study aimed to further separate the effect of feeling warm from using temperature as a direct piece of evidence. Participants were told the study was about product evaluation and were given either a therapeutic heat pack or a cold pack to hold while rating their belief in global warming.
*   **Results:** Those holding the heat pack reported stronger belief in global warming. Since the heat source was clearly artificial, it demonstrated that the feeling of warmth itself, not a logical inference about the environment's temperature, was driving the effect.

### **Study 4: Testing Conceptual Priming**
*   **Method:** This study investigated whether the effect was simply due to heat-related concepts becoming more accessible. Participants were subliminally primed with either "hot" and "warm" or "cold" and "cool" before rating their belief in global warming.
*   **Results:** The conceptual priming did not affect global warming beliefs. This suggests that the influence of visceral states is not merely due to making related ideas more available in the mind, but involves a deeper process.

### **Study 5: Generalizing to a Different Visceral State (Thirst)**
*   **Method:** To see if the effect applied beyond warmth, researchers manipulated thirst. 151 participants were randomly assigned to eat a salty snack (pretzels) or nothing. They then rated their belief in the likelihood of future droughts and desertification.
*   **Results:** Participants who ate the pretzels and became thirsty subsequently rated droughts and desertification as more likely to occur. This demonstrated that the visceral fit phenomenon is not limited to temperature and global warming.

### **Studies 6a and 6b: Testing the Simulational Fluency Account**
*   **Method:** These two studies tested the final proposed mechanism: that visceral states make it easier to mentally simulate a congruent world, and this ease of simulation (fluency) is mistaken for likelihood.
    *   **Study 6a:** Participants were put in either a warm or neutral room and then asked to rate how easily they could form a mental image of a hot outdoor scene.
    *   **Study 6b:** A new set of participants was asked to imagine a hot outdoor scene. They were randomly told that the image should be either easy or difficult to generate, and then they rated their belief in global warming.
*   **Results:** Study 6a found that participants in a warm room could more easily simulate a hot outdoor scene. Study 6b showed that participants who were told to construe the image fluently reported a stronger belief in global warming. Together, these studies provide evidence for a causal chain: "visceral states can influence one's beliefs by making matching states of the world easier to simulate and therefore seem more likely."

### **General Discussion**
The authors conclude that their findings robustly support the "visceral fit" hypothesis. Across multiple studies, experiencing a visceral state (warmth or thirst) increased belief in a congruent environmental problem (global warming or drought). The mechanism responsible appears to be "simulational fluency," where the bodily sensation makes it easier to imagine a world that matches that feeling, which in turn makes that world seem more probable.
