## 1. Official Abstract
>The a priori categorization of a social environment into distinct groups is a sufficient condition for differential intergroup behaviour. This has been shown in previous experiments in which subjects discriminated against members of an outgroup in the distribution of rewards, even though this discrimination was not determined by the subjects' individual interests. The present study was designed to separate the effects of categorization from the effects of some 'real' similarity between the subjects who were assigned to the same group. For this purpose, two separate conditions were created. In the 'similarity' condition, subjects were assigned to groups on the basis of their expressed preferences for one of two painters; but they were then asked to make their decisions about the distribution of rewards as separate individuals and not as members of a group. In the 'categorization' condition, subjects were assigned to their groups on an explicitly random basis, and then made their decisions as members of one of the two groups. The results showed that social categorization per se was a variable of major importance for intergroup behaviour. In the 'similarity' condition, there was very little discrimination, whereas in the 'categorization' condition there was very marked discrimination in favour of the ingroup. The results also showed that 'fairness' was an important determinant of the subjects' decisions. These findings are discussed in the light of their relevance to a cognitive theory of social identity and intergroup behaviour.

### Detailed Interpretation

The 1973 paper "Social categorization and similarity in intergroup behaviour" by Michael Billig and Henri Tajfel is a crucial study in the development of Social Identity Theory. It was designed to experimentally isolate the variable of social categorization as a cause for intergroup discrimination, separating it from the variable of interpersonal similarity. The study is one in a series that builds upon the "minimal group paradigm" established by Tajfel and his colleagues in 1971, which found that merely categorizing people into groups is enough to produce ingroup favoritism.

**Core Problem:**
Previous minimal group studies had categorized participants based on trivial but "real" criteria, such as a preference for the painters Klee or Kandinsky. This left an important question unanswered: was the observed ingroup favoritism caused by the act of categorization itself, or was it because participants assumed they were similar to their ingroup members (e.g., "we both like Klee") and were thus more attracted to them? Billig and Tajfel's study aimed to disentangle these two potential causes.

**Methodology:**
The experiment was conducted with British schoolboys and used two main conditions to test the hypotheses:
1.  **Similarity Condition:** Participants were divided based on their stated preference for painters, establishing a basis of "real" similarity. However, they were explicitly told to make decisions about reward distribution as individuals, not as group members. This condition tested the effect of similarity without explicit group categorization.
2.  **Categorization Condition:** Participants were explicitly and randomly assigned to groups (e.g., by a coin toss), removing any basis of real similarity. They were then instructed to make decisions as members of their assigned group. This condition tested the effect of pure social categorization without underlying similarity.

In both conditions, participants distributed points to anonymous others using specially designed matrices that measured strategies like fairness, maximum ingroup profit, and maximum difference (favoring the ingroup at the expense of the outgroup).

**Key Findings:**
The results were clear and highly influential:
*   The mere act of **social categorization was the primary driver of discriminatory behavior**. In the condition where grouping was random but the "group" label was made salient, subjects displayed significant ingroup favoritism and discrimination against the outgroup.
*   Conversely, in the condition where participants were grouped based on actual similarity but the "group" identity was not made explicit, there was very little discrimination.
*   This demonstrated that social categorization *per se*, even when arbitrary, was a more powerful determinant of intergroup behavior than the perceived similarity of attitudes among individuals.
*   The study also noted that "fairness" remained a significant factor in participants' decisions, indicating that the discrimination was not absolute.

**Contribution and Significance:**
This study provided critical evidence for the cognitive, rather than interpersonal, basis of intergroup discrimination. It powerfully supported a core tenet of what would become Social Identity Theory: that a part of an individual's self-concept is derived from their group memberships. The act of being categorized into a group (an "ingroup") in relation to another (an "outgroup") is sufficient to trigger a process of social comparison, leading individuals to behave in ways that create a positive distinction for their group. By showing that random categorization trumped real similarity, the paper solidified the argument that the cognitive process of categorization is a fundamental building block of intergroup relations, prejudice, and conflict.
