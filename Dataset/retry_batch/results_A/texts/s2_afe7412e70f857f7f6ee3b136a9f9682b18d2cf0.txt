## 1. Official Abstract
> According to Simulation Theory, to understand what is going on in another person's mind, the observer uses his or her own mind as a model of the other mind. Recently, philosophers and cognitive neuroscientists have proposed that mirror neurones (which fire in response to both executing and observing a goal directed action) provide a plausible neural substrate for simulation, a mechanism for directly perceiving, or 'resonating' with, the contents of other minds. This article makes the case against Simulation Theory, using evidence from cognitive neuroscience, developmental psychology, and social psychology. In particular, the errors that adults and children make when reasoning about other minds are not consistent with the 'resonance' versions of Simulation Theory.

### Detailed Interpretation

Rebecca Saxe's 2005 paper, "Against simulation: the argument from error," presents a significant challenge to the Simulation Theory (ST) of mindreading, particularly the "resonance" version that gained prominence with the discovery of mirror neurons. Simulation Theory posits that we understand the mental states of others by using our own minds as a model, essentially simulating their experiences. The mirror neuron system, which shows similar activity when an individual performs an action and when they observe another performing that action, was proposed as a powerful neural basis for this process.

Saxe's central thesis, the "argument from error," contends that if mindreading were a process of direct simulation or resonance, the errors we make in predicting others' thoughts and beliefs should look very different. Instead, she argues that the *systematic* and predictable nature of these errors is inconsistent with ST and points towards a different mechanism, often referred to as "Theory-Theory" (TT). TT suggests that we use a folk-psychological theory—a set of abstract concepts and rules—to infer others' mental states.

The core of Saxe's argument is built on evidence from three main domains:

1.  **Developmental Psychology:** Saxe points to classic false-belief tasks. Children often make predictable, age-related errors, such as assuming that a person with incomplete information will necessarily be wrong or failing to recognize that someone can hold a belief that they themselves know to be false. Saxe argues that a simulation mechanism should not produce such consistent, rule-like errors. If a child were simply running a simulation of another's mind, it's not clear why they would systematically fail in this specific way. These errors are better explained by an incomplete or developing "theory" about how beliefs are formed and how they relate to reality.

2.  **Social Psychology:** Adults also make systematic errors in social judgment, often projecting their own knowledge and perspective onto others. This phenomenon, known as "egocentricity" or the "curse of knowledge," leads to predictable biases. While some have argued this supports simulation (as it starts from one's own case), Saxe's point is that the pattern of these errors suggests a flawed inferential process rather than a simple mirroring that has gone awry.

3.  **Cognitive Neuroscience:** Saxe argues that brain regions associated with the mirror neuron system are distinct from the regions implicated in "Theory of Mind" tasks, such as reasoning about false beliefs. The latter tasks consistently activate a specific network of brain regions, including the temporo-parietal junction (TPJ), which is not typically considered part of the core mirror system. This neurological dissociation suggests that understanding actions (the domain of mirror neurons) and understanding beliefs (the domain of Theory of Mind) rely on different neural substrates.

In conclusion, Saxe's "argument from error" uses the consistent and predictable mistakes people make in mental state attribution as evidence against the idea that we simply "resonate" with or simulate others' minds. The paper posits that these error patterns are more gracefully explained by a system that relies on abstract, theory-based reasoning about the mind, even if that theory is sometimes flawed or incorrectly applied. The paper was influential in the ongoing debate between Simulation Theory and Theory-Theory, forcing proponents of ST to provide more sophisticated accounts that could explain these systematic errors.
