## 1. Official Abstract
"Bystander intervention is a potentially potent tool in the primary prevention of sexual assault but more information is needed to guide prevention programs (Banyard 2008). This article presents a situational model of sexual assault prevention through bystander intervention that is based on Latané and Darley’s (1970) classic model of bystander intervention. The model identifies five barriers to bystander intervention in situations at risk for sexual assault: (1) failure to notice the event, (2) failure to identify the situation as high risk, (3) failure to take intervention responsibility, (4) failure to have the skills to intervene, and (5) failure to intervene due to audience inhibition. The article also presents the results of a study that tested some of the model’s propositions. Undergraduates (378 women and 210 men, primarily White) at a central coast California university completed an anonymous questionnaire measuring the five barriers and bystander intervention behavior. As expected, the barriers were negatively correlated with intervention, were greater for men than for women, and intervention likelihood was affected by perceptions of victim worthiness, especially for men. Hypotheses predicting a positive relationship between having a relationship with the potential victim or perpetrator and intervention were supported. Implications for sexual assault bystander intervention programming are provided."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible. The following is a synopsis organized by the paper's original sections.

### Introduction
The paper begins by establishing sexual assault as a significant problem on college campuses and introduces bystander intervention as a promising strategy for primary prevention. Author Shawn Meghan Burn notes that while prevention efforts have traditionally focused on potential victims and perpetrators, the bystander approach engages the entire community. The central framework adopted is Latané and Darley's (1970) five-step decision model of bystander helping. Burn adapts this model to the specific context of sexual assault, proposing five key steps (and corresponding barriers) for a bystander: noticing the event, identifying it as high-risk, taking personal responsibility, deciding how to help, and acting to intervene. The introduction posits that various situational and social factors can inhibit intervention at each of these stages. For example, ambiguity about consent can be a barrier to identifying a situation as risky, and diffusion of responsibility can prevent an individual from feeling personally obligated to act. The study aims to empirically test this situational model.

### Method
The study involved 588 undergraduate students (378 women, 210 men) from a California university. Participants completed an anonymous questionnaire designed to measure their experiences and perceptions related to bystander intervention in situations with a risk of sexual assault. The survey included measures for:
*   **Bystander Behavior:** How often participants had intervened in such situations.
*   **Barriers to Intervention:** A newly developed scale to assess the five barriers proposed by the model: failure to notice, failure to identify risk (e.g., due to ambiguity of consent, pluralistic ignorance), failure to take responsibility (diffusion of responsibility), skills deficit (not knowing what to do), and audience inhibition (fear of embarrassment).
*   **Situational Factors:** Questions about the participant's relationship to the potential victim and perpetrator, and perceptions of the victim's worthiness (e.g., if the victim was intoxicated or dressed provocatively).

### Results
The statistical analysis confirmed the model's core propositions. Key findings include:
*   **Barriers and Intervention:** All five identified barriers were negatively correlated with the likelihood of intervention. The more barriers a person perceived, the less likely they were to have intervened.
*   **Gender Differences:** Men reported significantly more barriers to intervention than women. Specifically, men were more likely to fail to take responsibility and to be inhibited by an audience.
*   **Victim Worthiness:** Perceptions of a victim's worthiness significantly influenced the decision to intervene, particularly for male bystanders. If a potential victim was perceived as having put themselves at risk (e.g., through intoxication), men were less likely to help.
*   **Relationship to Parties:** Having a personal relationship with either the potential victim or the potential perpetrator was positively correlated with intervention. Bystanders were more likely to act if they knew the people involved.

### Discussion
In the final section, Burn discusses the implications of these findings for designing more effective sexual assault prevention programs. The results validate the situational model, showing that bystander inaction is not simply a matter of apathy but is influenced by a series of specific, identifiable psychological and social barriers.

The author argues that prevention programs must be tailored to overcome these specific barriers. For instance:
*   To counter **ambiguity**, programs should educate students on recognizing high-risk situations and early warning signs, and challenge norms that blur the lines of consent.
*   To combat **diffusion of responsibility**, programs should emphasize personal accountability and the power of a single individual to make a difference.
*   To address **skills deficits**, training must provide concrete, practical strategies for intervention, ranging from direct confrontation to distraction or seeking help from others.
*   The study highlights the particular importance of targeting interventions toward men, focusing on dismantling norms around masculinity that may inhibit helping behavior and challenging victim-blaming attitudes.

Ultimately, the paper concludes that a situational, barrier-focused approach provides a robust framework for developing evidence-based bystander intervention programs that can empower communities to effectively reduce sexual assault.
