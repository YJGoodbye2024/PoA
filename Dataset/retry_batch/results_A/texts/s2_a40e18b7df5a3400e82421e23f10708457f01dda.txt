## 1. Official Abstract
"The attraction effect refers to an inferior product's ability to increase the attractiveness of another alternative when the inferior product is added to a choice set. This article examines potential explanations for the attraction effect and its boundary conditions. The article reports several empirical investigations and suggests that the attraction effect may be moderated by such variables as stimulus meaningfulness and familiarity with the product category. The implications are relevant to research on context effects in consumer choice."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on the abstract and analysis from citing scholarly articles.

The 1987 paper by Ratneshwar, Shocker, and Stewart, "Toward understanding the attraction effect: The implications of product stimulus meaningfulness and familiarity," is a foundational text in the study of context effects in consumer choice. It investigates the "attraction effect," a phenomenon where the appeal of an option is enhanced by the introduction of a third, asymmetrically dominated option (a "decoy"). The paper's primary contribution is its exploration of the boundary conditions of this effect, specifically how a consumer's familiarity with a product category and the meaningfulness of the product stimuli can reduce or eliminate the attraction effect.

The authors conducted several empirical studies to test their hypotheses. Their findings suggest that the attraction effect, while robust in certain conditions, is not universally applicable. When consumers have a high degree of familiarity or knowledge about a product category, they are less likely to be swayed by the presence of a decoy. This is because knowledgeable consumers are thought to rely more on their established preferences and less on the immediate context of the choice set.

Similarly, the "meaningfulness" of the product attributes presented to the consumer plays a crucial role. When the attributes are meaningful and readily understandable, consumers can more easily evaluate the options on their merits, thus diminishing the influence of the decoy. The paper posits that the attraction effect is most pronounced in situations where the consumer is uncertain and the choice context is ambiguous.

Subsequent research has built upon and, in some cases, challenged the findings of Ratneshwar et al. While some studies have corroborated the idea that expertise can mitigate the attraction effect, others have found the relationship between knowledge and the attraction effect to be complex and not always straightforward. For instance, some research has found that knowledge only sometimes reduces the attraction effect, suggesting that other factors, such as motivation, may also be at play. Despite these nuances, the paper is consistently cited as a key work for its early and insightful investigation into the moderators of the attraction effect, highlighting that the effect is not a simple cognitive bias but is contingent on the characteristics of the decision-maker and the decision-making context.
