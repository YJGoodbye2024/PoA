## 1. Official Abstract
> In this paper we present a prototype approach to awe. We suggest that two appraisals are central and are present in all clear cases of awe: perceived vastness, and a need for accommodation, defined as an inability to assimilate an experience into current mental structures. Five additional appraisals account for variation in the hedonic tone of awe experiences: threat, beauty, exceptional ability, virtue, and the supernatural. We derive this perspective from a review of what has been written about awe in religion, philosophy, sociology, and psychology, and then we apply this perspective to an analysis of awe and related states such as admiration, elevation, and the epiphanic experience.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on analyses from reliable third-party and author-provided sources.

In their seminal 2003 paper, psychologists Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt presented a foundational "conceptual approach to awe" that has guided the scientific study of the emotion. Prior to this work, awe was a topic largely explored by religion, philosophy, and sociology, with only a fleeting interest from psychologists. The paper's primary contribution was to propose a theoretical framework for defining, conceptualizing, and studying awe.

The core of their thesis is that the experience of awe is a combination of two central cognitive appraisals:
1.  **Perceived Vastness**: This refers to encountering something immense in size, scope, or complexity that transcends one's current frame of reference. This vastness is not limited to physical size (like the Grand Canyon) but can also be conceptual, such as a grand theory or a person of immense prestige.
2.  **A Need for Accommodation**: Vastness alone is not enough to elicit awe; it must also challenge one's existing mental structures. This "need for accommodation" is the cognitive effort required to update and adjust one's understanding of the world to assimilate the new, vast experience. It is this process of mental realignment that makes awe a "mind-blowing" experience. For example, an experience that requires accommodation but lacks vastness would likely be perceived as surprise, not awe.

Keltner and Haidt argued that these two features are present in all clear instances of awe. They also identified five additional appraisals that shape the specific feeling or "hedonic tone" of the awe experience. These secondary features explain why awe can feel enlightening, inspiring, or even frightening:
*   Threat
*   Beauty
*   Exceptional Ability
*   Virtue
*   The Supernatural

The paper reviewed the history of awe across various disciplines to derive this perspective. Early conceptions treated awe primarily as a religious or spiritual emotion, often related to reverence for a deity or a transformative spiritual event. Keltner and Haidt's work was instrumental in the "secularization or the democratization of awe," providing a framework to study it as a distinct psychological state.

Subsequent research, building on this 2003 paper, has empirically validated that awe is a discrete emotion, distinct from related states like fear, joy, or beauty. Studies have shown that awe can lead to a diminished sense of self (the "small self"), increased feelings of connectedness to others, and a greater tendency toward prosocial behaviors like generosity.
