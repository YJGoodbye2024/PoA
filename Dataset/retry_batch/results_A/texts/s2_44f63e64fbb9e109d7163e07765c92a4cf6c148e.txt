## 1. Official Abstract
>Strong a or weak, extreme or mild, certain or uncertain, attitudes are as richly diverse as the judgments and behavior they oft en influence. Years of systematic study of these attitudinal qualities have tackled the age-old questions of when and how attitudes relate to behavior. What sorts of attitudes relate reliably to judgments and behavior? Under what conditions is attitude-behavior correspondence most likely to occur? By what processes do attitudes exert their influence? The MODE model (Motivation and Opportunity as Determinants of the attitude-behavior relation) was developed to address these historical and fundamental questions (Fazio, 1990; Fazio & Towles-Schwen, 1999). We will describe the tenets of the model in this chapter, and in so doing illuminate the multiple paths from attitude to behavior delineated by the MODE model.

The 2009 chapter by Michael A. Olson and Russell H. Fazio, "Implicit and Explicit Measures of Attitudes: The Perspective of the MODE Model," serves as a key theoretical statement positioning the MODE model as a comprehensive framework for understanding the relationship between implicit (automatic) and explicit (deliberative) attitudes and their consequent effects on behavior. The MODE model—an acronym for **M**otivation and **O**pportunity as **DE**terminants of behavior—provides a dual-process theory explaining how attitudes guide actions.

**Core Argument of the MODE Model:**

The central thesis presented in the chapter is that the MODE model offers a more parsimonious explanation for the functioning of implicit and explicit attitudes than dual-system theories that propose entirely separate implicit and explicit attitude structures. Instead of two distinct attitude systems, the MODE model posits two different *processes* by which a single underlying attitude can influence behavior. The process that takes precedence is determined by the presence or absence of motivation and opportunity.

*   **Spontaneous Process:** When an individual lacks either the motivation or the cognitive opportunity (e.g., time, resources) to think deliberately, their behavior is guided by a spontaneous process. In this mode, the most accessible attitudes are automatically activated upon encountering the attitude object and directly shape perceptions and behavior. Implicit measures, such as the Implicit Association Test (IAT), are designed to capture these automatically activated associations.
*   **Deliberative Process:** When an individual is both motivated and has the opportunity to engage in careful thought, they will enter a deliberative processing mode. In this path, they can consciously retrieve and consider their beliefs and attitudes, weigh information, and control their responses. Explicit measures, like self-report questionnaires, are influenced by this deliberative process. Responding to an explicit measure is a verbal behavior subject to motivational concerns, such as social desirability.

**Interpretation of Implicit vs. Explicit Measures:**

From the perspective of the MODE model, the frequent divergence between implicit and explicit measures does not necessarily mean they tap into two different underlying attitudes. Rather, the model explains this dissociation by considering the processes involved in each type of measurement. Explicit measures are subject to deliberative control and motivational factors (e.g., the desire to not appear prejudiced), while implicit measures are designed to bypass this control to assess more automatic, accessible evaluations.

Olson and Fazio argue that the interaction between automatically activated attitudes and a person's motivation to control their expression is critical. For instance, in the context of racial prejudice, the model can explain why someone with a negative automatically-activated attitude (as revealed by an implicit measure) might not exhibit prejudiced behavior. If they are motivated to control prejudiced reactions and have the opportunity to do so, they can override the initial automatic response.

In summary, the chapter positions the MODE model as an overarching framework that accounts for when and how both spontaneous and deliberated attitudes guide behavior. It argues that the key is not whether a person has two different attitudes (one implicit, one explicit), but rather which processing path—spontaneous or deliberative—is engaged in a given situation, which in turn determines whether automatically accessible associations or controlled cognitions will have a greater influence on their actions.
