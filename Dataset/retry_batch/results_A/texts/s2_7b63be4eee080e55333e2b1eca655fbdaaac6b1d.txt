### 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. The following synopsis is structured according to the paper's original sections.

#### **Introduction**
The authors introduce the concept of automatic attitudes—evaluations that are activated unintentionally and exist outside of conscious awareness. They contrast these with explicit attitudes, which are deliberate and self-reported. The central question is whether these seemingly rigid automatic attitudes are, in fact, malleable. The paper challenges the notion that implicit biases are fixed, proposing that they are context-dependent. The authors hypothesize that "implicit evaluations of groups... depend on the context in which they are expressed and on the set of exemplars that are triggered in that context." The research aims to test if exposing individuals to counter-stereotypical exemplars (e.g., admired members of a disliked group and disliked members of a liked group) can alter their automatic biases as measured by the Implicit Association Test (IAT).

#### **Experiment 1: Malleability of Automatic Racial Attitudes**
This experiment investigated whether automatic prejudice against Black Americans could be temporarily reduced.

*   **Method:** Participants were divided into three groups. The first group was exposed to images of admired Black individuals (e.g., Martin Luther King Jr., Denzel Washington) and disliked White individuals (e.g., Jeffrey Dahmer, Timothy McVeigh). The second group saw disliked Black individuals (e.g., Mike Tyson, O. J. Simpson) and admired White individuals (e.g., John F. Kennedy, Albert Einstein). A control group viewed nonracial exemplars (e.g., flowers and insects). Following this exposure, participants' automatic racial attitudes were measured using the IAT, and their explicit attitudes were measured with feeling thermometers and semantic differential scales. These measurements were taken immediately after the exposure and again 24 hours later.

*   **Results:** The results showed a significant shift in automatic attitudes for the first group. Exposure to admired Black and disliked White exemplars "significantly weakened automatic pro-White attitudes for 24 hr beyond the treatment." Conversely, the group exposed to pro-White exemplars showed a stronger pro-White bias. The control group showed no change. Importantly, these changes were observed in the implicit IAT scores but not in the explicit, self-reported attitude measures. This finding suggests that the manipulation specifically targeted automatic associations rather than conscious beliefs.

#### **Experiment 2: Malleability of Automatic Age-Related Attitudes**
The second experiment was designed to determine if the findings from Experiment 1 could be generalized to another domain of prejudice: ageism.

*   **Method:** The procedure was similar to Experiment 1, but the focus was on attitudes toward older and younger people. One group of participants was exposed to pictures of admired older individuals (e.g., Nelson Mandela, Mother Teresa) and disliked younger individuals (e.g., Tonya Harding, Macaulay Culkin). The other group saw disliked older and admired younger individuals. An IAT measuring automatic preference for "young" versus "old" was administered afterward, along with explicit measures.

*   **Results:** The results replicated the findings of the first experiment. Participants who were exposed to admired older exemplars and disliked younger ones showed a significant reduction in their automatic preference for young people. As in Experiment 1, this effect was not observed on the explicit measures of ageism. This replication strengthened the claim that the effect was not specific to race but could be a more general phenomenon of attitude malleability.

#### **General Discussion**
In the final section, the authors discuss the implications of their findings. They conclude that automatic attitudes are not immutable and can be sensitive to immediate social context. The study provides a clear method for temporarily modifying these biases by manipulating the specific group members that come to mind.

The authors argue that this malleability occurs because attitudes are not retrieved as stable, stored evaluations. Instead, they are constructed "on the fly" based on the information that is most accessible at that moment. By making positive out-group members and negative in-group members more accessible, the resulting automatic evaluation changes. The paper concludes that these findings offer a potential strategy for prejudice reduction: "these studies provide a strategy that attempts to change the social context and, through it, to reduce automatic prejudice and preference."
