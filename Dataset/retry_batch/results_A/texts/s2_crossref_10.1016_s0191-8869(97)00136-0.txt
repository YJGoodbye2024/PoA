## 1. Official Abstract
The relationships between jealousy, personality, attachment styles and birth order were examined in a sample of 100 Dutch men and 100 Dutch women. Three types of jealousy were examined: reactive jealousy (a negative response to the emotional or sexual involvement of the partner with someone else), preventive jealousy (efforts to prevent intimate contact of the partner with a third person), and anxious jealousy (obsessive anxiety, upset, and worrying about the possibility of infidelity of the partner). The three types of jealousy were particularly correlated with neuroticism, social anxiety, rigidity and hostility. As hypothesized, individuals with an anxious-ambivalent attachment style reported more of all three types of jealousy than those with an avoidant or a secure attachment style. Most important, and also as hypothesized, laterborns were more jealous according to all three measures than firstborns, a finding that was not due to personality differences between first- and laterborns, nor to a different distribution of attachment styles among first- and laterborns.

### **Detailed Interpretation**

This 1997 study by Bram P. Buunk is a significant contribution to the understanding of jealousy, moving beyond a monolithic view to explore its different facets and antecedents. The research investigates how individual differences—specifically personality, adult attachment styles, and birth order—relate to distinct types of jealous experiences.

**Conceptual Framework:**
The study's primary innovation was its distinction between three types of jealousy:
*   **Reactive Jealousy:** An emotional response to a partner's actual infidelity or involvement (sexual or emotional) with another person.
*   **Preventive (or Possessive) Jealousy:** Behavioral actions and efforts aimed at preventing a partner from having intimate contact with potential rivals.
*   **Anxious Jealousy:** Cognitive and obsessive worrying, rumination, and anxiety about the *possibility* of a partner's infidelity, which can occur even without a real threat.

**Methodology:**
The research was conducted on a sample of 200 Dutch adults, comprising 100 men and 100 women who were in a relationship. The study employed self-report measures to assess the three types of jealousy, personality traits, attachment styles, and the participants' birth order.

**Key Findings:**
The analysis produced several important findings that link individual differences to jealousy:

*   **Personality Correlates:** All three forms of jealousy were found to be significantly correlated with personality traits such as neuroticism, social anxiety, rigidity, and hostility. This suggests that individuals with a predisposition to negative emotionality and interpersonal difficulties are more prone to experiencing jealousy in all its forms.

*   **Attachment Style:** The study confirmed a strong link between attachment theory and jealousy. Individuals with an **anxious-ambivalent** attachment style reported significantly higher levels of reactive, preventive, and anxious jealousy compared to those with secure or avoidant attachment styles. This aligns with attachment theory, which posits that those with an anxious style crave closeness but fear abandonment, making them hypervigilant to relationship threats.

*   **The Role of Birth Order:** The most notable and unique finding of the paper was the independent effect of birth order. Later-born individuals reported higher levels of all three types of jealousy than first-borns. Crucially, the study demonstrated that this relationship was not an artifact of personality or attachment style. Even after controlling for these factors, the effect of birth order remained significant. This suggests that the experience of being "dethroned" by a younger sibling, as posited in birth order theory, may create a lasting sensitivity to rivalry and threats within close relationships that is distinct from one's core personality or attachment model.

**Conclusion & Significance:**
Buunk's paper effectively argues that jealousy is not a single feeling but a multidimensional construct with different expressions (emotional, behavioral, cognitive). It established that while personality and attachment are strong predictors of jealousy, birth order also plays a unique and independent role. The finding that later-borns are more prone to jealousy, irrespective of their attachment style or personality, provided a new avenue for research into the developmental origins of jealousy.
