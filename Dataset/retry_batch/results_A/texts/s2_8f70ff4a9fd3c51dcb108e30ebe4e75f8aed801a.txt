## 1. Official Abstract
"Ten years of research on the false-consensus effect (Ross, Greene, & House, 1977) and related biases in social perception (e.g., assumed similarity and overestimation of consensus) are examined in the light of four general theoretical perspectives: (a) selective exposure and cognitive availability, (b) salience and focus of attention, (c) logical information processing, and (d) motivational processes. The findings indicate that these biases are robust and occur across a wide variety of settings. The data further suggest that two or more specific mechanisms may operate simultaneously or in concert to produce assumed similarity and false-consensus effects. Discussion focuses on identifying the process or sets of processes operating in specific situations. We identify several gaps in the knowledge of mediating relationships and suggest directions for future research. We also discuss issues related to definition and measurement."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible. The following is a synopsis structured according to the paper's primary sections.

### **Introduction**
The paper begins by defining the false-consensus effect as the tendency for people to "see their own behavioral choices and judgments as relatively common and appropriate to existing circumstances while viewing alternative responses as uncommon, deviant, or inappropriate." This review synthesizes the first decade of research following the seminal work by Ross, Greene, and House (1977). The authors, Marks and Miller, state their objective is to examine the empirical findings through the lens of four main theoretical explanations that have been proposed for the effect.

### **Four Theoretical Perspectives**
The core of the paper is dedicated to reviewing the evidence for four different, though not mutually exclusive, theoretical mechanisms that could explain the false-consensus effect.

1.  **Selective Exposure and Cognitive Availability:** This perspective argues that the false-consensus effect stems from the fact that people tend to associate with others who are similar to them in background, attitudes, and values. This selective exposure leads to a biased sample of the population from which individuals generalize. Consequently, when asked to estimate broader consensus, people's own views are more "available" in their minds, leading them to overestimate their prevalence. The authors review studies showing that people do indeed tend to be exposed to and recall information that is consistent with their own beliefs, thereby inflating their consensus estimates.

2.  **Salience and Focus of Attention:** This explanation posits that a person's own position on an issue is more salient and attended to than any other position. When an individual focuses on their own stance, it becomes a cognitive anchor point. The process of estimating consensus for other viewpoints involves adjusting from this anchor, but this adjustment is often insufficient. The paper discusses research indicating that the simple act of contemplating one's own belief makes it more prominent in thought, which in turn biases estimates of social consensus.

3.  **Logical Information Processing:** According to this view, the false-consensus effect can be a product of rational, albeit sometimes flawed, reasoning. An individual may attribute their own behavior to situational factors ("I chose this because the situation logically called for it"). If they believe their action was determined by the situation, it is logical for them to assume that other rational people in the same situation would behave similarly. The review notes that this perspective treats the effect not as a bias in information processing, but as a result of a person's causal attributions.

4.  **Motivational Processes:** This perspective suggests that the false-consensus effect is driven by the need to maintain a positive self-image and validate one's beliefs. Overestimating how many people share one's opinions can serve several motivational functions: it can provide a sense of social support, affirm the correctness of one's views, and bolster self-esteem. The authors review evidence suggesting that the desire for self-validation and social belonging can motivate individuals to perceive a greater degree of consensus for their own positions.

### **Discussion**
In the final section, Marks and Miller conclude that the false-consensus effect is a robust phenomenon, consistently demonstrated across a wide range of topics and experimental settings. They argue that no single theoretical perspective can exclusively account for all the findings. Instead, it is more likely that these mechanisms work in concert. For example, selective exposure might create a biased sample of information, which is then amplified by the salience of one's own opinion and further reinforced by the motivation to see oneself as part of a majority.

The authors also identify gaps in the existing research, calling for more studies that can distinguish between the different theoretical explanations and clarify the specific conditions under which each mechanism is most influential. They conclude by discussing methodological issues related to the definition and measurement of the effect, suggesting that future research would benefit from more precise operational definitions.
