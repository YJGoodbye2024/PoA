## 1. Official Abstract
"Under growing public and government pressure, the television networks have adopted warning labels for violent programs. Tainted fruit theory posits that warning labels will decrease interest in violent programs, whereas forbidden fruit theory posits that warning labels will increase interest in violent programs. In Experiment 1, it was found that warning labels increased interest in violent programs, especially when the label source was authoritative. In Experiment 2, it was found that high-reactance individuals were especially interested in viewing violent programs with warning labels. In Experiment 3, it was found that warning labels increased interest in violent programs more than did information labels."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible.

### **Introduction**
The paper introduces two competing theories regarding the effect of warning labels on violent television programming. The "tainted fruit" theory suggests that such labels will make the programs less attractive to viewers. In contrast, the "forbidden fruit" theory, rooted in psychological reactance theory, predicts the opposite outcome: warning labels will increase attraction to the restricted content. Psychological reactance is a motivational state that arises when an individual's freedom is threatened, leading them to desire the forbidden option more strongly. The authors note that despite the implementation of warning labels by television networks due to public and governmental pressure, there was little empirical research to determine which of these two effects would prevail.

### **Experiment 1**
The first experiment aimed to test whether warning labels increase or decrease interest in violent programs and to determine if the authoritativeness of the label's source mattered. The findings supported the "forbidden fruit" hypothesis. Participants showed a greater interest in violent programs that had warning labels compared to those without. This effect was particularly pronounced when the warning label was attributed to an authoritative source, such as the U.S. federal government.

### **Experiment 2**
This experiment investigated the role of individual personality differences, specifically the trait of "reactance." The study hypothesized that individuals with high reactance—a greater tendency to resist threats to their freedom—would be more attracted to violent programs with warning labels. The results confirmed this hypothesis, showing that high-reactance individuals expressed a significantly stronger interest in viewing violent programs that carried a warning label. This provides direct support for the idea that psychological reactance underpins the "forbidden fruit" effect.

### **Experiment 3**
The final experiment was designed to distinguish between two types of labels: "warning labels" that advise viewers against watching and "information labels" that simply describe the content. The purpose was to see if the restrictive nature of the warning was the key factor driving increased interest. The results showed that warning labels increased attraction to violent programs more than purely informational labels did. This finding further isolates the reactance effect, suggesting that it is the perceived attempt to restrict viewing, rather than the information about the content itself, that makes the program more desirable.

### **Conclusion**
Across three experiments, the research consistently found that warning labels paradoxically increase attraction to violent television content, a phenomenon the authors term the "forbidden fruit" effect. This effect is stronger when the warning comes from an authoritative source and is more pronounced among individuals with a high degree of psychological reactance. The authors conclude that those aiming to reduce the viewership of violent media should be cautious, as warning labels may have the opposite of their intended effect.
