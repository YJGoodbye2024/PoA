## 1. Official Abstract
"The study assesses how a social desirability (SD) bias influences the relationship between several independent and dependent variables commonly investigated in ethics research. The effect of a SD bias was observed when a questionnaire was administered under varying conditions of anonymity and with different measurement techniques for the SD construct. Findings reveal that a SD bias is present in the majority of relationships studied, and it most frequently plays a moderating role. While the measure of SD influences the strength and type of relationship, condition of anonymity has relatively little effect on the level of SD."

**Detailed Interpretation**

The 1991 paper by Donna M. Randall and Maria F. Fernandes is a foundational study in business ethics research that empirically investigates the impact of social desirability response bias (SDRB). SDRB is the tendency of individuals to answer survey questions in a way they believe will be viewed favorably by others, rather than answering truthfully. This can significantly distort findings in sensitive research areas like ethics, where self-reported data is common. Randall and Gibson's earlier work in 1990 noted that while nearly 90% of business ethics studies used self-reported data, very few controlled for this bias, setting the stage for this focused inquiry.

The core of Randall and Fernandes' study was to determine how SDRB affects the relationships between variables typically studied in ethics research. They examined this by manipulating two key factors: the anonymity of the survey administration and the specific instrument used to measure social desirability.

Key findings and contributions of the paper, as interpreted by subsequent research, include:

*   **Prevalence of SDRB:** The study confirmed that a social desirability bias is a significant and present factor in the majority of relationships examined within ethics research. This finding challenged the validity of previous studies that relied on self-reported behaviors and attitudes without accounting for this bias.
*   **SDRB as a Moderator:** The research demonstrated that SDRB most often acts as a *moderating* variable. This means that the bias changes the strength or even the direction of the relationship between an independent variable (e.g., a demographic factor) and a dependent variable (e.g., an ethical judgment or behavioral intention).
*   **Anonymity is Not a Panacea:** A crucial and somewhat counterintuitive finding was that the condition of anonymity had "relatively little effect" on the level of socially desirable responding. Other studies have confirmed that even when anonymity is assured, SDRB can still be a significant factor. This suggests that the bias is not just about external impression management but may also involve elements of self-deception.
*   **Choice of Measurement Matters:** The study found that the specific measurement tool used to assess social desirability has a significant impact on the results. The authors compared different scales, and later research highlights that the impression management subscale of Paulhus's Balanced Inventory of Desirable Responding (BIDR) had a high correlation with other measures used in the 1991 study. This underscored the need for researchers to be deliberate and transparent in their choice of SDRB measurement tools.

In essence, Randall and Fernandes's work served as a critical methodological warning to the field of business ethics. It demonstrated empirically that simply assuring anonymity is not enough to mitigate response bias. Researchers, they argued, must actively measure and control for social desirability to produce valid and reliable findings. The paper is frequently cited to justify the inclusion of SDRB scales in subsequent ethics and marketing research and to caution against over-interpreting gender differences in ethics studies, as females sometimes score higher on social desirability scales.
