## 1. Official Abstract
> How does emotion explain the relationship between social rejection and aggression? Rejection reliably damages mood, leaving individuals motivated to repair their negatively valenced affective state. Retaliatory aggression is often a pleasant experience. Rejected individuals may then harness revenge's associated positive affect to repair their mood. Across 6 studies (total N = 1,516), we tested the prediction that the rejection–aggression link is motivated by expected and actual mood repair. Further, we predicted that this mood repair would occur through the positive affect of retaliatory aggression. In Study 1, rejected participants reported that they expected to feel better after aggressing. In Studies 2, 3, and 4, the link between rejection and aggression was mediated by participants' implicit motive to seek pleasure. In Studies 3 and 4, these effects were mediated by sadistic impulses toward finding aggression pleasant. Suggesting the occurrence of actual mood repair, rejected participants' affective states were equivalent to their accepted counterparts after an act of aggression (Studies 5 and 6). This mood repair occurred through a dynamic interplay between preaggression affect and aggression itself, and was driven by increases in positive affect (Studies 5 and 6). Together, these findings suggest that the rejection–aggression link is driven, in part, by the desire to return to affective homeostasis. Additionally, these findings implicate aggression's rewarding nature as an incentive for rejected individuals' violent tendencies.

### Detailed Interpretation

The paper "Combating the Sting of Rejection With the Pleasure of Revenge" presents a novel framework for understanding the well-established link between social rejection and aggression. The central thesis is that aggression is not merely a "hot," anger-fueled reaction to rejection, but can also be a "cold," calculated strategy for mood repair. The authors, David S. Chester and C. Nathan DeWall, propose that because rejection damages a person's mood, individuals become motivated to restore their emotional balance (affective homeostasis). They argue that retaliatory aggression, or revenge, serves as an effective, if destructive, tool for this mood repair because it can be a genuinely pleasant and rewarding experience.

The research is built upon a series of six studies involving over 1,500 participants, which collectively test the hypothesis that the motivation to aggress after rejection is driven by the expectation and actual experience of mood improvement.

Key findings across the six studies are as follows:
*   **Expectation of Mood Repair:** The research first established that rejected individuals expect to feel better after taking revenge. This suggests a cognitive, forward-looking component to retaliatory aggression.
*   **Implicit Motives:** A crucial part of the authors' argument is that this drive for pleasure is not always conscious. Using implicit measures, they showed that the connection between rejection and aggression was mediated by an underlying, automatic motive to seek pleasure and by sadistic impulses, defined as the tendency to find aggression pleasant.
*   **Evidence of Actual Mood Repair:** The final studies demonstrated that aggression successfully repairs mood. After being given an opportunity to aggress against the person who rejected them, rejected participants' moods returned to a level equivalent to that of participants who had not been rejected at all. This provides strong evidence for the mood-enhancing effect of retaliation.
*   **The Role of Positive Affect:** The analysis confirmed that this mood repair was specifically driven by an *increase in positive affect* (feeling good), not just a decrease in negative affect (feeling less bad). This supports the idea that revenge is sought for its rewarding, pleasure-inducing qualities.

In essence, the paper reframes retaliatory aggression as a form of emotion regulation. Rejected individuals use the pleasure of revenge as a tool to counteract the emotional pain of being ostracized. This work implicates aggression's rewarding nature, rooted in the brain's dopaminergic reward circuitry, as a powerful incentive for violence following social rejection.
