## 1. Official Abstract
"Attempts to understand the personal characteristics of others, in interactions with them, are complicated by the fact that one tends to find what one expects. This happens not only because processing of information is selective, but also because expectancies cause one to act in ways that elicit behavior interpretable as confirming those expectancies, even when the expectancies might have been mistaken. Studies provide ample evidence of such self-fulfilling prophecies in social interaction and are beginning to spell out the crucial steps in the process for confirming expectancies. Such studies help link the psychology of first impressions to the psychology of long-term relationships by showing how expectancies are sustained or modified through behavioral sequences that are partially determined by initial expectancies."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on analyses and citations from related academic literature.

E. E. Jones's 1986 paper, "Interpreting Interpersonal Behavior: The Effects of Expectancies," is a foundational work in social psychology that explores how our preconceived notions about others shape our social reality. The central argument is that our expectancies do not just passively filter our perception but actively influence interactions to create self-fulfilling prophecies. This process occurs through two primary mechanisms: perceptual confirmation, where we selectively notice and interpret behavior that aligns with our expectations, and behavioral confirmation, where our own actions cause another person to behave in ways that confirm our initial beliefs.

The paper delves into the mechanics of how these expectancies operate in interpersonal dynamics. Jones argues that people often see what they expect to see, a process that involves selecting evidence that confirms stereotypes while ignoring contradictory information. This concept is a cornerstone of the "reflection-construction model," which examines how a perceiver's expectations can be both a reflection of a target's actual attributes and a construction of social reality through self-fulfilling influence. The power of these expectancies is particularly potent in new or ambiguous situations where individuals may be more susceptible to external cues to guide their behavior.

A key contribution of the paper is its role in bridging the psychology of first impressions with the study of long-term relationships. Jones posits that initial expectancies, whether accurate or not, trigger behavioral sequences that can sustain those beliefs over time. For instance, a teacher who expects a student to perform well may treat that student in a more encouraging manner, thereby fostering the very academic success they anticipated—a phenomenon known as the "Pygmalion effect." Conversely, negative expectancies can perpetuate inequity; for example, employers' stereotypes about certain groups can lead to behaviors that elicit confirmation of those negative beliefs in the workplace.

The theory also acknowledges that this is an interactive process. It involves active participation from both the perceiver and the target, whose interplay determines the outcome. The influence of these pre-interaction cognitions is often strongest at the beginning of an encounter and may diminish as the interaction unfolds and individuals adjust to each other's actual behaviors. Ultimately, Jones's work emphasizes that social perception is not a passive recording of reality but a constructive process where our beliefs can actively shape the interpersonal world we experience.
