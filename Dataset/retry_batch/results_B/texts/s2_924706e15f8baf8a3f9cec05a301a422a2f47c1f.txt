## 1. Official Abstract
Researchers have recently questioned the benefits associated with having high self-esteem. The authors propose that the importance of self-esteem lies more in how people strive for it rather than whether it is high or low. They argue that in domains in which their self-worth is invested, people adopt the goal to validate their abilities and qualities, and hence their self-worth. When people have self-validation goals, they react to threats in these domains in ways that undermine learning; relatedness; autonomy and self-regulation; and over time, mental and physical health. The short-term emotional benefits of pursuing self-esteem are often outweighed by long-term costs. Previous research on self-esteem is reinterpreted in terms of self-esteem striving. Cultural roots of the pursuit of self-esteem are considered. Finally, the alternatives to pursuing self-esteem, and ways of avoiding its costs, are discussed.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible for analysis. The paper is a theoretical review that proposes a model about the consequences of pursuing self-esteem.

### **Introduction: The Pursuit of Self-Esteem**
The paper begins by challenging the widely held belief in American culture that high self-esteem is a primary good. The authors, Jennifer Crocker and Lora E. Park, propose that the crucial issue is not the *level* of self-esteem (high vs. low) but rather *how* people pursue it. They argue that when self-worth is attached to specific outcomes or domains (contingencies of self-worth), individuals adopt a goal of self-validation. This drive to prove one's worth in contingent domains leads to a range of negative consequences, even if it provides short-term emotional boosts. The central thesis is that the pursuit of self-esteem is often "costly."

### **When and Where Do People Pursue Self-Esteem?**
People pursue self-esteem when they stake their self-worth on succeeding in specific areas, known as "contingencies of self-worth." These domains can include academic competence, physical appearance, others' approval, or outdoing others in competition. When an outcome in a contingent domain is perceived as a reflection of one's overall value, the goal becomes validation. For example, a student whose self-worth is contingent on academics may view getting into graduate school not just as an achievement, but as a validation of their fundamental ability and worth. Failure in these domains is not just a setback; it is interpreted as a personal indictment, leading to thoughts like "I am a failure."

### **The Costs of Pursuing Self-Esteem**
The core of the paper details the negative consequences of adopting self-validation goals, which arise from the pursuit of self-esteem.

*   **Costs for Learning:** The pursuit of self-esteem undermines learning and competence. When focused on validating the self, individuals may avoid challenges, dismiss negative feedback, and become anxious, which interferes with performance and skill development. They become preoccupied with their performance rather than the task itself, which hinders the learning process.
*   **Costs for Relatedness:** Striving for self-esteem can damage interpersonal relationships. When threatened in a domain of contingency, individuals may become defensive, self-preoccupied, and less empathetic or supportive toward others. This focus on the self at the expense of others' needs can lead to conflict and social disconnection.
*   **Costs for Autonomy and Self-Regulation:** The pursuit of self-esteem can interfere with autonomous self-regulation. Instead of choosing goals that align with intrinsic interests and values, people may select goals aimed at validating their self-worth. This external pressure can lead to procrastination and decreased motivation for activities that do not offer a self-esteem boost.
*   **Costs for Mental and Physical Health:** Over time, the cycle of pursuing self-esteem, experiencing threats, and reacting defensively can be detrimental to health. The pressure to constantly validate one's worth can lead to anxiety and depression. The authors also link this pursuit to increased stress and potential negative impacts on physical health.

### **Reinterpreting the Literature on Self-Esteem**
Crocker and Park reinterpret previous findings on self-esteem through the lens of their model. For instance, the defensive and sometimes aggressive reactions of individuals with high self-esteem when threatened can be understood as a consequence of their *pursuit* of self-esteem in a contingent domain, rather than a direct product of having high self-esteem itself. Similarly, the behaviors of those with low self-esteem, such as seeking reassurance, are framed as strategies to restore a sense of worth.

### **Alternatives to Pursuing Self-Esteem**
In the final section, the authors discuss alternatives to the costly pursuit of self-esteem. The key is to shift one's primary goals away from self-validation. They propose two alternative goal structures:
1.  **Learning and Development Goals:** Adopting goals focused on learning, improvement, and mastering challenges, rather than proving one's worth. With this orientation, failure is seen as an opportunity for growth, not a reflection of personal inadequacy.
2.  **Goals Larger Than the Self:** Focusing on contributing to something beyond the self, such as the well-being of others or a collective cause. This shifts attention away from self-evaluation and toward a more meaningful purpose.

By adopting these alternative goals, individuals can reduce their preoccupation with self-worth, avoid the defensive reactions to threats, and engage more authentically with challenges and with other people.
