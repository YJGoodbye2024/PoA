## 1. Official Abstract
"Research has shown that extraverted leadership is a consistent predictor of leadership emergence and effectiveness. However, we propose that the extraverted leadership advantage is overestimated. Drawing on dominance complementarity theory, we propose that although extraverted leadership enhances group performance when employees are passive, this effect reverses when employees are proactive, because extraverted leaders are less receptive to proactivity. In Study 1, we found that in pizza stores, leader extraversion was positively related to store profits when employees were low in proactivity and negatively related to store profits when employees were high in proactivity. In Study 2, we replicated these findings in a laboratory experiment: passive groups performed better when led by an extravert, whereas proactive groups performed better when led by an introvert. Our results show that the effectiveness of extraverted leadership depends on the proactivity of followers and highlight the unique advantages of introverted leadership in empowering proactive followers."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### Introduction
The authors begin by highlighting the established link between extraversion and leadership effectiveness, noting that extraverts are often perceived as "prototypical" leaders. However, they question whether this advantage holds true under all conditions, particularly in modern organizations where employee proactivity is encouraged. The central argument is that the very traits that make extraverts effective leaders—assertiveness and dominance—can make them less receptive to proactive suggestions from employees. Drawing on dominance complementarity theory, which suggests that interactions are smoother when dominance from one party is met with submission from the other, the paper hypothesizes that extraverted leadership is beneficial when employees are passive but detrimental when they are proactive. Conversely, introverted leaders are proposed to be more effective when managing proactive employees because they are more likely to listen to and act on their suggestions.

### Study 1: Field Study in a Pizza Delivery Chain
*   **Methods:** The first study was conducted with a U.S. national pizza delivery company. Data was collected from 57 different stores. Employee proactivity was measured by surveying employees on how often they engaged in behaviors like voicing suggestions and trying to improve procedures. Leader extraversion was rated by the store's employees. The key performance metric was the profitability of each store over a seven-week period.
*   **Results:** The study found a significant interaction between leader extraversion and employee proactivity. When employees demonstrated low levels of proactivity, stores led by extraverted managers were more profitable. However, this relationship reversed when employees were highly proactive; in this context, stores led by less extraverted (more introverted) managers achieved higher profits. This supported the hypothesis that the effectiveness of extraverted leadership is contingent on the nature of the followers.

### Study 2: Laboratory Experiment
*   **Methods:** To establish causality and replicate the findings, a second study was conducted in a laboratory setting. 163 college students were assigned to 56 groups. Participants were randomly assigned to roles as leaders or followers. Leaders were instructed to act in either an extraverted manner (e.g., be talkative, energetic) or an introverted one (e.g., be quiet, reserved). Follower groups were similarly primed to behave either passively or proactively during a t-shirt folding task. The primary outcome was the number of t-shirts the group folded in ten minutes.
*   **Results:** The laboratory experiment constructively replicated the findings from the field study. When follower groups were passive, groups with extraverted leaders performed significantly better. When follower groups were proactive—offering suggestions for a more efficient process—the groups with introverted leaders performed significantly better. The analysis showed that proactive groups felt their leader was less receptive to their input when the leader was acting extraverted, which undermined the group's performance.

### General Discussion
The authors conclude that their findings challenge the universal assumption of the extraverted leadership advantage. The core insight is that the value of an extraverted or introverted leadership style is dependent on the proactivity of the employees. The paper highlights an "irony of modern organizational life": many companies simultaneously encourage both extraverted leadership and employee proactivity, a combination which, according to this research, may lead to suboptimal performance. The practical implication is that organizations should focus on creating a complementary fit between leaders and employees. For teams expected to be proactive and innovative, a less extraverted leader may be more effective. Conversely, for teams performing routine tasks where passivity is the norm, an extraverted leader may be a better fit.
