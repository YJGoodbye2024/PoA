## 1. Official Abstract
"Hypothesized that information which is disproportionately available in memory will have a correspondingly disproportionate impact on evaluative judgments. In a mock jury decision, the availability of selected information in memory was varied according to the relative vividness of the evidence (prosecution evidence more vivid or defense evidence more vivid) and the favorableness of the defendant ("good guy" or "bad guy"). 54 university students judged the defendant's guilt immediately and after 48 hrs; in addition, they recalled the evidence after the 48-hr interval. Ss recalled more vivid evidence and more evidence that disagreed with the defendant's favorableness. Their judgments of apparent guilt paralleled their differential recall of the prosecution and defense evidence. These availability differences occurred only after the retention interval and did not affect judgments given immediately after reading the arguments. Results support a model in which judgments are based on the availability and the diagnosticity of the information."

### Detailed Interpretation

**Hypothesis and Background**
The 1980 study by Reyes, Thompson, and Bower was designed to test the "availability heuristic," a cognitive shortcut where people make judgments based on how easily examples come to mind. The central hypothesis was that information that is more "available" in memory—meaning it is more easily recalled—will have a greater influence on subsequent judgments. The study is situated within a broader examination of how construct accessibility and memory recall influence social judgments.

**Methodology**
To test this, the researchers created a mock jury trial scenario. Participants, who were 54 university students, were presented with a transcript of evidence from a court case. The experiment manipulated two key variables:
1.  **Vividness of Arguments:** The evidence for either the prosecution or the defense was presented in a more vivid, salient, and detailed manner, while the opposing arguments were presented in a more pallid, abstract style.
2.  **Defendant's Character:** The defendant was portrayed with background information that made him seem either like a "good guy" or a "bad guy" before the trial evidence was presented.

Participants were asked to judge the defendant's guilt at two different times: immediately after reading the evidence and again after a 48-hour delay. Following the second judgment, they were also asked to recall as many of the prosecution and defense arguments as they could.

**Key Findings**
The study produced several important findings:
*   **Delayed Effect of Availability:** The availability of arguments in memory did not significantly impact judgments made *immediately* after reading the evidence. However, after a 48-hour delay, a clear effect emerged.
*   **Recall and Judgment Correlation:** After 48 hours, participants' judgments of guilt were strongly correlated with the arguments they could recall. If they remembered more prosecution arguments, they were more likely to judge the defendant as guilty, and vice versa.
*   **Vividness Enhances Recall:** The vividness of the information played a crucial role in what was remembered. Participants recalled more of the arguments that were presented in the vivid style. This demonstrates a direct link between the vividness of information and its cognitive availability over time.
*   **Influence of Pre-existing Beliefs:** The "favorableness" of the defendant also influenced memory. Participants tended to better recall evidence that contradicted their initial impression of the defendant (e.g., remembering more prosecution arguments against a "good guy").

**Conclusion and Significance**
The results provided strong support for a model where judgments are not always made "on-line" as information is received. Instead, for delayed judgments, people often rely on the information they can retrieve from memory at that moment. The study demonstrates that the ease of recalling information (availability), which can be manipulated by factors like vividness, can create significant judgmental biases. This paper is frequently cited in research on persuasion, health communication, and legal decision-making to explain why vivid, emotionally resonant information can sometimes be more influential than pallid, statistical data, especially over time.
