## 1. Official Abstract
"Narratives of the self are not fundamentally possessions of the individual; rather they are products of social interchange-possessions of the socius. From this perspective, the individual has all but vanished into the world of relationship. In this chapter, we first trace the origins of this view to both the romanticist and modernist traditions. In both, the concept of the self is rendered problematic. We then turn to a constructionist perspective, in which the self is viewed as a social construction, a product of discourse. From this perspective, we have been especially concerned with the self-narrative, the individual's account of the relationship among self-relevant events across time. In developing a conception of narrative, we have found it useful to distinguish between those accounts that establish a goal state (stability, progressive, and regressive narratives), and those that do not. We have also been concerned with the functions of narrative in social life. As we argue, they are employed in relationships to sustain, enhance, or impede various actions. They are symbolic systems used for such social purposes as justification, criticism, and social solidification. Finally, we touch on a variety of intriguing questions invited by this perspective on the self."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on analyses from citing academic works.

The central thesis of Gergen and Gergen's 1988 paper, "Narrative and the Self as Relationship," is a radical departure from traditional psychological views of the self. The authors propose that the self, and the narratives we use to define it, are not inherent, individual properties but are instead social constructions that emerge from relationships. As they state in their abstract, "narratives of the self are not fundamentally possessions of the individual; rather they are products of the social interchange." In this view, the concept of an autonomous, independent self "has all but vanished into the world of relationship."

This perspective is rooted in social constructionism, which posits the self as a product of discourse. The Gergens argue that the stories we tell about ourselves—our "self-narratives"—are the primary means by which we become intelligible to both ourselves and others. These narratives are not free-form creations but follow established conventions, providing a "forestructure" for our identity.

The paper emphasizes the functional and action-oriented nature of these narratives. They are described as tools "employed in relationships to sustain, enhance, or impede various actions" and as "symbolic systems used for such social purposes as justification, criticism, and social solidification." A key element in making these narratives coherent is the plot, which organizes life events by establishing a goal. The authors identify three primary narrative forms that establish a goal:
*   **Stability narratives:** Maintaining a consistent state over time.
*   **Progressive narratives:** Depicting improvement or movement toward a goal.
*   **Regressive narratives:** Showing a decline or movement away from a goal.

Ultimately, Gergen and Gergen's work treats self-narratives as purposeful constructions used for self-presentation within a social context. The self is achieved through ongoing "dialogic processes," shifting the focus from internal cognitive structures to the dynamics of social interchange. This perspective views stories as the very medium through which people make themselves understandable, effectively replacing the metaphor of the mind as a machine with narrative as the root metaphor for psychological understanding.
