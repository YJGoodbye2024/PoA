## 1. Official Abstract
"The present paper examines validity of three proposed self-regulation predictors of school outcomes – Conscientiousness, Grit and Emotion Regulation Ability (ERA). In a sample of private high school students (N = 213) we measured these constructs along with indices of school success obtained from records (rule violating behavior, academic recognitions, honors, and GPA) and self-reported satisfaction with school. Regression analyses showed that after controlling for other Big Five traits, all school outcomes were significantly predicted by Conscientiousness and ERA, but not Grit. The discussion focuses on the importance of broad personality traits (Conscientiousness; measures of typical performance) and self-regulation abilities (ERA; measures of maximal performance) in predicting school success."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not directly accessible for a complete section-by-section parsing. However, based on detailed information from the abstract and multiple academic sources citing the paper, the following synopsis of its core structure and findings has been constructed.

### **Introduction**
The paper sets out to examine the validity of three psychological constructs as predictors of success in school: the Big Five personality trait of Conscientiousness, the popular concept of Grit (perseverance and passion for long-term goals), and Emotion Regulation Ability (ERA), a component of emotional intelligence. The authors hypothesized that both personality traits (like Conscientiousness) and abilities (like ERA) would independently predict school outcomes. The study aimed to compare the predictive power of these three self-regulation attributes on a variety of school success indicators.

### **Methods**
The study was conducted with a sample of 213 students at a private high school in the northeastern United States. The researchers measured the three key constructs—Conscientiousness, Grit, and Emotion Regulation Ability (ERA)—using established psychological instruments. To measure school success, they collected data from multiple sources: official school records provided objective indicators such as Grade Point Average (GPA), academic honors and recognitions, and instances of rule-violating behavior. Additionally, they gathered self-reported data from students regarding their satisfaction with school.

### **Results**
The core finding of the study emerged from regression analyses. After controlling for the other Big Five personality traits (besides Conscientiousness), the results showed that both Conscientiousness and Emotion Regulation Ability (ERA) were significant predictors of the various school success outcomes. In contrast, Grit did not significantly predict these outcomes once the other factors were accounted for. This indicates that students who were more organized, diligent, and capable of managing their emotions tended to have better academic and behavioral outcomes, but that a student's level of "grit" did not provide additional predictive power in this context.

### **Discussion/Conclusion**
In the discussion, the authors interpret their findings by highlighting the distinct roles of broad personality traits versus specific abilities in self-regulation. They frame Conscientiousness as a measure of "typical performance"—a person's general tendency to be diligent and self-disciplined. In contrast, they classify Emotion Regulation Ability (ERA) as a measure of "maximal performance"—what a person *can do* in terms of managing emotions when required. The conclusion underscores that both of these—the disposition to work hard (Conscientiousness) and the ability to manage emotions (ERA)—are important for predicting a student's success in school, more so than the trait of Grit in this particular study.
