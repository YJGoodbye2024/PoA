## 1. Official Abstract
"We examine predictions and judgments of confidence based on one-sided evidence. Some subjects saw arguments for only one side of a legal dispute while other subjects (called 'jurors') saw arguments for both sides. Subjects predicted the number of jurors who favored the plaintiff in each case. Subjects who saw only one side made predictions that were biased in favor of that side. Furthermore, they were more confident but generally less accurate than subjects who saw both sides. The results indicate that people do not compensate sufficiently for missing information even when it is painfully obvious that the information available to them is incomplete. A simple manipulation that required subjects to evaluate the relative strength of the opponent's side greatly reduced the tendency to under-weigh missing evidence."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not directly accessible for a section-by-section parsing. However, a detailed summary and abstract from a university repository allowed for the following analysis of the paper's core insights.

### **Objective and Method**
The study investigated how people make predictions and judge their own confidence when presented with incomplete, one-sided information. The experiment was centered around several legal disputes. Participants were divided into groups: some were shown arguments for only one side of a case (either the plaintiff or the defendant), while a control group of "jurors" was presented with the arguments from both sides. The task for the subjects was to predict the decisions of the jurors.

### **Key Findings**
The core results of the experiments can be summarized in three main points:
1.  **Bias from One-Sided Evidence:** Subjects who received evidence from only one side made predictions that were significantly biased in favor of that side. For instance, the mean prediction for the "Plaintiff-only" group was statistically higher than for the jury group, while the "Defendant-only" group's prediction was significantly lower.
2.  **Overconfidence with Less Information:** Participants who saw only one side of the case were more confident in their predictions than those who saw both sides. This increased confidence was demonstrated by their use of tighter uncertainty ranges for their predictions.
3.  **Decreased Accuracy:** Despite being more confident, the predictions made by subjects with one-sided information were generally less accurate than those made by subjects who had access to both sides of the argument.

### **Conclusion and Mitigation**
The authors concluded that people do not adequately adjust their judgments to account for missing information, even when they are fully aware that the evidence they have is incomplete. The biasing effect of seeing only one side was most significant when the arguments for both sides were balanced and judged to be highly persuasive. However, the study also found a simple and effective way to reduce this bias: a manipulation that prompted subjects to actively consider and evaluate the strength of the opposing side's arguments significantly diminished the tendency to underweight the missing evidence.
