## 1. Official Abstract

> In an effort to help people of all ages and at all career stages, Jeffrey Pfeffer, the Thomas D. Dee II Professor of Organizational Behavior at the Graduate School of Business, Stanford University, has written *Power: Why Some People Have It—and Others Don’t*. Based on his popular class at Stanford, the book is a guide for how to get and keep power and use it to build a better organization and a better-feeling life. Power, Pfeffer argues, is not a dirty word. Power is a force that can be used and harnessed for individual gain, but also for the good of the organization and society. Power is not something that can be learned from those in charge—their advice often puts a rosy spin on their ascent, focusing on what should have worked, rather than what actually did. Instead, Pfeffer reveals the true paths to power and career success. Iconoclastic and grounded in the realpolitik of human interaction, *Power* is an essential organizational survival manual and a new standard in the field of leadership and management.

### **Core Argument**

Jeffrey Pfeffer's central thesis in *Power* is that traditional notions of success—relying on job performance, likability, and the belief in a just world—are dangerously naive and insufficient for career advancement. He posits that power is the true mechanism for achieving goals, influencing outcomes, and even promoting personal health and longevity. The book serves as a pragmatic, and at times controversial, guide to understanding and navigating the real-world dynamics of organizational politics to build influence.

### **Key Themes and Strategies**

**1. Debunking the "Just World" Hypothesis:**
Pfeffer argues that a primary obstacle to gaining power is the "just-world phenomenon"—the belief that people get what they deserve. He contends that the world is not fair, and high performance alone does not guarantee advancement or influence. The leadership literature is often misleading, promoting idealistic qualities like modesty and authenticity, while ignoring the political savvy required to succeed.

**2. The Personal Qualities of the Powerful:**
Pfeffer identifies seven key personal attributes that facilitate the acquisition of power:
*   **Ambition:** The drive to achieve.
*   **Energy:** The capacity to work long and hard.
*   **Focus:** Concentrating on a specific goal or niche.
*   **Self-Knowledge:** An objective understanding of one's own strengths and weaknesses.
*   **Confidence:** Projecting an aura of power and competence, which becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy.
*   **Empathy with Others:** Understanding others' perspectives and needs to build alliances.
*   **Conflict Tolerance:** The willingness to engage in necessary confrontations.

**3. Strategic Actions for Building Power:**
The book outlines several actionable strategies for accumulating power:
*   **Get Noticed:** Performance is useless if those in power are not aware of it. Individuals must actively seek visibility.
*   **Build Networks:** Creating strong and effective social networks is crucial. This involves building relationships with those in power by helping them with their priorities, asking for assistance, and using flattery.
*   **Control Resources:** Power comes from controlling resources that others need, such as budget, information, or jobs.
*   **Act and Speak with Power:** One must project confidence and authority. Pfeffer suggests that if you act powerfully, others will perceive you as having power.
*   **Build a Favorable Reputation:** Cultivating a reputation for control and authority is a vital driver of advancement.

**4. The Necessity and Morality of Power:**
Pfeffer makes a strong case that seeking power is not just for personal gain but is essential for health and effectiveness. He cites studies showing that a lack of control over one's work environment leads to stress and higher mortality rates. Power is necessary to get things done, even for noble causes. When confronted with the idea that political maneuvering might harm an organization, Pfeffer's response is pragmatic, suggesting that organizations do not prioritize individual welfare and that power hierarchies are an unavoidable aspect of human nature.
