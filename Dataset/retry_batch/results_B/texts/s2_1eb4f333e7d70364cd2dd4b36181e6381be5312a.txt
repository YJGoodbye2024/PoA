## 1. Official Abstract
"News stories, advertising campaigns, and political propaganda often repeat misleading claims, increasing their persuasive power. Repeated statements feel easier to process, and thus truer, than new ones. Surprisingly, this illusory truth effect occurs even when claims contradict young adults' stored knowledge (e.g., repeating The fastest land animal is the leopard makes it more believable). In four experiments, we tackled this problem by prompting people to behave like "fact checkers." Focusing on accuracy at exposure (giving initial truth ratings) wiped out the illusion later, but only when participants held relevant knowledge. This selective benefit persisted over a delay. Our findings inform theories of how people evaluate truth and suggest practical strategies for coping in a "post-truth world."

### Introduction
The paper addresses the "illusory truth effect," a phenomenon where repeated statements are perceived as more truthful than new ones, simply because repetition makes them easier to process (more fluent). This effect is troublingly robust, occurring even for claims that are known to be false. The authors propose that the key to combating this illusion is to encourage "internal fact checking" at the moment of first exposure. They hypothesize that prompting individuals to evaluate a statement's truthfulness initially will activate their stored knowledge, thereby preventing the fluency of repetition from creating an illusion of truth for statements they know are false.

### Experiment 1
*   **Methods:** Participants were shown a series of true and false statements. One group was asked to rate how "interesting" each statement was (the standard condition, which does not focus on accuracy). The other group was asked to rate how "truthful" each statement was (the accuracy focus condition). Later, both groups rated the truthfulness of a larger set of statements, which included repeated items from the first phase and new items.
*   **Results:** The standard "interest rating" group showed a typical illusory truth effect; they rated repeated falsehoods as more true than new ones. In contrast, the "accuracy focus" group did not show an illusory truth effect for falsehoods they likely knew were false.
*   **Discussion:** The results suggest that an initial accuracy focus successfully prevented the illusory truth effect for known falsehoods, presumably by activating relevant knowledge during the initial exposure. However, the authors noted this could also be due to participants in the accuracy group spending more time on the task or being generally more skeptical.

### Experiment 2
*   **Methods:** To address the limitations of Experiment 1, this experiment included falsehoods that participants were unlikely to know. The hypothesis was that the accuracy focus would only prevent the illusory truth effect for "known" falsehoods, as participants would have no prior knowledge to activate for "unknown" items.
*   **Results:** As predicted, participants who initially rated truthfulness only showed an illusory truth effect for the *unknown* falsehoods. For falsehoods they already knew were false, the illusion was eliminated. The standard "interest rating" group showed the illusory truth effect for both known and unknown falsehoods.
*   **Discussion:** This experiment confirmed that the benefit of an accuracy focus is selective; it relies on the participant having relevant knowledge to draw upon. When no knowledge is available, people fall back on the feeling of fluency, and the illusory truth effect returns.

### Experiment 3
*   **Methods:** This experiment tested whether the protective effect of an accuracy focus would persist over time. The procedure was similar to the previous experiments, but the final truth-rating phase occurred after a one-week delay.
*   **Results:** Even after a week, the results held. The accuracy focus group showed no illusory truth effect for known falsehoods, while the standard group did.
*   **Discussion:** The findings demonstrate that the benefits of an initial accuracy check are durable, offering a lasting defense against the illusory truth effect for previously known information.

### Experiment 4
*   **Methods:** The final experiment investigated a more subtle way to encourage an accuracy mindset. Participants were told they were helping to pre-test trivia questions for a future study. One group was simply asked to read the statements. Another group was asked to answer the questions with a "true" or "false" response.
*   **Results:** Simply asking participants to answer the questions as true or false was enough to engage an accuracy mindset and eliminate the illusory truth effect for known falsehoods.
*   **Discussion:** This suggests that even a subtle prompt to evaluate truth, rather than just passively receive information, is an effective strategy. It encourages a "fact-checker" mindset that leverages existing knowledge to fend off the persuasive power of repetition.

### General Discussion
The authors conclude that the illusory truth effect is not inevitable. When people are prompted to consider the accuracy of a statement upon first encountering it, they can successfully activate their existing knowledge to override the misleading feeling of fluency that comes with repetition. This "selective benefit" only works when a person has relevant knowledge stored in memory. The research provides a practical strategy for navigating environments saturated with misinformation: adopting an active, evaluative mindset rather than passively consuming information.
