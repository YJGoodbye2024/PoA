## 1. Official Abstract
This work provides an analysis of the basic cognitive components of envy. In particular, the roles played by the envious party's social comparison with, and ill will against, the better off are emphasised. The ill will component is characterised by the envier's ultimate goal or wish that the envied suffer some harm, and is distinguished from resentment and sense of injustice, which have often been considered part of envy. The reprehensible nature of envy is discussed, and traced back to the analysis of its components. Finally, we explore both points of overlap and distinguishing features between envy and other emotions such as jealousy or emulation, and make a few general remarks, pointing to the necessity of overcoming conceptual looseness in the notion of envy.

### Introduction
The authors begin by noting that envy, despite being a widespread and powerful emotion, has been relatively neglected in psychological studies compared to feelings like anger, guilt, or even jealousy. They argue that the concept of envy is often used loosely and propose a more precise analysis of its cognitive components. The central aim is to dissect the "cognitive anatomy" of envy, focusing on two core ingredients: social comparison and ill will.

### The Cognitive Anatomy of Envy
This section deconstructs the cognitive foundations of the emotion. The authors posit that envy is rooted in a frustrating social comparison. The "envious" person (A) perceives that another person (B) possesses a good (G) that A desires but lacks. Crucially, the good is self-relevant to A, meaning its possession is important to A's self-esteem.

A key argument is that the core pain of envy does not stem from the mere lack of the good, but from the "perceived inferiority to the envied." This feeling of inferiority is what leads to the second major component: ill will. Miceli and Castelfranchi define ill will as the ultimate goal for the envied person to suffer harm or lose their advantage. This is a destructive desire aimed at leveling the envier's standing by bringing the other down, not by lifting oneself up. They argue that this "ill will" is distinct from a sense of injustice or resentment, which they believe are not essential components of "envy proper." For instance, they argue that envy can be even more intense when the envied person's advantage is perceived as deserved, as this can make the envier's own inferiority feel more salient and distressing.

### The Reprehensible Nature of Envy
The authors explore why envy is almost universally condemned. They trace this condemnation back to its core components. The envious person not only suffers from a perceived inferiority but also harbors a secret, hostile wish for another's misfortune. This ill will is socially undesirable because it is "against the other's good for the sake of it" and offers no constructive path forward. Furthermore, envy is seen as self-damaging; the envious person is trapped in a state of "impotent hostility," focusing on the other's success rather than on their own potential for improvement. The act of admitting envy is an admission of both inferiority and malevolence, explaining why it is so often concealed.

### Distinguishing Envy from Other States
This section clarifies the boundaries between envy and related emotions to address conceptual confusion.
*   **Resentment and Injustice:** The authors argue that while envy can co-occur with a sense of injustice, they are not the same. A sense of injustice implies a belief that one has been wronged or that some norm has been violated. Envy, in its purest form, does not require this; it simply requires the painful recognition of another's superior standing.
*   **Jealousy:** The paper distinguishes envy from jealousy by framing envy as a two-person relationship (the envier and the envied) centered on a desired good, whereas jealousy is a triadic relationship (the self, a loved one, and a rival) centered on the threatened loss of affection or a valued relationship. Envy is about wanting what someone else *has*, while jealousy is about fearing losing what one *already has*.
*   **Coveting and Emulation:** Coveting is simply the desire for a good, without the comparative and hostile dimensions of envy. Emulation, on the other hand, is a positive, non-hostile response to another's success. It involves being inspired by the other's achievement to improve oneself. The authors classify this as a separate phenomenon from "envy proper," which they define by its malicious component.

### General Discussion and Conclusion
Miceli and Castelfranchi conclude by reiterating the need for a precise, component-based understanding of emotions. They argue that "envy proper" is characterized by the combination of a painful self-evaluation stemming from social comparison and a core goal of ill will against the advantaged person. They suggest that other related states, often conflated with envy (like benign envy, emulation, or resentment), should be distinguished as separate emotional phenomena. Their analysis aims to provide a clearer foundation for future empirical research into the causes, consequences, and distinct nature of the envious mind.
