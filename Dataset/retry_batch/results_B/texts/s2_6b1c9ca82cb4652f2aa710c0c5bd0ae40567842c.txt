## 1. Official Abstract
"In Study 1, college students' preferences for different brands of strawberry jams were compared with experts' ratings of the jams. Students who analyzed why they felt the way they did agreed less with the experts than students who did not. In Study 2, college students' preferences for college courses were compared with expert opinion. Some students were asked to analyze reasons; others were asked to evaluate all attributes of all courses. Both kinds of introspection caused people to make choices that, compared with control subjects', corresponded less with expert opinion. Analyzing reasons can focus people's attention on nonoptimal criteria, causing them to base their subsequent choices on these criteria. Evaluating multiple attributes can moderate people's judgments, causing them to discriminate less between the different alternatives."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible. The following is a synopsis organized by the paper's original sections.

### Introduction
The authors challenge the conventional wisdom, famously articulated by figures like Benjamin Franklin, that careful reflection and listing "pros" and "cons" lead to better decisions. They propose that introspection, particularly analyzing the reasons for one's feelings, can be disruptive and may lead to choices that are not optimal. Previous research has indicated that people often lack direct introspective access to higher-order cognitive processes and that analyzing reasons can change attitudes and reduce attitude-behavior consistency. The paper hypothesizes that when people analyze their reasons, they focus on criteria that are easy to verbalize and seem like plausible justifications, even if these are not the factors that truly inform their initial, often adaptive, preferences. This shift in focus can lead to a change in preference and, consequently, to lower-quality decisions. The authors set out to test whether the attitude changes resulting from analyzing reasons are detrimental to the decision-making process.

### Study 1: Jam Preferences
*   **Method:** 49 undergraduate psychology students were asked to rate their liking for five different brands of strawberry jam. These jams had been previously rated by trained sensory experts for *Consumer Reports*, providing an "expert" benchmark. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups. The "reasons analysis" group was instructed to "analyze why you feel the way you do about each" and write down their reasons before rating the jams. The control group was told they would be rating jams as part of a consumer research study but were not asked to analyze their reasons.
*   **Results and Discussion:** The preferences of the control group participants correlated significantly more with the expert ratings than the preferences of the participants in the "reasons analysis" group. Introspection caused participants to alter their preferences in a way that made them less aligned with expert opinion. The authors argue that thinking about why they liked the jams caused participants to focus on attributes that were less important than the ones the experts considered, leading them to make less-than-optimal judgments.

### Study 2: Course Selection
*   **Method:** The second study aimed to replicate and extend the findings using a more consequential decision: selecting a college course. Participants were 103 students who needed to choose a sophomore-level psychology course for the following semester. They were divided into three groups: a control group, a "reasons analysis" group (similar to Study 1), and an "all attributes" group that was asked to consider and rate the importance of a comprehensive list of attributes for all available courses. The "expert opinion" in this case was derived from the average ratings of a large group of psychology majors who had previously taken the courses.
*   **Results and Discussion:** Both forms of introspection led to choices that corresponded less with expert opinion compared to the control group. Participants in the "reasons analysis" group made choices that were less aligned with the expert ratings. Participants in the "all attributes" evaluation group also showed a lower correspondence with expert opinion, suggesting that this type of detailed evaluation can moderate judgments, making the alternatives harder to distinguish from one another. The study also tracked which courses the students actually enrolled in later, finding that the control group's choices were more predictive of their actual enrollment in highly-rated courses.

### General Discussion
The authors conclude that introspection is not always beneficial and can, under certain circumstances, lead to worse outcomes. When people analyze the reasons for their preferences, they tend to focus on criteria that are accessible and easy to verbalize, which may not be the criteria that guided their initial, gut feelings. This can cause a temporary shift in attitude, leading them to make choices they might later regret. The research suggests that when the reasons for a preference are hard to articulate (e.g., for affectively-based attitudes), thinking about them can disrupt the decision-making process. The authors note that while introspection can sometimes be helpful, people may be better off trusting their initial feelings when making decisions in domains where they lack expertise or where the underlying reasons for a preference are not easily verbalized.
