## 1. Official Abstract
> Research and theoretical developments on the theory of cognitive dissonance are reviewed. After considering the self-consistency, self-affirmation, and aversive consequences revisions, the authors review research that has challenged each of the revisions and that supports the original version of the theory. Then, the authors review the action-based model of dissonance, which accepts the original theory's proposal that a sufficient cognitive inconsistency causes dissonance and extends the original theory by proposing why cognitive inconsistency prompts dissonance. Finally, the authors present results from experiments examining predictions derived from the action-based model and neural processes involved in dissonance reduction.

Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on the abstract and analysis from citing academic sources.

The 2007 paper, "Cognitive Dissonance Theory After 50 Years of Development," by Eddie Harmon-Jones and Cindy Harmon-Jones serves as a comprehensive review and a theoretical advancement of one of social psychology's most pivotal theories. The authors' central mission is twofold: to defend the foundational principles of Leon Festinger's original theory against popular revisions and to introduce their "action-based model" as a crucial extension that explains the underlying motivational source of dissonance.

**Critique of Revisions and Defense of the Original Theory**

The paper systematically examines three major revisions that attempted to reinterpret the cause of dissonance:
1.  **Self-Consistency Revision:** This view posits that dissonance arises not from any simple cognitive conflict, but from a conflict between a behavior and one's self-concept (e.g., "I am a good person, but I did a bad thing").
2.  **Self-Affirmation Theory:** This revision proposes that the negative feeling is a threat to one's sense of moral and adaptive integrity. Dissonance reduction, therefore, is a process of reaffirming the adequacy of the self.
3.  **Aversive Consequences Model (The "New Look"):** This model argues that dissonance is only triggered when a person feels responsible for bringing about a foreseeable negative outcome.

Harmon-Jones and Harmon-Jones review empirical evidence that challenges the necessity of these conditions. They argue that while factors like self-concept or aversive consequences can *intensify* the magnitude of dissonance, they are not essential preconditions for it to occur. The paper asserts that the original, more parsimonious formulation by Festinger—that a sufficient inconsistency between any two cognitions is enough to cause the uncomfortable state of dissonance—remains the most accurate and encompassing explanation.

**The Action-Based Model of Dissonance**

The paper's primary theoretical contribution is the formal presentation of the **action-based model**. This model accepts Festinger's premise that cognitive inconsistency is the cause of dissonance but goes a step further by asking *why* this inconsistency is psychologically uncomfortable.

The model's core proposition is that cognitions ultimately serve to guide action. When cognitions that have clear implications for behavior are in conflict, they interfere with the ability to engage in effective and unconflicted action. This potential for behavioral paralysis or interference is what generates the aversive, motivating state of dissonance. The process of dissonance reduction—changing beliefs or attitudes to restore consistency—is therefore an adaptive mechanism that serves to facilitate decisive action.

To support this model, the authors present findings from their own experiments, which include examinations of neural processes (such as electrodermal activity and frontal cortical activation) involved in the experience and reduction of dissonance.

In summary, the paper argues for a return to the core principles of dissonance theory while simultaneously extending it. It refutes the necessity of the major "self" and "consequence" based revisions and reframes cognitive dissonance as a fundamental motivational state that arises from the need to produce effective and unconflicted behavior.
