## 1. Official Abstract
Psychologists have worried about the distortions introduced into standardized personality measures by social desirability bias. Survey researchers have had similar concerns about the accuracy of survey reports about such topics as illicit drug use, abortion, and sexual behavior. The article reviews the research done by survey methodologists on reporting errors in surveys on sensitive topics, noting parallels and differences from the psychological literature on social desirability. The findings from the survey studies suggest that misreporting about sensitive topics is quite common and that it is largely situational. The extent of misreporting depends on whether the respondent has anything embarrassing to report and on the design features of the survey. The survey evidence also indicates that misreporting on sensitive topics is a more or less motivated process in which respondents edit the information they report to avoid embarrassing themselves in the presence of an interviewer or to avoid repercussions from third parties.

(Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17723033/)

### Detailed Interpretation

The 2007 paper "Sensitive questions in surveys" by Roger Tourangeau and Ting Yan, published in *Psychological Bulletin*, is a highly influential review that bridges the gap between psychological research on social desirability bias and survey methodology research on reporting errors for sensitive topics. The core argument of the paper is that misreporting on sensitive subjects is a common, situational, and motivated process where respondents actively edit their answers to avoid embarrassment or other negative consequences.

**Defining Sensitive Questions:**
Tourangeau and Yan define question sensitivity through three main features:
1.  **Intrusiveness:** The question is perceived as intrusive or inappropriate to discuss, such as topics related to personal income or religion.
2.  **Threat of Disclosure:** The respondent fears the consequences of their answer being revealed to third parties. For example, a teenager admitting to alcohol use might not fear telling a researcher but would fear their parents overhearing.
3.  **Social Undesirability:** The question asks the respondent to admit to a behavior or attitude that violates social norms, such as illicit drug use, criminal acts, or failing to vote.

The authors emphasize that sensitivity is not inherent to a topic but is contextual and depends on the respondent's perspective. A question about voting, for instance, is not sensitive for someone who actually voted.

**Consequences and Factors of Misreporting:**
The paper establishes that asking sensitive questions can lead to higher nonresponse rates and significant measurement errors. This misreporting is often a deliberate act of "editing" by the respondent. The likelihood and degree of this editing are influenced by several factors:
*   **Mode of Data Collection:** Self-administered questionnaires, especially computerized ones, tend to yield higher reports of socially undesirable behaviors compared to interviewer-administered surveys because they enhance privacy and reduce the fear of judgment.
*   **Question Design and Context:** The authors review various techniques designed to mitigate social desirability bias. These include using "forgiving wording" to normalize a sensitive behavior, providing anonymity assurances, and employing specialized methods like the Randomized Response Technique (RRT) and the Item Count Technique (ICT). The paper notes that while self-administration is generally effective, the record for techniques like RRT and ICT is more mixed.

**Synthesis of Psychology and Survey Research:**
A key contribution of the paper is its synthesis of two research traditions. While psychologists have often treated social desirability as a stable personality trait, survey methodologists tend to view it as a situational phenomenon. Tourangeau and Yan argue that misreporting is a motivated process influenced by the immediate survey context (the "questioning situation") rather than just a respondent's personality. They conclude that reporting errors are largely situational, depending on both the respondent's actual circumstances and specific features of the survey design. This perspective has been influential in guiding subsequent research on how to best design surveys to collect valid data on sensitive topics.
