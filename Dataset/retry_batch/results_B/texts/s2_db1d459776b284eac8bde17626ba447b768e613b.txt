## 1. Official
**_C. Carver, Jennifer K. Connor-smith_** Abstract

> Personality psychology addresses views of human nature and individual differences. Biological and goal-based views of human nature provide an especially useful basis for construing coping; the five-factor model of traits adds a useful set of individual differences. Coping—responses to adversity and to the distress that results—is categorized in many ways. Meta-analyses link optimism, extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness to more engagement coping; neuroticism to more disengagement coping; and optimism, conscientiousness, and agreeableness to less disengagement coping. Relations of traits to specific coping responses reveal a more nuanced picture. Several moderators of these associations also emerge: age, stressor severity, and temporal proximity between the coping activity and the coping report. Personality and coping play both independent and interactive roles in influencing physical and mental health. Recommendations are presented for ways future research can expand on the growing understanding of how personality and coping shape adjustment to stress.

### Detailed Interpretation

Carver and Connor-Smith's 2010 review, "Personality and Coping," is a significant paper that synthesizes the relationship between personality traits and the strategies individuals use to manage stress. The paper's core argument is that personality is a key predictor of coping responses, which in turn affects mental and physical health outcomes. The analysis is primarily structured around the Five-Factor Model of personality (Neuroticism, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, and Openness to Experience) and the trait of Optimism.

A central distinction made in the paper is between **engagement coping** (also called approach coping) and **disengagement coping** (or avoidance). Engagement strategies are aimed at actively dealing with a stressor or the resulting emotions, and include problem-solving, seeking social support, cognitive restructuring, and positive reappraisal. Disengagement strategies are aimed at avoiding the stressor or related feelings and include denial, wishful thinking, and venting.

The paper's key findings, as highlighted by other researchers, are the specific links between personality traits and these coping categories:

*   **Neuroticism:** This trait is strongly associated with negative feelings and vulnerability to stress. Individuals high in neuroticism tend to rely on disengagement or emotion-focused coping, such as denial, avoidance, and venting, which may offer temporary relief but prevent active problem-solving.
*   **Extraversion:** Characterized by sociability, assertiveness, and positive emotions, extraversion is linked to engagement coping. Specifically, extraverts are more likely to seek instrumental social support (advice and information) and use problem-solving strategies.
*   **Conscientiousness:** This trait involves discipline, planning, and goal-directed behavior. Unsurprisingly, it is a strong predictor of engagement coping, particularly through active, task-oriented strategies like planning, logic, and positive reappraisal. Conscientiousness is also negatively associated with disengagement coping.
*   **Agreeableness:** Agreeable individuals, who are often empathetic and cooperative, also tend to use engagement coping. Their strategies often involve seeking social support and using positive reappraisal. The trait is linked with inhibiting negative feelings and is negatively correlated with overt emotional expression as a coping mechanism.
*   **Openness to Experience:** This trait, which involves imagination and a willingness to try new things, is associated with successful adaptation. People high in openness are more likely to use engagement strategies like positive reappraisal, humor, and problem-solving. However, they may also utilize some disengagement strategies like wishful thinking.
*   **Optimism:** The paper treats optimism as a distinct and important individual difference. Reflecting a general confidence in positive future outcomes, optimism is strongly linked to engagement coping and is a key predictor of adaptive responses to stress.

In summary, Carver and Connor-Smith's review establishes that personality traits are not merely descriptive labels but are predictive of how individuals will respond to adversity. The paper concludes that traits like extraversion, conscientiousness, agreeableness, and openness generally predict more adaptive, problem-focused coping, while neuroticism predicts maladaptive, avoidance-based coping.
