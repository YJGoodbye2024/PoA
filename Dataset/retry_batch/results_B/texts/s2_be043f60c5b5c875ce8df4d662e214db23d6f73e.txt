## 1. Official Abstract
Social touch is a powerful force in human development, shaping social reward, attachment, cognitive, communication, and emotional regulation from infancy and throughout life. In this review, we consider the question of how social touch is defined from both bottom-up and top-down perspectives. In the former category, there is a clear role for the C-touch (CT) system, which constitutes a unique submodality that mediates affective touch and contrasts with discriminative touch. Top-down factors such as culture, personal relationships, setting, gender, and other contextual influences are also important in defining and interpreting social touch. The critical role of social touch throughout the lifespan is considered, with special attention to infancy and young childhood, a time during which social touch and its neural, behavioral, and physiological contingencies contribute to reinforcement-based learning and impact a variety of developmental trajectories. Finally, the role of social touch in an example of disordered development -autism spectrum disorder-is reviewed.

This review paper, "Social touch and human development," synthesizes the critical role of social touch across the human lifespan, with a particular focus on early development. The authors, Cascio, Moore, and McGlone, argue that social touch is a fundamental force that influences social, cognitive, and emotional development. The analysis is structured around defining social touch through two lenses: the neurophysiological mechanisms ("bottom-up") and the contextual, interpretive factors ("top-down").

**Bottom-Up and Top-Down Perspectives on Social Touch**
The core of the paper's argument lies in its dual approach to defining social touch.

*   **Bottom-Up (Neurophysiological) View:** The paper highlights the C-touch (CT) afferent system as a key neurological basis for affective, or pleasant, touch. This system consists of specialized, unmyelinated nerve fibers that respond optimally to gentle, slow stroking at skin temperature. This contrasts with "discriminative touch," which is involved in identifying textures and shapes. The CT system is considered a "social touch system" because its activation is linked to social rewards and the release of hormones like oxytocin, which facilitates social bonding. Research shows that the CT system is functional from birth, and parents instinctively caress infants at velocities optimal for activating these fibers.

*   **Top-Down (Contextual) View:** The authors stress that the interpretation of touch is not solely dependent on nerve signals. It is heavily modulated by top-down factors, including the cultural context, the relationship between individuals, the setting, and gender. For instance, while the speed of a caress is a key physical property, in adults, the identity of the person giving the touch can be more important in determining if it is perceived as pleasant.

**The Role of Social Touch in Development**
The paper emphasizes that social touch is crucial from infancy for reinforcement-based learning and the development of healthy social-emotional skills.

*   **Infancy and Childhood:** During this period, affective touch is a "key ingredient" for forming secure parental attachments and familial bonds. It helps infants and children develop healthy neural reward systems by teaching them to respond to social cues. The paper notes that a lack of positive touch in early life is associated with significant negative consequences, including impaired growth, cognitive deficits, and a higher risk for sensory processing disorders and autism spectrum disorder (ASD).

*   **Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD):** The paper concludes by specifically reviewing the role of social touch in ASD. It suggests that atypical processing of social touch is a feature of the disorder. Other research confirms that brain mechanisms for processing affective touch can be atypical in individuals with autism. This highlights the importance of understanding the neurobiological underpinnings of touch for developing potential therapeutic approaches.

In essence, the paper serves as an introduction to a special issue on the topic, acknowledging the breadth of research, from molecular studies of skin receptors to the social modulation of touch pleasantness. It frames social touch as a complex interplay between innate neurobiology and learned social context, establishing it as a vital area of study in developmental cognitive neuroscience.
