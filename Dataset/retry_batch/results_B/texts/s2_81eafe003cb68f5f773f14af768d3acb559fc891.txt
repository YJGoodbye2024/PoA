## 1. Official Abstract
"Although emotional expressivity figures prominently in several theories of psychological and physical functioning, limitations of currently available measurement techniques impede precise and economical testing of these theories. The 17-item Emotional Expressivity Scale (EES) was designed as a self-report measure of the extent to which people outwardly display their emotions. Reliability studies showed the EES to be an internally consistent and stable individual-difference measure. Validational studies established initial convergent and discriminant validities, a moderate relationship between self-rated and other-rated expression, and correspondence between self-report and laboratory-measured expressiveness using both college student and community populations. The potential for the EES to promote and integrate findings across diverse areas of research is discussed."

### Detailed Interpretation

The 1994 paper by Kring, Smith, and Neale is a foundational study in the field of personality and social psychology, primarily focused on the creation and validation of a new tool: the Emotional Expressivity Scale (EES). The central purpose of the paper was to address a gap in psychological measurement by providing a reliable, self-report instrument to quantify "dispositional expressiveness"—the extent to which individuals outwardly display their emotions, regardless of the specific emotion being felt.

**Development and Structure of the EES:**
The authors defined emotional expressiveness as the outward display of emotions through verbal and nonverbal behaviors. They developed the EES as a 17-item questionnaire where individuals self-report their expressive tendencies. The scale was designed to capture a general expressivity trait rather than focusing on specific emotions, based on the principle that a single scale should measure a single, unified construct. The items are in a Likert-format and assess the expression of both positive and negative emotions, as well as the expression of intimacy.

**Key Findings from Validation Studies:**
The core of the paper is its series of validation studies, which established the EES as a robust measurement tool.
*   **Reliability:** The research demonstrated that the EES is internally consistent (the items correlate well with each other) and stable over time, meaning it measures a consistent individual trait.
*   **Convergent and Discriminant Validity:** The validation studies successfully showed that the EES correlates with other, similar measures (convergent validity) while remaining distinct from unrelated psychological constructs (discriminant validity).
*   **Self-Report vs. Objective Measures:** A crucial finding was the correspondence between individuals' self-reported expressivity on the EES and their actual expressive behavior as measured in a laboratory setting. This confirmed that the self-report scale is a valid proxy for observable behavior. The paper also found a moderate relationship between how people rate their own expressiveness and how others rate them.

**Impact and Significance:**
The development of the EES has had a significant impact on subsequent research. By providing a simple and validated tool, Kring et al. enabled researchers across diverse fields—including health psychology, psychopathology, and social psychology—to investigate the role of emotional expression. For instance, later research using the EES has linked higher levels of emotional expressivity to greater pleasure from social interactions and positive personality traits like extraversion and openness to experience. Conversely, lower expressivity has been associated with conditions such as social anhedonia, depression, and schizophrenia. The paper is frequently cited as the basis for defining and measuring emotional expressivity in academic studies.
