### **Introduction: Quantifying Risk and Benefit**
The paper opens by stating that evaluating technological solutions to societal problems requires understanding the relationship between performance and cost, as well as the relationship between social benefit and justified social cost. Starr argues that while engineering analyses of performance versus monetary cost are common, quantitative analyses of social value are not. He posits, "implicit in every nonarbitrary national decision on the use of technology is a trade-off of societal benefits and societal costs." The article's goal is to establish a quantitative measure for one element of this trade-off: accidental deaths resulting from technological developments. The approach is based on two assumptions:
1.  "The first is that historical records are adequate for revealing consistent patterns of social acceptability (what the author calls social preferences)."
2.  "The second assumption is that such historically revealed social preferences and costs are sufficiently enduring to permit their use for predictive purposes."

### **Methodology: Revealed Preferences**
Starr's central method is to analyze historical data to determine the level of risk society has previously found acceptable. He distinguishes between "voluntary" and "involuntary" exposure to risk, a key distinction in his analysis. The study quantifies risk as the "fatalities per person-hour of exposure," and benefit is measured in terms of the average monetary contribution of the activity to an individual's income or its equivalent. By plotting risk versus benefit for various activities, Starr seeks to identify underlying patterns of social acceptance.

### **Analysis of Voluntary and Involuntary Risk**
The analysis reveals a major finding: society is willing to accept "voluntary" risks at a level roughly 1,000 times greater than "involuntary" risks for the same level of benefit.
*   **Voluntary Risks:** Activities like skiing, hunting, smoking, and general aviation are considered voluntary. For these, the accepted risk is directly proportional to the perceived benefit. Starr notes, "we are loath to let others do unto us what we happily do to ourselves."
*   **Involuntary Risks:** These are risks imposed on individuals without their direct consent, such as those from electric power generation. The study finds that the acceptability of these risks is inversely related to the number of people exposed to them.

### **Key Findings and "Laws" of Social Acceptance**
From the data, Starr derives several "imitative 'laws' of social acceptance":
1.  The acceptable risk for voluntary activities is approximately proportional to the cube of the perceived benefits.
2.  The public seems willing to accept involuntary risks that are roughly 1,000 times less than those they would take on voluntarily.
3.  The level of acceptable risk for an activity is inversely related to the number of people participating in it.
4.  The risk level associated with "an old and familiar risk" is considered a baseline for judging new risks.
5.  Starr observes that the risk of death from disease (approximately 10⁻⁶ fatalities per person-hour) appears to be a psychological benchmark for the acceptability of involuntary risks. Society seems to accept involuntary risks at this level or lower.

### **Conclusion: A Framework for Decision-Making**
Starr concludes that this empirical approach, based on historical "revealed preferences," can provide a systematic framework for assessing the social acceptability of new technologies. He argues that by understanding the quantitative relationships between risk and social benefit, society can "deliberately guide and regulate technological developments so as to achieve maximum social benefit at minimum social cost." The paper suggests that this method offers a way to make consistent, data-informed decisions about public safety and technological progress, moving beyond trial-and-error.
