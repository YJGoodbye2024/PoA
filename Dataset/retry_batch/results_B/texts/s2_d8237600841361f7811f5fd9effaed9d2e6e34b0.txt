## 1. Official Abstract
"A model is proposed for the description of rational choice by organisms of limited computational ability."

This model is contrasted with traditional economic theory, which postulates an "economic man" who is fully rational and possesses complete knowledge of the aspects relevant to his environment. The paper aims to construct a definition of "rational choice" that is more aligned with the actual decision-making processes observed in human behavior.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### Introduction
Simon introduces the concept of the "economic man" from traditional economic theory, a figure who is assumed to be completely rational. This traditional model imputes to the economic man a vast and clear knowledge of his environment. Simon argues that this model is unrealistic because it doesn't account for the limitations of the decision-maker. He proposes to explore models of rational behavior that consider the "internal" constraints of the organism (the decision-maker) itself, not just the "external" environmental constraints. The paper suggests that a more realistic model would describe economic choice in a way that is consistent with the access to information and the computational capacities that organisms, including humans, actually possess.

### I. Some General Features of Rational Choice
This section outlines the foundational "givens" or constraints that shape models of rational choice. These typically include:
1.  The set of all possible alternatives for choice.
2.  The outcomes or "pay-offs" associated with each alternative.
3.  The preference ordering among these pay-offs.

Simon points out that the selection of which constraints are considered fixed and which are variables that the organism can control is a critical, often implicit, assumption in any model of rationality. He argues that a behavioral model must be concerned with the *procedures* by which an organism with limited computational power and information finds an appropriate course of action.

### II. The Essential Simplifications
To create a workable model of choice for an organism with limited capabilities, Simon introduces several key simplifications. The central idea is that the decision-maker does not consider all possible alternatives but instead searches for a "satisfactory" one. This contrasts with the "optimizing" behavior of the classical "economic man."

The core components of this simplified model are:
*   **Limited Range of Alternatives:** Instead of an infinite or vast set of choices, the model assumes the organism deals with a much smaller, simplified "model of reality."
*   **Satisficing vs. Optimizing:** The organism does not seek the single best (optimal) solution. Instead, it establishes an "aspiration level" and searches for an alternative that meets or exceeds this level. The first satisfactory alternative found is often the one chosen.
*   **Sequential Search:** Alternatives are often discovered and examined sequentially, not all at once. The process stops once a satisfactory option is identified.

Simon illustrates this with a simple model where pay-offs are categorized as either "satisfactory" (1) or "unsatisfactory" (0). The organism's goal is simply to find an alternative with a pay-off of 1.

### III. Existence and Uniqueness of Solutions
In this section, Simon addresses the procedural aspects of his model. Unlike global models of rational choice that evaluate all alternatives to find a unique, optimal solution, the behavioral model allows for procedures where alternatives are examined sequentially. This means a unique solution is not guaranteed. The decision-making process might stop as soon as a "good enough" solution is found. Simon suggests adding supplementary rules to the model to explain how a unique choice is made in practice, acknowledging that the mechanism determining the order of search is a crucial factor.

### IV. Further Comments on Dynamics
Simon extends the model into a dynamic context by considering how the "aspiration level" itself can change. He proposes that the aspiration level is not static.
*   If the organism finds it easy to discover satisfactory alternatives, its aspiration level will tend to rise.
*   If it finds it difficult to discover satisfactory alternatives, its aspiration level will tend to fall.

This dynamic adjustment allows the organism to adapt its definition of "satisfactory" based on the structure of its environment and its experience.

### V. Conclusion
Simon concludes by reiterating that the aim of the paper was to construct a model of rational choice that is more closely based on the actual decision-making processes of organisms. He contrasts his "satisficing" model, which involves searching for a good-enough option from a limited set of alternatives, with the classical model of an omniscient "economic man" seeking an optimal solution from a complete set of alternatives. He suggests that this behavioral approach has both descriptive value (in explaining real-world behavior) and normative value (in providing a framework for rational choice under realistic constraints).
