### Detailed Interpretation

**Background and Motivation**

*The effects of assimilation and contrast in judgments of clinical materials* (1957) by Donald T. Campbell, William A. Hunt, and Nan A. Lewis is a foundational study in the psychology of clinical judgment. Its primary goal was to demonstrate that the evaluation of clinical materials is not absolute but is significantly influenced by the context in which they are presented. The impetus for this research came from the failure of a previous study by Arnhoff to show these predicted "anchoring effects" in the judgment of responses from schizophrenic patients. Campbell and his colleagues hypothesized that this failure was not due to the absence of the effect, but rather an issue with the experimental design.

**Experimental Design and Core Concepts**

To successfully elicit the desired phenomena, the researchers implemented a design that featured a "drastic change in stimulus-context." The core of the experiment involved having participants—likely clinicians or psychology students—rate clinical materials for their degree of psychopathology. The key manipulation was the context provided by other materials being rated in the same session.

The study was designed to produce two distinct, opposing outcomes:

1.  **Assimilation Effect:** This occurs when the judgment of a target case is pulled *toward* the contextual anchor. For example, when a moderately pathological case is presented within a series of severely pathological cases, it might be rated as more severe than it would be in isolation.
2.  **Contrast Effect:** This is the opposite phenomenon, where the judgment of a target case is pushed *away* from the contextual anchor. For instance, when the same moderately pathological case is presented within a series of very mild or normal cases, it is judged as being even more pathological by comparison.

Campbell, Hunt, and Lewis successfully demonstrated that by presenting different contextual stimuli simultaneously with a test stimulus, they could systematically alter how that test stimulus was judged.

**Findings and Significance**

The paper's critical finding was that context does not just influence judgment, but can produce qualitatively different effects (assimilation vs. contrast). This work provided robust evidence against the idea that clinical judgment is a purely objective or stable process. It showed that a clinician's rating of a patient's case file could be swayed simply by the other cases reviewed in the same sitting.

This study became a cornerstone for later, more comprehensive theories of social judgment, such as Helson's Adaptation-Level Theory and Sherif and Hovland's Social Judgment Theory. For example, a subsequent, highly influential study by Sherif, Taub, and Hovland (1958) built directly on these findings. They further clarified the conditions under which assimilation versus contrast would occur, demonstrating that the "likeness" or discrepancy between the anchor and the stimulus was the key determinant. When an anchor is close to the item being judged, assimilation is more likely; when the anchor is very different, contrast is more likely.

In essence, Campbell, Hunt, and Lewis (1957) provided a critical, early experimental demonstration that the "frame of reference" is paramount in psychological judgments, a principle that has since been confirmed across numerous domains, from social psychology to consumer behavior and legal psychology.
