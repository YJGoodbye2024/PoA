## 1. Official Abstract
People encountering deviants who violate a stereotype try to maintain the stereotype by subtyping the deviants. They use the deviants' additional attributes to justify subtyping them. Participants read about counterstereotypic targets. Participants who were given no additional information about targets, and so had no grounds for subtyping them, did generalize from them and changed their stereotypes. However, participants who were told that targets had an additional, neutral attribute appeared to use it as grounds for subtyping them; their stereotypes remained unchanged. Participants came to view the neutral attributes as atypical of the stereotype and as associated with deviance, that is, as good reasons for subtyping the deviant. Neutral attributes blocked generalization from truly counterstereotypic targets but not from overly stereotypic ones, suggesting that their effect was due to participants' attempts to explain away individuals who strongly challenge their stereotypes.

The 1995 paper by Ziva Kunda and Kathryn C. Oleson, "Maintaining stereotypes in the face of disconfirmation: constructing grounds for subtyping deviants," is a foundational work in the social psychology of stereotyping. It experimentally demonstrates the cognitive mechanisms people use to protect their existing stereotypes from information that contradicts them. The central argument is that when faced with an individual who disconfirms a group stereotype (a "deviant"), perceivers do not necessarily abandon the stereotype. Instead, they actively seek ways to "fence off" the counterstereotypic individual as a special case, a process known as subtyping.

The key finding of the research is that even a neutral or irrelevant attribute can be seized upon as a justification for subtyping. In their experiments, when participants encountered a person who violated a stereotype (e.g., an aggressive woman, if the stereotype is that women are passive), they would change their overall stereotype of the group if that was the only information they had. However, if a neutral, additional piece of information was provided (e.g., the woman grew up in an urban environment), participants would use this attribute to create a new, smaller category—a subtype like "urban women"—leaving the general stereotype of "women" intact. This demonstrates a powerful and subtle cognitive bias aimed at preserving pre-existing beliefs.

Subsequent research has built upon and further clarified the findings of Kunda and Oleson. For instance, a later paper by the same authors in 1997 found that the extremity of the deviance is a critical factor. Moderately counter-stereotypical individuals are more likely to cause a change in the overall stereotype, whereas extremely deviant individuals are often dismissed as exceptions and can even produce a "boomerang effect," strengthening the original stereotype. This suggests there is a threshold for how much deviation from a stereotype a person will tolerate before they engage in subtyping to protect their worldview.

The concept of subtyping, as detailed by Kunda and Oleson, is now a core element in understanding prejudice and stereotype maintenance. It explains why exposure to counter-stereotypical individuals often fails to reduce prejudice at a broader level. The paper illustrates that people are adept at maintaining their beliefs by treating exceptions as unrepresentative of the group as a whole. This work is frequently cited in studies on intergroup relations, cognitive biases, and the persistence of social stereotypes.
