## 1. Official Abstract
"In this study, the existence of the team halo effect, the phenomenon that teams tend not to be blamed for their failures, is documented. With 2 studies using both real teams and controlled scenarios, the authors found evidence that the nature of the causal attribution processes used to diagnose failure scenarios leads to individuals being more likely to be identified as the cause of team failure than the team as a collective. Team schema development, as indexed by team experience, influences this effect, with individuals who have more team experience being less likely to show the team halo effect."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on analyses and summaries from third-party sources.

The central argument of this paper is the documentation of the "team halo effect," a cognitive bias where teams are often shielded from blame for failures, with responsibility instead being attributed to individual members. This phenomenon occurs because of the causal attribution processes people use to understand why a failure happened. When a team fails, observers tend to single out an individual as the cause rather than assigning blame to the team as a collective entity.

The research was conducted through two distinct studies that utilized both real-world teams and controlled, scenario-based experiments to test this hypothesis. The findings supported the existence of the team halo effect, demonstrating that in failure scenarios, individuals are more likely to be held accountable than the team as a whole.

A significant moderating factor in this effect is an individual's level of experience with teams. The authors found that people with more extensive team experience have a more developed "team schema," or mental model of how teams work. This deeper understanding appears to mitigate the bias; individuals with greater team experience are less prone to attribute a team's failure to a single person and are more capable of seeing the collective responsibility. This suggests that as familiarity with team dynamics grows, the tendency to protect the team entity from blame diminishes.

In essence, while teams are often credited for their successes, they are less likely to be blamed for their failures due to this halo effect. Instead, the cognitive process of "counterfactual thinking" is often applied, where managers or observers imagine alternative scenarios. This process tends to isolate decisions made by specific individuals, making them the focal point for blame rather than the team's collective process or dynamics.
