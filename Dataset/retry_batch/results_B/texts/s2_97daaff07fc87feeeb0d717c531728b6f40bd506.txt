## 1. Official Abstract
The present field study investigates how an individual's interpersonal conflict resolution behavior is affected by the individual's personality (assessed by the Big 5) and a situational factor (the other party's conflict behavior), as well as how the two factors interact. A hierarchical regression analysis based on 256 student–roommate/friend dyads shows that both factors are important. Extraversion and Agreeableness were significantly related to most conflict strategies used by students. The results show that interactions between the parties in conflict situations are strongly governed by the norm of reciprocity.

### **Research Goal and Methodology**
The study aimed to understand how an individual's conflict resolution strategy is influenced by two key factors: their inherent personality traits and the immediate situational context, specifically the behavior of the other person in the conflict. To achieve this, the researchers conducted a field study involving 256 pairs of students who were either roommates or friends. They used a hierarchical regression analysis to determine the significance of personality (as defined by the Big Five model) and the other party's actions on conflict behavior.

### **Key Findings: Personality and Conflict Style**
The study established significant connections between specific Big Five personality traits and the conflict resolution styles individuals tend to use.
*   **Agreeableness and Extraversion:** These two traits were found to be the most significant predictors of conflict strategies. Individuals with high Agreeableness and Extraversion were more likely to use an "integrating" style, which seeks a collaborative, win-win solution.
*   **Dominating Style:** A "dominating" conflict style was positively associated with Extraversion but negatively associated with Agreeableness.
*   **Other Associations:** Further analysis cited by other researchers indicates that an "obliging" style was positively linked to Agreeableness, while Extraversion was negatively associated with both "obliging" and "avoiding" styles.

### **Key Findings: The Role of Reciprocity**
A central conclusion of the paper is that conflict interactions are strongly governed by the "norm of reciprocity." This means that the behavior of one party in a conflict tends to provoke an equivalent response from the other party. The authors highlight that this is a primary mechanism through which conflicts can escalate; when one person uses a hostile approach, the other is likely to reciprocate with hostility, creating a negative spiral. This finding positions reciprocity as a critical factor in determining whether a conflict is managed constructively or destructively.
