## 1. Official Abstract
" Positive psicologia ha prosperado en los últimos 5 años. Los autores revisan los desarrollos recientes en el campo, incluyendo libros, reuniones, cursos y conferencias. También discuten la clasificación recién creada de fortalezas y virtudes del carácter, un complemento positivo a las diversas ediciones del Manual Diagnóstico y Estadístico de los Trastornos Mentales (p. ej., Asociación Americana de Psiquiatría, 1994), y presentan algunos hallazgos transculturales que sugieren una sorprendente ubicuidad de las fortalezas y virtudes. Finalmente, los autores se centran en las intervenciones psicológicas que aumentan la felicidad individual. En un estudio por Internet de 6 grupos, con asignación aleatoria y controlado por placebo, los autores probaron 5 supuestas intervenciones de felicidad y 1 ejercicio de control plausible. Encontraron que 3 de las intervenciones aumentaron de forma duradera la felicidad y disminuyeron los síntomas depresivos. Las intervenciones positivas pueden complementar las intervenciones tradicionales que alivian el sufrimiento y algún día pueden ser el legado práctico de la psicología positiva."

### Detailed Interpretation

The 2005 paper "Positive Psychology Progress: Empirical Validation of Interventions" by Martin Seligman and his colleagues serves as a significant update on the state of positive psychology, a field that had been rapidly expanding for five years. The authors' primary goal was to shift the focus of psychology from merely fixing what is wrong to also building on what is right.

The paper begins by chronicling the growth of this new field, noting the increase in publications, conferences, and academic courses dedicated to the study of human strengths and virtues. A key development discussed is the creation of the "Character Strengths and Virtues" handbook, intended as a positive counterpart to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM). This classification system was a foundational step to systematically study and measure positive human traits. The authors also point to cross-cultural research that suggests many of these character strengths are universally valued.

The core of the paper is dedicated to the empirical validation of interventions designed to increase happiness. The authors define "happiness" as a combination of three distinct elements: positive emotion and pleasure (the pleasant life), engagement (the engaged life), and meaning (the meaningful life).

To test interventions aimed at enhancing happiness, the researchers conducted a randomized, placebo-controlled study over the internet. The study involved five exercises designed to promote happiness and one control exercise. The key finding was that three of these interventions led to lasting increases in participants' reported happiness and a decrease in depressive symptoms. This demonstrated that specific, targeted exercises could have a durable positive effect on well-being.

In their conclusion, the authors argue that these positive interventions are a valuable supplement to traditional therapeutic approaches that focus on alleviating suffering. They propose that the practical application of scientifically validated interventions to increase happiness could become the most important legacy of the positive psychology movement. The study is seen as a pivotal moment, providing empirical evidence that happiness can be cultivated through specific, practiced skills.
