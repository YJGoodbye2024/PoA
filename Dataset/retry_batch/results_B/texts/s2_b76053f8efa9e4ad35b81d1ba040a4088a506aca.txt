## 1. Official Abstract
Four studies examined the intrapersonal and interpersonal consequences of seeking out others when good things happen (i.e., capitalization). Two studies showed that communicating personal positive events with others was associated with increased daily positive affect and well-being, above and beyond the impact of the positive event itself and other daily events. Moreover, when others were perceived to respond actively and constructively (and not passively or destructively) to capitalization attempts, the benefits were further enhanced. Two studies found that close relationships in which one's partner typically responds to capitalization attempts enthusiastically were associated with higher relationship well-being (e.g., intimacy, daily marital satisfaction). The results are discussed in terms of the theoretical and empirical importance of understanding how people "cope" with positive events, cultivate positive emotions, and enhance social bonds.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### Introduction
The introduction establishes the central theme: while a vast body of research focuses on how people cope with negative events, little is known about how they respond to positive events. The authors introduce the concept of "capitalization," defined as the process of "seeking out others to share the good news". They propose that sharing positive events may yield benefits beyond the positive event itself by increasing its salience and generating additional positive affect. The authors also theorize that the *reaction* of the person with whom the news is shared is critical. They introduce a 2x2 model of responses: Active vs. Passive and Constructive vs. Destructive. An Active-Constructive response (e.g., "That's great! I'm so proud of you. Tell me more.") is hypothesized to be the most beneficial, while other responses (Passive-Constructive, Active-Destructive, Passive-Destructive) are expected to be less effective or even detrimental. The introduction posits that capitalization and the subsequent responses have both intrapersonal (affect, well-being) and interpersonal (relationship satisfaction, intimacy) benefits.

### Study 1: Capitalization and Daily Well-Being
*   **Method:** This study used a daily-experience diary format. Participants reported on their most important positive event of the day, whether they shared it with others, and their daily positive and negative affect and life satisfaction.
*   **Results:** The findings showed that on days when individuals shared a positive event, they experienced higher levels of positive affect and life satisfaction. This effect remained significant even after controlling for the pleasantness of the positive event itself, indicating that the act of telling someone added a unique benefit.

### Study 2: The Role of Perceived Responses
*   **Method:** Building on the first study, Study 2 again used a daily diary method but added a crucial component: participants rated the response they received when sharing their positive event.
*   **Results:** The study replicated the findings of Study 1, confirming that sharing positive events is linked to higher positive affect. Crucially, it demonstrated that the perceived response mattered significantly. When individuals perceived the response as Active-Constructive, the benefits of capitalization were enhanced. Other types of responses did not provide this additional boost. This supports the hypothesis that enthusiastic, engaged responses are a key mechanism through which capitalization yields intrapersonal benefits.

### Study 3: Capitalization in Romantic Relationships
*   **Method:** This study shifted focus to the interpersonal benefits within romantic relationships. Couples provided data on their relationship well-being, including satisfaction, intimacy, commitment, and trust. They also rated their partner's typical response style to their capitalization attempts.
*   **Results:** A strong association was found between perceived Active-Constructive responding from a partner and higher relationship well-being for both men and women. Measures of intimacy, in particular, were strongly correlated with a partner's enthusiastic responses to good news. This suggests that how a partner reacts to one's successes and triumphs is a significant factor in relationship quality.

### Study 4: Daily Marital Satisfaction
*   **Method:** This was another daily diary study involving married couples. For 10 days, both partners reported on their daily marital satisfaction and intimacy, as well as any capitalization attempts and their partner's perceived response.
*   **Results:** The results provided daily-level evidence for the findings in Study 3. On days when a person shared a positive event and perceived their partner's response as Active-Constructive, they reported higher levels of marital satisfaction and intimacy for that day. This study reinforces the idea that positive event-sharing is a recurring process that helps sustain and enhance close relationships.

### General Discussion
The authors conclude that "coping" with positive events is a vital, though understudied, psychological process. The four studies provide consistent evidence that sharing positive events (capitalization) is associated with increased personal well-being (intrapersonal benefits) and higher relationship quality (interpersonal benefits). A key takeaway is that these benefits are significantly amplified when the listener responds in an active and constructive manner. The authors argue that such responses make the discloser feel understood, validated, and cared for, which strengthens social bonds. The paper suggests that reactions to positive events may be an even clearer indicator of relationship health than reactions to negative events.
