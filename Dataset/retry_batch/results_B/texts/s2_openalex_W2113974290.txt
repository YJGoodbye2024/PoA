## 1. Official Abstract
"One hundred twenty male undergraduates waiting either alone, with a friend, or with a stranger, overheard a woman fall and cry out in pain. Two-person groups were less likely to offer help to the injured woman than were subjects who overheard the emergency while alone. Pairs of friends were less inhibited from intervening than were strangers and helped significantly faster. In this ambiguous situation, each bystander may look to others for guidance before acting, misinterpret their apparent lack of concern, and decide the situation is not serious. Friends seem less likely to misinterpret each other's initial inaction than strangers."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
### Introduction
Following up on their prior research into the bystander effect (often called the "Genovese syndrome"), Latané and Rodin sought to investigate whether the presence of other people inhibits intervention even when there is no physical danger to the bystander and a clear victim is in distress. Their previous work with a smoke-filled room involved personal danger, but this experiment was designed to isolate the social psychological factors that delay or prevent helping. The core hypothesis was that the more people present during an emergency, the less likely any individual is to help. The researchers were particularly interested in whether the relationship between bystanders (friends versus strangers) would influence this effect.

### Method
The experiment was staged at Columbia University, with 120 male undergraduate students as subjects. Participants were asked to complete a questionnaire in a waiting room. They were placed in one of four conditions:
1.  **Alone:** The subject was by himself in the room.
2.  **With a Friend:** The subject was in the room with a friend they had brought with them.
3.  **With a Stranger:** The subject was paired with another participant they did not know.
4.  **With a Passive Confederate:** The subject was paired with a research assistant pretending to be a participant, who was instructed to ignore the subsequent emergency.

While the subjects were filling out questionnaires, they heard a staged emergency from the adjoining room. An audio recording was played depicting a female experimenter climbing a chair, followed by a loud crash and her crying out in pain. She could be heard moaning that her foot was caught and she couldn't get up. The researchers timed how long it took for subjects to intervene, if they did so at all.

### Results
The results strongly supported the bystander effect hypothesis. The speed and likelihood of intervention were highest among subjects who were alone and lowest when other people were present.

*   **Alone Condition:** 70% of subjects who were alone intervened to help the woman.
*   **Paired with a Stranger:** When two strangers were together, the intervention rate dropped significantly. Only 40% of these pairs had one or both members offering help. In another reporting of the results for two-person groups, the rate was as low as 20%.
*   **Paired with a Passive Confederate:** When the subject was with a confederate who was instructed not to react, the intervention rate plummeted to just 7%.
*   **Paired with a Friend:** Interestingly, pairs of friends were more likely to intervene than pairs of strangers, with a 70% intervention rate for the pair. Friends were less inhibited and helped significantly faster than strangers.

### Discussion
Latané and Rodin concluded that the presence of other people creates a "diffusion of responsibility," where individuals feel less personal obligation to act because others are available to do so. Furthermore, in ambiguous situations, people tend to look to others for cues on how to behave. If others appear calm and unconcerned (as they too are looking for cues), everyone may collectively misinterpret the situation as a non-emergency, a phenomenon known as "pluralistic ignorance."

The difference in helping rates between friends and strangers is significant. The researchers suggested that friends are less likely to misinterpret each other's initial inaction. They are more likely to communicate with each other, break the cycle of pluralistic ignorance, and feel less social embarrassment about potentially overreacting. The study powerfully demonstrated that the failure to help is not necessarily due to apathy or indifference, but rather to potent social dynamics that influence how people define a situation and their responsibility within it.
