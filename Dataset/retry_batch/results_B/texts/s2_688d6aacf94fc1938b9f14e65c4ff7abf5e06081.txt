## 1. Official Abstract
"in two experiments we investigated the effects of stereotyping on (a) reactions to a behavioral transgression and (b) the recall of information bearing on it. Subjects read a case file describing a transgression committed by a target (in Experiment 1, a job-related infraction; in Experiment 2, a criminal act). In some cases, the target's transgression was stereotypic of the target's ethnic group (conveyed through his name), and in other cases it was not. After reading the case file, subjects judged the likelihood that the transgression would recur and recommended punishment for the offense. These judgment data supported the hypothesis that stereotypes function as judgmental heuristics. Specifically, subjects used a stereotype of the target to infer the reasons for his transgression, and then based their punishment decisions on the implications of these inferences, considering other relevant information only when a stereotype-based explanation of the behavior was not available. However, recall data suggested that once a stereotype-based impression of the crime and its determinants was formed, subjects reviewed other available information in an attempt to confirm the implications of this impression. This led to differential recall of presented information, depending on whether its implications were consistent with, inconsistent with, or irrelevant to those of the stereotype."

### Detailed Interpretation

The 1985 paper by Galen V. Bodenhausen and Robert S. Wyer, Jr., "Effects of stereotypes on decision making and information-processing strategies," presents a foundational investigation into how stereotypes act as cognitive shortcuts, or "judgmental heuristics," in social perception and decision-making. The research demonstrates that when individuals are presented with a person's transgression, they are inclined to use stereotypes to explain the behavior, which in turn influences their judgments and memory.

The core of the study is built on two experiments where participants evaluated cases of misconduct. The key manipulation was whether the transgression was stereotypical of the actor's ethnic group, which was suggested by their name.

**Key Findings:**

*   **Stereotypes as Heuristics in Judgment**: The study's primary finding is that people use stereotypes to infer the cause of a person's negative behavior. When a behavior aligned with a pre-existing stereotype (e.g., an ethnic stereotype associated with a specific crime), participants used that stereotype as a simple explanation. Consequently, their decisions, such as recommending punishment or judging the likelihood of the behavior happening again, were based on the inferences drawn from the stereotype. Other relevant information presented in the case file was often only considered when a stereotype-based explanation was not readily available. This supports the "cognitive miser" theory, which posits that individuals prefer less effortful thinking and will rely on simple heuristics like stereotypes to simplify complex judgments.

*   **Biased Information Processing and Recall**: The study also revealed that stereotypes significantly affect how information is processed and remembered. After forming an initial impression based on a stereotype, participants appeared to engage in a confirmatory search of the other available information. This resulted in a biased memory of the case details. Information that was consistent with the stereotype was more likely to be recalled, while information that was inconsistent or irrelevant was less likely to be remembered. This demonstrates that stereotypes don't just influence the final judgment, but also shape the very information that the judgment is based upon by guiding attention and memory.

In essence, the paper provides strong evidence for a two-stage process. First, if a stereotype is available and seems relevant, it is used to quickly form a judgment. Second, this initial judgment then directs the subsequent processing of information in a biased manner to confirm the stereotype's implications. This work has been highly influential, contributing to the understanding of stereotypes not just as a source of prejudice, but as a fundamental aspect of cognitive functioning that can systematically bias social perception and memory.
