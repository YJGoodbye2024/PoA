## 1. Official Abstract
"Antagonism, the low pole of Agreeableness, references traits related to immorality, combativeness, grandiosity, callousness, and distrustfulness. It is a robust correlate of externalizing behaviors such as antisocial behavior, aggression, and substance use; in fact, in many cases, it is the strongest trait correlate. lt represents the core of many important and impactful psychopathological constructs (e.g., psychopathy, antisocial and narcissistic personality disorders). It is also central to models of general and disordered personality, psychopathology, and interpersonal behavior. As Neuroticism is core to understanding the intense distress and suffering that comes with internalizing disorders, Antagonism is core to understanding the impairment and suffering (to the individual and society at large) that comes with externalizing disorders."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the author-accepted manuscript was accessible for analysis. The following is a synopsis structured according to the paper's original sections.

### **Introduction**
The authors begin by asserting that Antagonism, the opposite pole of the Big Five personality trait Agreeableness, is a critically important construct for understanding a wide range of maladaptive behaviors and personality disorders. They argue that despite its significance, it has been "uniquely underappreciated" compared to other traits like Neuroticism. The paper's goal is to define Antagonism, demonstrate its importance by reviewing its connections to externalizing behaviors and psychopathology, and explore the reasons for its relative neglect in personality research.

### **What is Antagonism?**
This section defines Antagonism not merely as the absence of agreeable traits but as the presence of distinct, socially aversive ones. The authors break the broad domain of Antagonism down into five core bipolar traits:
*   **Callousness** vs. Compassion
*   **Immorality** vs. Morality
*   **Distrust** vs. Trust
*   **Combativeness** vs. Affability
*   **Arrogance** vs. Modesty

Broadly, Antagonism is characterized as "individual differences in the motivation to maintain positive social relations with others." Antagonistic individuals are more willing to sacrifice interpersonal harmony to achieve their own goals.

### **Why is Antagonism Important?**
The authors present a meta-analytic summary to argue that Antagonism is the most significant personality correlate for a range of externalizing problems. The analysis reveals several key findings:
*   **Dominant Correlate of Externalizing:** For psychopathy, antisocial personality disorder (APD), narcissistic personality disorder (NPD), all forms of aggression, and antisocial behavior, Antagonism accounts for more of the explained variance than the other four Five-Factor Model domains combined.
*   **Central to "Dark" Personalities:** Antagonism is identified as the core feature of psychopathy, holding different psychopathy inventories together. It is also the central feature of narcissism and the common thread in the "Dark Triad" (psychopathy, narcissism, Machiavellianism).
*   **Predictor of Other Outcomes:** Beyond externalizing behaviors, Antagonism is the second-largest personality predictor (after Neuroticism) of poor relationship satisfaction and victimization. It is also linked to a history of accidents.

### **Why is Antagonism Underappreciated?**
The paper proposes three primary reasons for the neglect of Antagonism in research:
1.  **A Focus on Agreeableness:** Research has historically concentrated on the positive pole (Agreeableness) and its links to prosocial outcomes, leaving the "darker," antagonistic side less explored. The authors note that measures of this domain often contain more content related to Agreeableness than Antagonism.
2.  **The "Jingle Fallacy":** Researchers have studied many constructs that are essentially facets of Antagonism (e.g., psychopathy, narcissism, reactive aggression) under different names, without integrating them into a cohesive framework. This has fragmented the literature and obscured the central role of the broader Antagonism trait.
3.  **Lack of a Unifying Model:** Unlike Neuroticism, which is widely accepted as the core of internalizing disorders, there has been no similar, broadly accepted model positioning Antagonism as the core of externalizing disorders. The authors advocate for such a model to unify research and clinical practice.

### **Conclusion**
Lynam and Miller conclude by reiterating that Antagonism is a "foundational trait" for understanding externalizing behaviors, personality disorders like APD and NPD, and general interpersonal functioning. They argue that just as Neuroticism is fundamental to understanding internalizing problems (like anxiety and depression), Antagonism is equally fundamental for understanding externalizing problems (like aggression and antisocial behavior). The authors call for greater appreciation and focused study of Antagonism as a core personality domain to advance the understanding of personality, psychopathology, and antisocial behavior.
