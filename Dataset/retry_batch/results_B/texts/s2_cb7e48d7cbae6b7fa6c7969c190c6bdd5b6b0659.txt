The target work is a book-length volume from a longitudinal study and does not contain a formal abstract in the modern sense. The "Preface" serves as the official introduction to the work's scope and purpose.

**Verbatim Preface:**
> "This volume reports a 25-year follow-up of the gifted group that was the subject of Volume I of Genetic Studies of Genius. The original group, numbering 1,528 subjects, was selected in 1921-22 from the public schools of California on the basis of an intelligence test score ranking in the top one per cent for the school population of that day. The story of their childhood and youth has been told in the earlier volumes of this series. The present report is concerned with their adult careers up to 1946. It deals with their educational histories, their vocational achievements, their interests, their marital adjustments, and their physical and mental health. In all these areas the purpose has been to discover what the typical gifted child is like as an adult and what factors have influenced his development.

> The social importance of a study of this kind is obvious. The group is one of extraordinary intellectual promise. If it is true, as some believe, that the welfare of a nation depends in large measure upon the contributions of its most gifted members, it is a matter of concern to know what becomes of them. The follow-up of this group to mid-life and beyond should go far to answer this question. It should also throw much light on the whole problem of vocational and personal adjustment and on the roles played by native endowment and by environmental influences in shaping the life pattern.

> The task of keeping in touch with so large a group over a period of a quarter of a century has been a formidable one. It has been made possible only by the continued financial support of the Commonwealth Fund of New York and by the generous co-operation of the subjects and their parents. To both of these we are deeply grateful."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible. The following is a synopsis structured according to the book's original sections.

This book is Volume IV of the *Genetic Studies of Genius*, a landmark longitudinal study initiated by Lewis Terman in 1921 to track the life developments of 1,528 children identified as "gifted" based on high IQ scores. This volume reports on a 25-year follow-up, analyzing the subjects' transition into adulthood.

#### **Introduction: Inception and Nature of the Research**
The study's primary goal was to debunk the prevailing stereotype that intellectually gifted children were often physically weak, socially inept, and prone to failure in adulthood. Terman's research set out to track a large group of children with IQs in the top one percent to see how their lives unfolded. This volume specifically examines their progress and characteristics as adults, covering data collected through field-worker visits, mail-in information blanks, and specialized tests in 1936, 1940, and 1945.

#### **Methods: Composition, Follow-up, and Data Collection**
The subjects were initially identified in California schools in 1921-22. The follow-up data for this 1947 volume was gathered through several means:
*   **Field Worker Interviews:** Trained staff conducted in-person interviews with the subjects.
*   **Information Blanks:** Detailed questionnaires were mailed to the subjects and, in earlier years, to their parents and teachers to gather information on health, education, vocation, and personal life.
*   **Trait Ratings:** Subjects, and sometimes their spouses or parents, were asked to rate their own personality traits.
*   **Standardized Tests:** The Concept Mastery test, a high-level intelligence test, was administered to assess adult intellectual status.

#### **Results: Health, Achievement, and Personal Life**
The findings of the 25-year follow-up provided a comprehensive portrait of the gifted group in adulthood:
*   **Physique and Health:** Contrary to stereotypes, the gifted group was, on the whole, physically superior to the general population. Terman's initial findings showed them to be healthier, taller, and more socially adapted than average children, and these trends continued into adulthood.
*   **Educational and Vocational Achievement:** The group was highly accomplished. A vast majority attended and graduated from college, with a significant number earning advanced degrees. Vocationally, they far surpassed the general population in entering professional fields like medicine, law, and academia. However, not all subjects were high-achievers. Terman compared the most successful men (the "A" group) with the least successful (the "C" group) and found that childhood ratings of "perseverance," "self-confidence," and "integration toward goals" were far more predictive of adult success than IQ alone.
*   **Mental Health and Marital Adjustment:** The study found that the gifted group's rates of mental health issues, such as psychosis and maladjustment, were generally comparable to or slightly better than those of the general population. The book also includes extensive data on marriage, divorce rates, and marital happiness, finding the group to be, on average, well-adjusted in their personal lives.
*   **Adult Intelligence:** The study examined the intelligence of the subjects' offspring, finding that their children's average IQ was around 127. While significantly above the population average, this was lower than the parents' scores, a phenomenon Terman and Oden attributed to the statistical principle of filial regression.

#### **Discussion and Conclusion**
Terman and Oden concluded that their 25-year follow-up largely confirmed their initial hypothesis: intellectually superior children tend to become well-adjusted, successful, and physically healthy adults. The study demonstrated that a high IQ in childhood was a strong predictor of academic and vocational success. However, it also highlighted the critical role of non-intellectual personality traits—such as persistence and goal orientation—in determining the extent of an individual's life achievements. The research advocated for the early identification and specialized education of gifted children to nurture their potential for the benefit of society.
