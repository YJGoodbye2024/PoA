## 1. Official Abstract
"Mental accounts are often characterized as self-control devices that consumers employ to prevent excess spending and consumption. However, under certain conditions of ambiguity, the mental accounting process is malleable; that is, consumers have flexibility in assigning expenses to different mental accounts. We demonstrate how consumers flexibly classify expenses, or construct accounts, to justify spending. An expense that can be assigned to more than one account (i.e., an ambiguous expense) is more likely to be incurred than an unambiguous expense that is constrained either by existing budgets or by previously constructed accounts. We explore the justification processes that underlie these results and their implications for mental accounts as self-control devices."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### **Introduction**
The paper begins by challenging the traditional view of mental accounting as a rigid self-control mechanism for tracking expenses and controlling consumption. The authors propose that mental accounting can be "malleable," especially under conditions of ambiguity. This malleability allows consumers to flexibly categorize expenses to justify attractive spending they might otherwise forgo. The core idea is that when an expense is ambiguous and can be assigned to more than one mental account, consumers are more likely to incur it, particularly if one of their existing accounts has a surplus. This contrasts with unambiguous expenses that are clearly constrained by a specific budget. The research focuses on the budgetary role of mental accounts and how flexibility in this process affects consumer spending decisions.

### **Study 1**
The first study aimed to demonstrate that an ambiguous expense, which could be plausibly categorized into different mental accounts, is more likely to be incurred than an unambiguous one.

*   **Methods:** Participants were presented with a scenario where they had a surplus in either their "food" budget or their "entertainment" budget. They were then given an opportunity to spend on one of three items: a food delivery (unambiguously a food expense), a music concert (unambiguously an entertainment expense), or a restaurant dinner (ambiguous, could be food or entertainment).
*   **Results:** The findings supported the hypothesis. The likelihood of purchasing the ambiguous restaurant dinner was high regardless of whether the surplus was in the food or entertainment account. In contrast, the likelihood of purchasing the unambiguous items (food delivery or concert) was high only when the surplus was in the corresponding account. This shows that consumers exploit the ambiguity of an expense to justify spending from an account with available funds.

### **Study 2**
This study explored the underlying justification process, testing whether consumers actively re-categorize an ambiguous expense to fit a budget with a surplus.

*   **Methods:** Participants were first asked to imagine having a surplus in their entertainment budget. They were then presented with the ambiguous restaurant dinner expense and asked to rate their likelihood of purchasing it. Afterward, they were asked to categorize the dinner expense as either "food" or "entertainment."
*   **Results:** As predicted, participants who had a surplus in their entertainment budget were not only more likely to purchase the restaurant dinner but also significantly more likely to classify it as an entertainment expense. This provides direct evidence that consumers flexibly re-label ambiguous expenses to justify their consumption decisions.

### **Study 3**
The third study investigated whether this malleability extends to the *construction* of mental accounts themselves, not just the classification of expenses.

*   **Methods:** Participants were given a list of expenses from the past month without any pre-defined categories. They were asked to create their own mental accounts by grouping these items. The key manipulation was the presence of an attractive but difficult-to-justify expense (e.g., an expensive CD box set).
*   **Results:** The study found that participants who were tempted by the attractive expense were more likely to create broader, more encompassing mental accounts (e.g., a general "entertainment" account rather than separate "music" and "movies" accounts). This allowed them to "hide" the cost of the tempting item within a larger budget, making the expenditure seem more justifiable. This demonstrates that malleability occurs not only in how expenses are assigned to existing accounts but also in how the accounts are initially constructed.

### **General Discussion**
The authors conclude that mental accounting is not just a rigid tool for self-control but can also be a flexible system that consumers use to justify desired expenditures. When faced with an attractive spending opportunity, consumers can exploit ambiguity in two main ways: by classifying the expense into an account with a surplus (as in Studies 1 and 2) or by restructuring the accounts themselves to better accommodate the expense (as in Study 3).

This "malleable" nature of mental accounting suggests that its effectiveness as a self-control device is limited. While budgets can constrain spending, their influence is weakened when expenses are ambiguous. The research implies that the very act of creating and labeling accounts is a dynamic process that can be influenced by short-term consumption temptations, allowing consumers to do what they want rather than what their budgets might strictly dictate.
