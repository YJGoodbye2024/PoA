## 1. Official Abstract
"The question of consistency between attitudes and behaviors has had a controversial history. After a period of assuming a strong consistency, researchers were confronted with a series of failures to find such a link. The field then entered a second stage of research in which the question "When do attitudes predict behavior?" was posed. The present article is concerned with a variable that has been found to moderate attitude-behavior consistency—the manner of attitude formation. Attitudes based on direct behavioral experience with an attitude object have been found to be more predictive of later behavior than attitudes based on indirect, nonbehavioral experience. The article reviews the evidence concerning this differential consistency and then focuses on the process or processes that may underlie the effect. A number of potential mediators are discussed. It is concluded that attitudes based on direct experience are more clearly defined, held with greater certainty, and more stable over time than are attitudes based on indirect experience. All of these factors may enhance attitude-behavior consistency. In addition, it is suggested that direct experience may lead to the formation of an attitude that is more likely to be accessed from memory upon observation of the attitude object. The process by which attitude accessibility may mediate the attitude-behavior relationship is detailed."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis

### **I. Introduction**
The authors begin by chronicling the history of attitude-behavior consistency research, noting that the initial assumption of a strong link was challenged by studies in the 1960s showing surprisingly weak relationships. This led to a "second generation" of research focused not on *if* attitudes predict behavior, but *when*. The central thesis of this chapter is introduced: the *manner* in which an attitude is formed is a crucial moderator of its consistency with behavior. Specifically, the authors propose that attitudes formed through **direct, behavioral experience** with an object are stronger and more predictive of subsequent behavior than attitudes formed through **indirect experience** (e.g., reading or hearing about it).

### **II. The Differential Predictive Validity of Attitudes Formed via Direct and Indirect Experience**
This section reviews the empirical evidence supporting the chapter's main thesis. The authors detail a series of their own experiments that consistently demonstrate the phenomenon.

*   **A. The Initial Field Experiment:** A study was conducted on student attitudes toward a housing crisis at Cornell University. Some students had direct experience (e.g., living in temporary housing), while others only had indirect experience. The study found that the correlation between attitudes and relevant behaviors (like signing a petition) was significantly higher for the direct-experience group.
*   **B. A Laboratory Investigation:** To establish causality, a lab experiment was designed where participants formed attitudes toward a set of puzzles. One group gained direct experience by working with the puzzles, while the other gained indirect experience by watching someone else work with them and reading instructions. In a subsequent "free-play" period, the correspondence between participants' attitudes toward the puzzles and the time they actually spent playing with them was much stronger in the direct-experience condition.

These studies establish that attitudes based on direct experience are more reliable predictors of behavior. The remainder of the chapter is dedicated to exploring *why* this is the case.

### **III. What Is It about Direct Experience?**
The authors explore the psychological mechanisms that could explain the moderating effect of direct experience. They propose and analyze several potential mediators.

*   **A. Amount of Information and Confidence:** The most straightforward explanation is that direct experience provides more, and better, information about the attitude object. This increased information leads to an attitude that is held with greater confidence. The authors present evidence showing that attitudes formed through direct experience are indeed held with more certainty, and that this confidence is correlated with higher attitude-behavior consistency.
*   **B. The Nature of the Information:** Beyond the quantity of information, the *quality* differs. Direct experience provides behavioral self-perception information ("I did X, therefore I must feel Y about it"). This type of information may be qualitatively different and more powerful than the cognitive information received through indirect means.
*   **C. Stability of the Attitude:** The chapter posits that attitudes formed via direct experience are more stable over time. An attitude measured at one point can only predict a future behavior if the attitude itself remains unchanged. Evidence is presented suggesting that direct-experience attitudes are indeed less susceptible to decay or change.
*   **D. Accessibility of the Attitude from Memory:** This is presented as a key explanatory mechanism. The authors argue that for an attitude to guide behavior, it must first be activated from memory when the person encounters the attitude object. They propose that direct experience creates a stronger, more "well-learned" association between the object and its evaluation in memory. This makes the attitude more accessible. An attitude that comes to mind more readily and automatically upon seeing the object is more likely to influence one's perception and subsequent actions, thus ensuring higher attitude-behavior consistency.

### **IV. Concluding Remarks**
The final section synthesizes the arguments. Fazio and Zanna conclude that the way an attitude is formed is a critical determinant of its strength and its power to predict behavior. They summarize that attitudes based on direct experience are more predictive because they are more clearly defined, held with greater confidence, more stable, and, crucially, more accessible from memory. The authors frame the accessibility of the attitude as a pivotal process. When an attitude is highly accessible, it is more likely to be activated automatically in the presence of the attitude object, guiding the individual's perceptions of the situation and, consequently, their behavior. This provides a comprehensive model for understanding *why* direct experience leads to greater attitude-behavior consistency.
