## 1. Official Abstract
In economic analyses of asymmetric information, better-informed agents are assumed capable of reproducing the judgments of less-informed agents. We discuss a systematic violation of this assumption that we call the "curse of knowledge." Better-informed agents are unable to ignore private information even when it is in their interest to do so; more information is not always better. Comparing judgments made in individual-level and market experiments, we find that market forces reduce the curse by approximately 50 percent but do not eliminate it. Implications for bargaining, strategic behavior by firms, principal-agent problems, and choice under uncertainty are discussed.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. A detailed interpretation based on secondary source analysis is provided below.

The 1989 paper, "The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings: An Experimental Analysis," by Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber, introduces and empirically tests the "curse of knowledge" cognitive bias within an economic context. The central argument is a direct challenge to a standard assumption in economics: that people with more information can accurately predict the decisions or judgments of those with less information. The authors demonstrate that, in reality, better-informed individuals are often "cursed" by their knowledge, finding it difficult to ignore what they know when trying to imagine a less-informed perspective.

The core of the research was a series of experiments designed to see if this bias persists in economic settings, specifically in situations involving asymmetric information. The authors investigated whether individuals with private information could disregard it when predicting the behavior of uninformed counterparts, even when it was financially advantageous to do so.

A key finding from their experiments was that the curse of knowledge is a robust phenomenon. Better-informed participants consistently overestimated the extent to which their private information was shared by others, which skewed their judgments. This bias persisted even when participants had clear incentives to be accurate. The authors found that simply having more information is not always beneficial and can sometimes lead to suboptimal decisions.

The study also explored the impact of market forces on this bias. By comparing individual decision-making with behavior in experimental markets, they discovered that market mechanisms could mitigate, but not eliminate, the curse of knowledge. The competitive pressures and feedback inherent in a market setting reduced the effect of the bias by about 50 percent, but it remained a significant factor.

The paper concludes by discussing the wide-ranging implications of this cognitive bias for various fields of economics. The curse of knowledge can lead to inefficiencies in:
*   **Bargaining:** A knowledgeable party might struggle to understand the perspective of a less-informed party, leading to negotiation breakdowns.
*   **Principal-Agent Problems:** Well-informed principals may create incentive schemes based on the flawed assumption that agents see the world as they do.
*   **Marketing and Product Design:** Experts (engineers, marketers) may assume that potential customers share their deep understanding of a product, leading to communication failures and poor sales.

This concept was famously illustrated in a later experiment by Elizabeth Newton in 1990, where "tappers" who tapped out the rhythm of a well-known song drastically overestimated how many "listeners" would be able to guess the song. The tappers, hearing the melody in their heads, were cursed by their knowledge and could not appreciate the listener's perspective of hearing only disconnected taps.
