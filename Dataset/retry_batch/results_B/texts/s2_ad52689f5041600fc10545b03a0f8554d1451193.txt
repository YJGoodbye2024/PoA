## 1. Official Abstract
"Most studies of decision making, based on artificial tasks assigned in laboratory settings, view people as biased and unskilled. In this modern classic, Gary A. Klein proposes a naturalistic approach to decision making, which views people as gaining experience that then enables them to use a combination of intuition and analysis to make decisions. To illustrate this approach, Klein tells stories of people—from pilots to chess masters—acting under such real-life constraints as time pressure, high stakes, personal responsibility, and shifting conditions."

### The Foundation: Naturalistic Decision Making (NDM)
Gary Klein's work is a cornerstone of the Naturalistic Decision Making (NDM) field. This approach diverges from traditional decision research, which often studies novice participants in controlled laboratory settings with artificial tasks. Instead, NDM focuses on how experts, such as firefighters, critical care nurses, pilots, and military commanders, make high-stakes decisions in their own "natural" environments. These settings are characterized by time pressure, high stakes, uncertainty, ambiguous goals, and changing conditions. Klein's aim was to document the strengths and capabilities that people use to handle these complex challenges, rather than focusing on cognitive biases and errors.

### The Core Theory: Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) Model
The central thesis of *Sources of Power* is the Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) model. Developed by Klein and his colleagues from studies of experienced fireground commanders, the model explains how people can make effective decisions quickly without conducting a formal analysis of comparing multiple options. Research showed that in approximately 80% of observed cases, experienced commanders did not compare options but generated a plausible course of action as the first one they considered.

The RPD model fuses two key processes: situation assessment and mental simulation. It generally follows these steps:
1.  **Pattern Recognition:** An expert decision-maker experiences a situation and uses their extensive experience to recognize it as a familiar, typical case. This pattern matching allows them to quickly understand the dynamics of the situation, including plausible goals, important cues, and what to expect.
2.  **Generate a Single, Workable Option:** Based on this recognition, the expert immediately identifies a workable course of action—the one that is typically successful in that type of situation. They do not generate a list of options to compare; they select the first one that seems adequate. This concept is known as "satisficing"—choosing the first workable option, not necessarily the single optimal one, which is highly efficient under pressure.
3.  **Mental Simulation:** The expert then rapidly runs a mental simulation, imagining how that course of action will play out. If the simulation is successful, they proceed to act. If they spot potential problems, they may try to adapt the plan. If the plan seems unworkable, they reject it and move on to consider the next most typical response, simulating it in turn. This evaluation happens sequentially, not concurrently.

The RPD model demonstrates that expert decision-making is not a-rational, but rather a blend of intuition (the pattern recognition) and analysis (the mental simulation).

### The "Sources of Power" for Decision-Making
Klein identifies several "sources of power" that enable experts to make decisions using the RPD model. These stand in contrast to the tools of classical decision theory, such as statistical analysis and deductive reasoning.
*   **Intuition:** The ability to use experience to recognize key patterns that are invisible to novices.
*   **Mental Simulation:** The capacity to project a situation into the future and visualize possible outcomes to test a course of action.
*   **Metaphor and Analogy:** Using past experiences to frame a new problem, which can help in understanding the situation and generating solutions.
*   **Storytelling:** The ability to organize concepts and experiences into meaningful narratives that can be recalled to guide future decisions.

### Causes of Poor Decisions
The book also explores why decisions fail. Contrary to a primary focus on cognitive biases, Klein argues that many poor outcomes stem from a lack of experience or a lack of critical information. Other contributing factors include extreme stress, time pressure, and an inability to properly assess the situation due to noise or flawed feedback. One specific failure mode highlighted is when decision-makers imagine away contradictory evidence during a mental simulation, a potential pitfall of relying on this technique.
