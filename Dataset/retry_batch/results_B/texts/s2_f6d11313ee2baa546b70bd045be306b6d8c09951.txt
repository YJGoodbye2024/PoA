## 1. Official Abstract
"We propose that buying- and selling-price estimates reflect a focus on what the consumer forgoes in the potential exchange and that this notion offers insight into the well-known difference between those two types of value assessment. We suggest that for sellers, the most prominent aspect of the transaction is giving up the item, whereas for buyers, the most prominent aspect is giving up the money necessary to obtain the item. This difference in focus, we argue, leads buyers and sellers to generate their price estimates using different considerations. Selling prices will be more heavily influenced by thoughts about the item, and buying prices will be more heavily influenced by thoughts about the money and alternative ways to spend it. Four studies examining buying- and selling-price estimates of tickets for National Collegiate Athletic Association basketball games offer consistent support for these ideas. The studies show that naturally occurring differences among respondents in attitudes relating to the tickets that sellers forgo (e.g., significance of the game) corresponded more closely to variation in selling prices than in buying prices. Using controlled manipulations we then showed that changes in aspects relating to the game (e.g., expected climate in the stadium) affected selling prices more than buying prices, but changes relating to the expenditure (e.g., list price of the ticket) influenced buying prices more than selling prices. We also showed that drawing attention to the benefits of possessing a ticket before asking for the price estimates influenced buying prices more than selling prices, supporting our claim that otherwise these benefits are naturally more salient to sellers than buyers."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### Focusing on Forgone Outcomes: Implications for Buyers and Sellers
The paper introduces a novel explanation for the endowment effect—the well-documented gap between the prices at which people are willing to sell a good they own (Willingness to Accept or WTA) and the prices they are willing to pay to acquire the same good (Willingness to Pay or WTP). The authors propose that this gap arises because buyers and sellers focus on different aspects of what they "forgo" in the transaction.

*   **Sellers** focus on forgoing the item itself and the experiences associated with it. Therefore, their selling price is heavily influenced by their feelings and thoughts about the product.
*   **Buyers** focus on forgoing the money required for the purchase. Their buying price is thus more sensitive to the financial aspect of the transaction and what else that money could be used for.

This "focus on the forgone" theory suggests that the valuation gap is not just about loss aversion, but about the different reference points and considerations that are salient to each party in an exchange.

### Study 1
This study aimed to demonstrate a natural correlation between attitudes toward the forgone item and selling prices. The context was the high demand for tickets to Duke University's NCAA basketball games. Students who had won tickets in a lottery (sellers) and those who had not (buyers) were surveyed.

*   **Method:** Participants were asked for their selling or buying price for a ticket to an upcoming game. They also answered questions about their attachment to the team and the perceived importance of the game.
*   **Results:** The study found that sellers' prices were significantly higher than buyers' prices, confirming the endowment effect. Crucially, the correlation between attitudes toward the team/game and price was much stronger for sellers than for buyers. This supports the hypothesis that sellers' valuations are more influenced by their feelings about the item being forgone.

### Study 2
Study 2 moved from correlation to causation by using controlled manipulations to test the core hypothesis. The authors manipulated aspects related to the item (forgone by sellers) and aspects related to the expenditure (forgone by buyers).

*   **Method:** A 3x3 experimental design was used, again involving Duke basketball tickets. The manipulations included:
    *   **Item-related:** The significance of the game (regular season, conference tournament, or a hypothetical "super-important" game).
    *   **Expenditure-related:** The original list price of the ticket ($15, $30, or $45).
*   **Results:** As predicted, the significance of the game had a much larger impact on selling prices than on buying prices. Conversely, the ticket's list price had a significantly greater effect on buying prices than on selling prices. This provides direct evidence that sellers are more sensitive to item-related attributes, while buyers are more sensitive to expenditure-related attributes.

### Study 3
This study further tested the theory by manipulating different dimensions of the item and expenditure.

*   **Method:** The experiment manipulated four dimensions:
    *   **Expenditure-related:** The game's base ticket price and a potential cash rebate for attendees.
    *   **Item-related (Experience):** The importance of the game and the expected "climate" in the stadium (e.g., how fun and exciting it would be).
*   **Results:** The findings were consistent with previous studies. The experience-related factors (game importance and stadium climate) had a greater impact on selling prices. The expenditure-related factors (base price and rebate) had a greater impact on buying prices. This reinforces the idea that buyers focus on the financial sacrifice, while sellers focus on the loss of the experience.

### Study 4
The final study was designed to show that the difference in focus occurs naturally and can be altered by shifting a person's attention. The hypothesis was that buyers do not typically focus on the benefits of owning the item, but if prompted to do so, their buying prices would increase and become more similar to selling prices.

*   **Method:** Participants were divided into three groups: a standard buyer group, a standard seller group, and a "buyer-focus" group. The buyer-focus group was asked to read a vivid description of the benefits of attending the game *before* stating their buying price.
*   **Results:** The standard groups replicated the usual price gap. However, the buying prices from the "buyer-focus" group were significantly higher than those of the standard buyers and were statistically indistinguishable from the sellers' prices. This supports the claim that buyers' lower prices are partly due to their natural tendency *not* to focus on what they stand to gain, a tendency that can be reversed by explicitly shifting their attention.

### Conclusion
The authors conclude that the gap between buying and selling prices can be explained by a fundamental difference in focus. Sellers anchor their valuation on the item they are forgoing, while buyers anchor on the money they must spend. This "focus on the forgone" framework provides a more nuanced explanation than simple loss aversion, suggesting that the valuation gap is driven by the different inputs that buyers and sellers naturally use when constructing their prices. The research demonstrates that this difference in perspective systematically alters how value is perceived in a transaction.
