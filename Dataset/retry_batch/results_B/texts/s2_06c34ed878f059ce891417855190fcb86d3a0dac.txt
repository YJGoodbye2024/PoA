## 1. Official Abstract
This meta-analysis reviews 208 laboratory studies of acute psychological stressors and tests a theoretical model delineating conditions capable of eliciting cortisol responses. Psychological stressors increased cortisol levels; however, effects varied widely across tasks. Consistent with the theoretical model, motivated performance tasks elicited cortisol responses if they were uncontrollable or characterized by social-evaluative threat (task performance could be negatively judged by others), when methodological factors and other stressor characteristics were controlled for. Tasks containing both uncontrollable and social-evaluative elements were associated with the largest cortisol and adrenocorticotropin hormone changes and the longest times to recovery. These findings are consistent with the animal literature on the physiological effects of uncontrollable social threat and contradict the belief that cortisol is responsive to all types of stressors.

### Detailed Interpretation

This highly influential 2004 paper by Sally S. Dickerson and Margaret E. Kemeny presented a landmark meta-analysis that synthesized the findings from 208 laboratory studies to determine which types of acute psychological stressors reliably activate the human hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, as measured by cortisol release. The central thesis of the paper is that not all stressors are created equal in their ability to provoke a cortisol response. The authors proposed and tested a theoretical model suggesting that cortisol is specifically responsive to stressors that threaten the social self.

The key finding of their meta-analysis was that laboratory tasks featuring **social-evaluative threat** (the potential for negative judgment by others) and **uncontrollability** were the most potent and reliable elicitors of a cortisol response. Tasks that combined both of these elements, such as those requiring participants to give a speech or perform difficult mental arithmetic in front of an audience, produced the largest and most consistent cortisol increases. This provided a crucial integration for the field, explaining the wide variability in cortisol reactivity that had been observed across different types of lab stressors.

The authors argued that this specific sensitivity to social threat is consistent with an evolutionary perspective. The physiological stress response, they contend, is particularly attuned to threats to one's social standing and acceptance, which would have been critical for survival in human ancestral environments. The paper directly challenged the then-common belief that cortisol is a non-specific response to any demanding or arousing situation. Instead, Dickerson and Kemeny demonstrated that it is the *psychological meaning* of the situation—specifically, the threat to one's social self—that is the critical ingredient.

This work has had a profound impact on the field of psychoneuroendocrinology. It established a theoretical framework that has guided stress research for years. For instance, the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST), a paradigm that incorporates both social evaluation and uncontrollability, is widely recognized as the gold standard for reliably inducing a cortisol response in laboratory settings, a fact underscored by this meta-analysis. The paper's findings are frequently cited in studies examining the physiological effects of social anxiety, public speaking fears, and performance pressure. It provided a clear, evidence-based model for understanding why some situations feel more "stressful" than others and how this psychological experience translates into a specific, measurable biological response.
