## 1. Official Abstract
> Presents a self-presentation approach to the study of social anxiety that proposes that social anxiety arises when individuals are motivated to make a preferred impression on real or imagined audiences, but perceive or imagine unsatisfactory evaluative reactions from subjectively important audiences. The authors presume that specific situational and dispositional antecedents of social anxiety operate by influencing people's motivation to impress others and their expectations of satisfactorily doing so. In contrast to drive models of anxiety but consistent with social learning theory, it is argued that the cognitive state of the individual mediates both affective arousal and behavior. The traditional inverted-U relation between anxiety and performance is reexamined in this light. Counseling implications are considered, including the recommendation that treatments be tailored to specific types of self-presentational problems.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on analyses from citing academic sources.

Schlenker and Leary's 1982 paper introduced a seminal self-presentational model of social anxiety, which posits that social anxiety is an outcome of a specific cognitive and motivational state. The core of the model is the idea that social anxiety emerges when two conditions are met simultaneously: an individual is motivated to make a particular, desired impression on others, but they doubt their ability to successfully achieve that impression. This framework shifted the focus toward the cognitive appraisals involved in social interactions.

**The Self-Presentational Model:**
The theory can be conceptualized as an interplay between motivation and expectation. Social anxiety is not seen simply as a fear of social situations, but as a direct result of the perceived gap between one's self-presentational goals and one's perceived ability to meet them. The intensity of the social anxiety experienced is theorized to be a function of how motivated a person is to make a good impression and how low their expectation of success is.

Key tenets of the model as described by secondary sources include:
*   **Motivation to Impress:** People are generally motivated to convey a certain impression of themselves to others to gain social acceptance and avoid rejection. Social anxiety is heightened in situations where making a good impression is particularly important.
*   **Doubt in Ability (Low Efficacy):** The second crucial component is the individual's doubt or lack of confidence in their ability to make the desired impression. This is a subjective assessment; a person may be perfectly capable in reality, but if they *believe* they will fail, anxiety can arise. This explains why individuals with social anxiety often have negative self-beliefs and expect negative evaluations from others.
*   **Cognitive Mediation:** The model is consistent with social learning theory by arguing that an individual's cognitive state is the mediator for both emotional arousal (the feeling of anxiety) and subsequent behavior (such as avoidance). It refines purely cognitive approaches by specifying that the cognitions underlying social anxiety are specifically about how one is perceived and evaluated by others.

**Impact and Integration:**
Schlenker and Leary's model has been highly influential and serves as a foundational component of more recent cognitive-behavioral models of social anxiety. For example, later theories incorporate the idea that socially anxious individuals have a strong desire to be accepted but believe they cannot meet the high performance standards they perceive others to hold. The self-presentational theory provides a framework that encompasses other findings, such as the link between social skills deficits and social anxiety, by explaining that poor skills can lead to a lower expectation of making a good impression, thus causing anxiety. The model also has direct implications for counseling, suggesting that treatments should be designed to address the specific self-presentational problems an individual faces, whether it's reducing the overwhelming motivation to impress or increasing their confidence in their ability to do so.
