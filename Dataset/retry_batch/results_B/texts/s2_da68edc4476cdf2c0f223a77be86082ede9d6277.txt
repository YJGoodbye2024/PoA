## 1. Official Abstract
"Evidence from four studies demonstrates that social observers tend to perceive a “false consensus” with respect to the relative commonness of their own responses. A related bias was shown to exist in the observers' social inferences. Thus, raters estimated particular responses to be relatively common and relatively unrevealing concerning the actors' distinguishing personal dispositions when the responses in question were similar to the raters' own responses; by contrast, responses dissimilar to the raters' own were estimated to be relatively uncommon and highly revealing of the actors' personal dispositions. These results were obtained both in questionnaire studies presenting subjects with hypothetical situations and choices and in authentic conflict situations. The implications of these findings for our understanding of social perception phenomena and for our analysis of the divergent perceptions of actors and observers are discussed. Finally, cognitive and perceptual mechanisms are proposed which might account for distortions in perceived consensus and for corresponding biases in social inference and attributional processes."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. The following synopsis is structured according to the paper's original sections.

### **Introduction**
The authors introduce the concept that all people act as "intuitive psychologists," constantly making judgments about the causes and meanings of behavior. A critical component of this intuitive psychology is estimating the commonness or deviance of a particular behavior. The paper proposes that individuals' own choices, judgments, and behaviors serve as a primary anchor for these estimations, leading to a systematic bias. The central thesis is that people "tend to perceive a 'false consensus'—to see their own behavioral choices and judgments as relatively common and appropriate to existing circumstances while viewing alternative responses as uncommon, deviant, or inappropriate." This egocentric bias is expected to influence not only perceptions of consensus but also the attributions people make about others; a behavior seen as common is attributed to situational factors, whereas a behavior seen as deviant is attributed to the actor's personal disposition.

### **Study 1: Four Hypothetical Scenarios**
*   **Methods:** In the first study, 320 Stanford undergraduate students were presented with one of four short, hypothetical stories. Each story described a situation that culminated in a clear choice between two behavioral options. For example, one story involved deciding whether to pay a traffic ticket or challenge it in court. Participants were asked to do three things: 1) estimate the percentage of their peers who would choose each of the two options, 2) indicate which option they would personally choose, and 3) describe the attributes of a typical person who would choose option A and a typical person who would choose option B.
*   **Results:** The results strongly supported the false consensus hypothesis. For all four scenarios, "subjects who endorsed a particular alternative themselves estimated that alternative to be a more probable response for 'people in general' than did subjects who endorsed the other alternative." Furthermore, participants made more extreme and confident predictions about the personality traits of people who chose the option they *didn't* choose. In other words, they saw the alternative choice as more revealing of someone's personal character than their own choice, which they perceived as the more "normal" or situationally appropriate response.

### **Study 2: A Questionnaire Survey**
*   **Methods:** This study aimed to generalize the findings from Study 1 using a different format. 80 Stanford undergraduates completed a 35-item questionnaire covering personal traits, problems, preferences, and expectations. For each item, they first chose one of two descriptive categories for themselves (e.g., "shy" vs. "outgoing") and then estimated the percentage of "college students in general" who would fall into each category.
*   **Results:** The false consensus effect was again clearly demonstrated. For 32 of the 34 relevant items, participants who placed themselves in a specific category consistently estimated the percentage of their peers in that same category to be higher than did those who placed themselves in the alternative category. For instance, students who described themselves as competitive gave a higher percentage estimate for how many other students are competitive compared to the estimates given by those who described themselves as cooperative.

### **Study 3: The "Sandwich Board" Study**
*   **Methods:** This study moved from hypothetical scenarios to a real-world behavioral choice. Stanford undergraduates were asked if they would be willing to walk around campus for 30 minutes wearing a large sandwich-board sign that read "Eat at Joe's." They were told it was for a study on communication, with the details to be revealed later. After either agreeing or refusing to wear the sign, they were asked to estimate the percentage of peers who would also agree or refuse and to make inferences about the personalities of those who made the same and opposite choices.
*   **Results:** The findings were consistent with the previous studies. Students who *agreed* to wear the sign believed that a majority (62%) of their peers would also agree. In contrast, students who *refused* to wear the sign believed that a majority (67%) of their peers would also refuse. As predicted, participants also made stronger dispositional attributions about those who made the opposite choice. Refusers, for example, made more extreme judgments about the personal characteristics of those who agreed to wear the sign, and vice-versa.

### **Study 4: Ratings of Conflict and Dissensus**
*   **Methods:** The final study involved participants who had previously taken part in a different experiment where they had to choose between cooperative and competitive strategies in a prisoner's dilemma game. They were later asked to rate the other players from that experiment.
*   **Results:** The false consensus effect held even in this context of direct social interaction and conflict. Participants' own choices in the game biased their perceptions of how common that choice was among the other players. They also made more confident and extreme judgments about the personal dispositions of players who had made a different choice from their own.

### **General Discussion**
The authors conclude that the "false consensus effect" is a robust and widespread bias. They propose several mechanisms that might cause it.
1.  **Selective Exposure and Availability:** People tend to associate with others who are similar to them, leading to a biased sample of "consensus" information from which they generalize. One's own position is also more readily available in one's mind, making it seem more common.
2.  **Causal Attribution:** People often feel their own choices are a logical and objective response to situational factors. Therefore, they assume that other rational people in the same situation would act similarly.
3.  **Ambiguity Resolution:** In ambiguous situations, people may interpret the circumstances in a way that favors their own behavioral choice. They then assume others are responding to the same interpretation, thus overestimating consensus for their behavior.

The paper concludes by emphasizing that this egocentric bias has significant implications for social interaction, leading to misunderstandings and the tendency to view those who disagree with us as somehow deviant or possessing unusual personality traits.
