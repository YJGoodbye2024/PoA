## 1. Official Abstract
Explanations of Milgram's findings have traditionally focused on a priori qualities of the situation and/or the actors, which are presumed to operate more or less simultaneously to produce obedience or defiance. Such a perspective largely overlooks the unfolding and evolving nature of both obedience and defiance. This article develops a contrasting, sequential model. A specific hypothesis is advanced: The sooner in the course of the experiment a subject begins to show notable resistance, the more likely he will be to end up defiant. Reanalysis of data collected by Milgram for his “Bridgeport” condition supports this hypothesis.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on an analysis of how the paper is described and cited in other academic works.

**Critique of Static Models and Proposal of a Sequential Model**
Modigliani and Rochat's 1995 paper serves as a significant critique of traditional interpretations of Stanley Milgram's famous obedience experiments. The authors argue that previous explanations focused too heavily on static, "a priori" qualities of the situation or the participants' personalities, assuming these factors worked together simultaneously to produce either obedience or defiance. In contrast, Modigliani and Rochat proposed a "sequential model," which reframes obedience and defiance not as single decisions, but as the outcomes of an evolving process that unfolds over time. This perspective emphasizes the "unfolding and evolving nature" of the encounter between the participant and the authority figure. The paper was one of the first to systematically analyze the verbal interchanges and interaction sequences between the participant and the experimenter using Milgram's own archival audio recordings.

**The "Timing of Resistance" Hypothesis**
The central hypothesis of the paper is that the timing of a participant's resistance is a key predictor of their final outcome. Specifically, "The sooner in the course of the experiment a subject begins to show notable resistance, the more likely he will be to end up defiant". To test this, the authors re-analyzed data from Milgram's "Bridgeport" condition, a variation of the experiment conducted in a modest office building to reduce the prestige associated with Yale University. Their analysis of the audiotapes involved categorizing participants' verbal protests into a scale of increasing intensity. The findings supported their hypothesis, showing that individuals who would ultimately defy the experimenter began to voice strong protests earlier in the shock sequence.

**Contribution and Influence**
The paper's primary contribution is its shift in focus from *why* people obey to *how* the process of obedience or defiance dynamically unfolds. It highlights the concept of a "slippery slope," where gradual commitment makes it progressively harder for a participant to stop once they have started. The authors suggest that the main goal for participants becomes escaping the "unpleasant situation" and the building stress, rather than making a purely moral stand.

By calling for "closer empirical analyses of the psychological and interpersonal dynamics that unravel over the course of encounters with an authority," Modigliani and Rochat's work opened up a new line of inquiry. It influenced subsequent researchers to explore the recordings from Milgram's archives and analyze the rhetorical and argumentative nature of the interactions, viewing them as a contest where the participant tries to argue their way out while the experimenter provides counter-arguments to ensure continuation. This process-oriented view has become a crucial part of understanding the complex dynamics at play in the Milgram paradigm.
