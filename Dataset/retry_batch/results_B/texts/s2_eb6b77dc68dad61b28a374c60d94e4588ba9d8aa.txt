James R. Averill's "Illusions of anger" is a theoretical paper that forwards a social constructionist view of emotion. The central thesis is that anger is not a primary, biological, or objective phenomenon but rather a subjective experience constructed and governed by cultural norms and social rules. Averill argues that emotions are transitory social roles, and anger specifically functions as a "socially constituted syndrome" that people use to interpret a situation and respond to it. This perspective places anger within a moral framework, where it is used to explain and justify behaviors according to the accepted rules of a society.

Based on extensive citation in other academic works, the core insights of the chapter can be summarized as follows:

**Anger as a Social Role:**
Averill proposes that an emotion is a "transitory social role" that includes an individual's appraisal of a situation and is interpreted as a passion rather than a deliberate action. This framework suggests that when people experience and express anger, they are, in effect, performing a role that is scripted by their culture. These "emotion rules" become so ingrained that they feel like second nature, followed without conscious effort. The purpose of these emotional roles is to reinforce a society's norms and values.

**The "Rules" of Anger:**
A key contribution of the paper is its identification of the specific social rules that govern the experience and expression of anger in American culture. Several sources cite these rules, including:
*   A person has the right or even a duty to become angry at intentional wrongdoing. Anger is also permissible for unintentional misdeeds if they are correctable (e.g., resulting from negligence or carelessness).
*   Anger should be directed only at entities that can be held responsible for their actions, such as other people, institutions, or oneself. It should not be displaced onto innocent third parties.
*   The response should be proportional to the offense.
*   The ultimate goal of functional anger is corrective action and problem-solving, aiming to rectify a wrong.

**Anger as a Narrative and Functional Tool:**
The paper posits that emotions have narrative features. An "anger narrative" serves the function of correcting wrongs and upholding social standards. This view contrasts with theories that see anger as primarily a disruptive or aggressive force. Instead, Averill frames functional anger as a tool for legitimizing a response to an offense and motivating actions that can restore social order. While it can facilitate aggression, its fundamental purpose within the social system is to manage and respond to perceived injustices.

In summary, "Illusions of anger" argues that the experience of anger is an illusion in the sense that it is not a raw, uncontrollable force. Instead, it is a highly structured, socially meaningful performance guided by implicit cultural rules that serve to regulate social interactions and uphold a moral order.
