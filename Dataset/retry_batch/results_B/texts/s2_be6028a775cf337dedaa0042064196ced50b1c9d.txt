## 1. Official Abstract
"This article tests these relationships using communication audit data from 218 employees in the oil industry. Using mediation analysis and structural equation modeling, we found that quality of information predicted trust of one’s coworkers and supervisors while adequacy of information predicted one’s trust of top management. Trust of coworkers, supervisors, and top management influenced perceptions of organizational openness, which in turn influenced employees’ ratings of their own level of involvement in the organization’s goals. This study suggests that the relationship between communication and trust is complex, and that simple strategies focusing on either quality or quantity of information may be ineffective for dealing with all members in an organization."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
### Introduction
The paper begins by highlighting the long-recognized importance of trust as a foundation for organizational effectiveness. While numerous studies have demonstrated that communication is central to developing and maintaining trust, the authors argue that many questions about the specific mechanisms of this relationship remain unanswered. To address this gap, the study poses three research questions aimed at better understanding the specific linkages between the quality and quantity of information, different levels of trust (in coworkers, supervisors, and top management), and the resulting effect on employee involvement. The overarching theory guiding the research is that an organization's communication practices influence performance outcomes by fostering trust, which in turn shapes perceptions of organizational openness and enhances employee involvement in achieving company goals.

### Methods
To investigate these relationships, the researchers analyzed data from an International Communication Association (ICA) survey completed by 218 employees within the oil industry. They employed statistical techniques, specifically mediation analysis and structural equation modeling, to test a proposed model. This model hypothesized how different facets of communication (quality and quantity) would predict trust in different organizational actors (coworkers, supervisors, and top management) and how this trust would then influence organizational openness and employee involvement. The study explicitly tested two main hypotheses: that the quality of information would be positively associated with higher trust, and that receiving a sufficient quantity of information would also be positively associated with higher trust.

### Results
The analysis revealed distinct pathways for how communication affects trust. The *quality* of information—defined by its timeliness, accuracy, and usefulness—was a significant predictor of an employee's trust in their coworkers and direct supervisors. In contrast, the *adequacy* or *quantity* of information was the key predictor of trust in top management.

The study further found that trust across all three levels (coworkers, supervisors, and top management) directly and positively influenced employees' perceptions of organizational openness. This perception of openness then served as a crucial mediator, directly influencing the employees' self-reported level of involvement and engagement with the organization's goals.

### Discussion & Conclusion
The authors conclude that the relationship between communication and trust is more complex than often assumed. Simple communication strategies that focus on only one dimension, such as increasing the sheer quantity of information, may not be universally effective. The findings imply that to build trust effectively, communication strategies must be tailored. To foster trust at the peer and supervisory level, organizations should prioritize delivering high-quality, accurate, and useful information. However, to build trust in senior leadership, it is crucial to provide a sufficient *quantity* of information, giving employees the context and rationale behind major decisions. Ultimately, by successfully building trust through these targeted communication efforts, organizations can cultivate a climate of openness that encourages greater employee involvement and dedication to organizational objectives.
