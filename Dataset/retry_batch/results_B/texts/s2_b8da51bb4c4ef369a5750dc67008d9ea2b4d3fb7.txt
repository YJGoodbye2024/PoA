## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on a critical analysis of the paper as cited in related academic literature.

The 2004 paper by Gleason, Jensen-Campbell, and Richardson is a significant contribution to the understanding of personality and aggression in adolescents. It specifically isolates **Agreeableness** from the Big Five personality traits as a primary predictor of aggressive behaviors. The study's core assertion, reinforced consistently in subsequent research, is that a strong **inverse relationship** exists between agreeableness and aggression; higher levels of agreeableness are linked to lower levels of aggression.

The research was conducted through a multi-method approach involving two separate studies with middle school children.

*   **Study 1 Findings:** The first part of the research established that agreeableness was negatively associated with both **direct aggression** (e.g., physical or verbal assaults) and **indirect aggression** (e.g., social exclusion, rumor-spreading). Notably, the paper concluded that this link was most pronounced for direct forms of aggression.

*   **Study 2 Findings:** The second study expanded on this by examining the relationship between agreeableness and the social-cognitive processes underlying aggression. It found that agreeableness could predict peer reports of aggression and related social cognitions. A key finding from this study was the mediating role of aggression in the relationship between agreeableness and overall adjustment, as reported by teachers. This suggests that low agreeableness leads to higher aggression, which in turn leads to poorer social and academic adjustment.

Other researchers frequently cite this paper to support the broader consensus that, among the Big Five traits (which also include Neuroticism, Extraversion, Conscientiousness, and Openness), Agreeableness is one of the most consistent and strongest predictors of aggressive behavior. While Neuroticism is also often linked to aggression, it is thought to operate through emotional instability and reactivity. In contrast, the Gleason et al. study helps frame low Agreeableness as being associated with a reduced motivation to maintain positive interpersonal relationships, which manifests as higher aggression. The paper's findings are considered robust and are often cited alongside meta-analyses in the field that confirm the strong inverse relationship between agreeableness and antisocial behaviors.
