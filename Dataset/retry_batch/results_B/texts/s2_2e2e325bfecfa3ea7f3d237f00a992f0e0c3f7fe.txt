## 1. Official Abstract
"Experienced ease of recall was found to qualify the implications of recalled content. Ss who had to recall 12 examples of assertive (unassertive) behaviors, which was difficult, rated themselves as less assertive (less unassertive) than subjects who had to recall 6 examples, which was easy. In fact, Ss reported higher assertiveness after recalling 12 unassertive rather than 12 assertive behaviors. Thus, self-assessments only reflected the implications of recalled content if recall was easy. The impact of ease of recall was eliminated when its informational value was discredited by a misattribution manipulation. The informative functions of subjective experiences are discussed."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible. The following is a synopsis of the paper, organized by its original sections. The paper was published in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1991, Vol. 61, No. 2, pages 195-202.

### **Introduction**
The paper revisits the "availability heuristic" first proposed by Tversky and Kahneman (1973), which suggests people judge the frequency of an event "by the ease with which instances or associations come to mind". The authors argue that previous research has confounded two distinct factors: the *amount* of recalled content and the subjective *ease* of recalling it. This paper aims to disentangle these two factors. The core hypothesis is that the subjective experience of ease or difficulty of recall is itself a source of information that people use when making judgments. The authors propose a series of experiments where the implications of the recalled content are pitted against the implications of the experienced ease of retrieval.

### **Experiment 1: Assertiveness**
The first experiment was designed to test if the difficulty of recall would influence self-judgments of assertiveness.

*   **Method:** Participants were asked to recall either 6 or 12 examples of their own assertive or unassertive behaviors. The assumption was that recalling 6 examples would be experienced as easy, while recalling 12 would be difficult. After the recall task, they rated their own assertiveness.
*   **Results:** As predicted, participants who recalled 6 examples of assertive behavior (an easy task) rated themselves as more assertive than those who recalled 6 examples of unassertive behavior. However, this pattern reversed for the difficult task. Participants who struggled to recall 12 examples of assertive behavior rated themselves as *less* assertive than those who struggled to recall 12 examples of unassertive behavior. The study concluded that "subjects rated themselves as more assertive after describing examples of assertive rather than unassertive behaviors only if the recall task was easy. When the recall task was difficult, their self-rating was opposite to the implications of recalled content". This demonstrates that the subjective experience of difficulty provided information that overrode the content of what was recalled.

### **Experiment 2: Generalizing the Findings**
This experiment aimed to replicate the findings of Experiment 1 and ensure they were not specific to the trait of assertiveness. It followed a similar design but did not introduce new theoretical elements, serving to confirm the initial results. The results from this experiment supported the findings of the first, indicating that the phenomenon was robust.

### **Experiment 3: The Role of Perceived Diagnosticity**
The third experiment introduced a misattribution manipulation to test whether people would stop relying on the "ease of retrieval" if they believed it was caused by an external factor.

*   **Method:** Participants were again asked to recall 6 or 12 examples of assertive or unassertive behaviors. This time, however, some participants were told that background music they were listening to would either facilitate or inhibit memory recall. This was intended to make them attribute their experience of ease or difficulty to the music rather than to their own personality.
*   **Results:** When the informational value of the retrieval experience was discredited by the music, its influence was eliminated. For instance, if a participant found it easy to recall 6 assertive behaviors but was told the music was helping them, they discounted the experience of ease and did not rate themselves as particularly assertive. The results showed that "discrediting the informational value of experienced ease of retrieval may lead to self-assessments that are opposite to the implications of recalled content under some conditions". This confirmed that people actively use the subjective experience of recall as a source of diagnostic information, and when its diagnosticity is called into question, they cease to rely on it.

### **General Discussion**
The authors conclude that judgment is influenced not only by the content that is recalled but also by the subjective experience of the recall process itself. The key finding is that when recall is difficult, the experience of that difficulty can lead to judgments that are opposite to the implications of the recalled content. For example, struggling to list many instances of one's own assertive behavior leads to the conclusion that one must not be very assertive.

The paper firmly establishes that the "ease of retrieval" is a distinct piece of information used in judgments. The authors state, "we conclude that people not only consider what they recall in making a judgment but also use the ease or difficulty with which that content comes to mind as an additional source of information". This work refines the original availability heuristic by isolating the phenomenal experience of ease from the number of recalled instances.
