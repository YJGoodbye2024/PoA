## 1. Official 's'Abstract
This meta-analysis of 172 studies (N = 2,263 anxious, N = 1,768 nonanxious) examined the boundary conditions of threat-related attentional biases in anxiety. Overall, the results show that the bias is reliably demonstrated with different experimental paradigms and under a variety of experimental conditions, but that it is only an effect size of d = 0.45. Although processes requiring conscious perception of threat contribute to the bias, a significant bias is also observed with stimuli outside awareness. The bias is of comparable magnitude across different types of anxious populations (individuals with different clinical disorders, high-anxious nonclinical individuals, anxious children and adults) and is not observed in nonanxious individuals. Empirical and clinical implications as well as future directions for research are discussed.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. A detailed interpretation has been compiled from the abstract and accessible portions of the full text.

This highly influential 2007 paper by Yair Bar-Haim and his colleagues presented a comprehensive meta-analysis of the phenomenon known as threat-related attentional bias in anxious individuals. At the time, numerous studies had investigated the idea that anxious individuals' attentional systems are uniquely sensitive to threatening stimuli in their environment, but the findings were varied. This paper sought to synthesize the existing literature to determine the reliability and boundaries of this effect.

The core of the study was a meta-analysis of 172 individual studies, which included a large combined sample of 2,263 anxious participants and 1,768 nonanxious control participants. The anxious groups were diverse, including individuals with diagnosed clinical anxiety disorders (like GAD, social phobia, PTSD, and panic disorder), non-clinical individuals with high self-reported anxiety, and both children and adults.

The main findings of the meta-analysis were significant:
*   **Reliable but Moderate Effect**: Across the many studies, experimental methods, and types of stimuli, there is a reliable and significant attentional bias towards threat in anxious individuals. However, the overall size of this effect is moderate, with a calculated effect size (Cohen's d) of 0.45.
*   **Conscious and Unconscious Bias**: The bias is not solely dependent on conscious, deliberate processing of threats. The analysis found that a significant attentional bias exists even when threatening stimuli are presented outside of the participants' conscious awareness. This suggests that automatic, pre-attentive processes play a role.
*   **Consistency Across Anxious Populations**: The magnitude of the attentional bias was found to be comparable across different types of anxiety. It did not significantly differ between adults and children, or between individuals with specific clinical disorders and those with high non-clinical trait anxiety.
*   **Absence in Nonanxious Individuals**: Crucially, the meta-analysis confirmed that this threat-related attentional bias is specific to anxious individuals and is not observed in nonanxious control groups.
*   **Stimulus Type**: The analysis also investigated whether the type of threatening stimulus mattered. It found no significant difference in the effect size between studies that used threatening words and those that used threatening pictures.

In conclusion, the paper provided robust, large-scale evidence that attentional bias toward threat is a core feature of anxiety. The authors discuss the empirical and clinical implications of these findings, solidifying the bias as a key mechanism in the maintenance of anxiety disorders and a potential target for treatment.
