## 1. Official Abstract
> Feelings-as-information theory conceptualizes the role of subjective experiences – including moods, emotions, metacognitive experiences, and bodily sensations – in judgment. It assumes that people attend to their feelings as a source of information, with different feelings providing different types of information. Whereas feelings elicited by the target of judgment provide valid information, feelings that are due to an unrelated influence can lead us astray. The use of feelings as a source of information follows the same principles as the use of any other information.

### Detailed Interpretation

**Core Concept: How Do I Feel About It?**
Feelings-as-Information Theory, also known as the affect-as-information hypothesis, posits that individuals use their subjective feelings and emotions as a source of information when making judgments and decisions. Instead of relying solely on the specific attributes of a target, people often simplify the evaluation process by asking themselves, "How do I feel about it?". The theory proposes that these feelings—which include moods, emotions, bodily sensations, and the subjective ease or difficulty of thought (metacognitive experiences)—serve as informational cues that guide subsequent cognitive processes and behaviors.

**The Informational Value of Feelings**
According to the theory, different feelings provide different types of information. For instance, negative affective states often signal that a situation is problematic or unsatisfactory, prompting more vigilant and effortful processing. Positive emotions, conversely, may suggest a benign environment. The theory treats feelings as a form of embodied information that influences key cognitive functions like judgment, processing style, and memory. This approach provides a unified framework for understanding how these varied subjective experiences consistently influence judgment.

**Misattribution and Context Sensitivity**
A central tenet of the theory is that the influence of a feeling depends on its perceived informational value. Individuals do not necessarily analyze the source of their feelings. Consequently, feelings that are incidental—meaning they are unrelated to the object of judgment—can be misattributed as a genuine reaction to it, leading to biased evaluations.

However, if a person becomes aware that their feelings stem from an incidental source (e.g., realizing their bad mood is due to the weather), the informational value of that feeling is discounted or undermined. In such cases, the influence of the feeling on the judgment is reduced or eliminated entirely. This highlights that inferences drawn from feelings are highly context-sensitive and malleable.

**Application to Metacognitive Experiences**
The theory extends beyond moods and emotions to include metacognitive experiences, such as the feeling of ease or difficulty when processing information or recalling something from memory. This "processing fluency" is itself a source of information. For example, information that is easy to process may be judged as more true, popular, or trustworthy. However, the meaning of this fluency is flexible; while ease of recall might suggest a high frequency of past events, it could also be interpreted as indicating that the events happened more recently. The specific inference depends on the lay theories an individual applies in that context. Like emotions, the influence of these metacognitive experiences is also eliminated if they are attributed to an incidental source.
