## 1. Official Abstract
"Although observers of human behavior have long been aware that people regularly struggle with internal conflict when deciding whether to behave responsibly or indulge in impulsivity, psychologists and economists did not begin to empirically investigate this type of want/should conflict until recently. In this paper, we review and synthesize the latest research on want/should conflict, focusing our attention on the findings from an empirical literature on the topic that has blossomed over the last 15 years. We then turn to a discussion of how individuals and policy makers can use what has been learned about want/should conflict to help decision makers select far-sighted options."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. The paper is a review that synthesizes findings on the internal conflict between what people *want* to do (impulsive, short-term desires) and what they feel they *should* do (rational, long-term interests). It explores the cognitive underpinnings of this conflict and reviews situational factors that influence decisions, proposing interventions to encourage more far-sighted choices.

The analysis is structured according to the paper's original sections:

### **A Cognitive Model of Want/Should Conflict**
The authors propose that many decisions are experienced as a conflict between two internal selves: a "want self" and a "should self". The "want self" is described as impulsive and emotional, while the "should self" is rational and deliberative. The paper builds on the work of Metcalfe and Mischel (1999) by linking this conflict to "hot" and "cool" cognitive systems. The "want self" aligns with the hot system, which is "emotional, impulsive, and reflexive," while the "should self" aligns with the cool system, which is "cognitive, forward-looking, and slow." The paper also connects this to dual-process models in judgment and decision-making, where System 1 is intuitive (want) and System 2 is deliberative (should).

### **Situational Factors That Influence Want/Should Conflict**
This section details the contextual factors that determine whether the "want" or "should" self prevails in a decision.

*   **Choice Timing and the Magnitude of Conflict:** People are more likely to favor "should" options when making decisions for the future, a phenomenon known as "dynamic inconsistency." For example, a study on grocery orders found that customers purchased more "should" items (like vegetables) and fewer "want" items (like ice cream) when placing orders further in advance of the delivery date.
*   **Construal Levels:** Drawing on Construal Level Theory, the paper argues that decisions for the distant future are construed at a higher, more abstract level, which favors the "should" self. In contrast, immediate decisions are construed at a lower, more concrete level, activating the "want" self. Research shows this alignment: "the interests of the should self are naturally aligned with higher level construal, whereas the interests of the want self are naturally aligned with lower level construal."
*   **Cognitive Load and Ego Depletion:** When cognitive resources are taxed or depleted, the impulsive "want" self is more likely to dominate. The paper cites studies showing that individuals under cognitive load are more likely to choose unhealthy snacks over healthy ones. Similarly, exerting self-control in one task can deplete a person's ability to make "should" choices in a subsequent task.
*   **The "What the Hell" Effect:** This effect describes a cycle where an initial failure to meet a "should" goal leads to subsequent abandonment of that goal. For instance, a dieter who eats one cookie is more likely to then eat many more, as the initial lapse makes further indulgence seem less costly.
*   **Licensing:** Engaging in a "should" behavior can "license" a person to subsequently indulge in a "want" behavior. The paper notes a key distinction: this effect primarily occurs when the initial "should" action is voluntary. If forced, it does not produce the same licensing effect.
*   **Joint Versus Separate Evaluation:** "Should" options tend to be favored when choices are evaluated simultaneously (jointly) rather than one at a time (separately). When evaluated in isolation, the affective, "want" features of an option are more salient. In joint evaluation, more rational and comparable attributes—hallmarks of the "should" self—become the focus.

### **How Can We Reduce Short-Sighted Decision Making?**
The final section translates the research findings into practical interventions for individuals and policymakers to promote "should" choices.

*   **Interventions on the Individual Level:**
    *   **Commitment Devices:** These are tools that allow an individual's "should" self to constrain their future "want" self. Examples include voluntarily placing money in a savings account with withdrawal penalties or using software that blocks internet access. The authors note that "many people are willing to pay a price to take up commitment devices in order to help their deliberative should selves overcome the impulsive desires of their want selves."
    *   **Temptation Bundling:** This strategy involves linking a "want" activity with a "should" activity. For example, only allowing oneself to watch a favorite TV show while exercising at the gym.

*   **Interventions on the Policy Level:**
    *   **Choice Architecture:** Policymakers can design environments that nudge people toward "should" behaviors without restricting freedom of choice. This includes setting beneficial defaults (e.g., opt-out retirement savings plans) and requiring active, explicit choices for "want" options.
    *   **Increasing the Salience of "Should" Attributes:** Policies can be designed to make the long-term consequences of decisions more vivid, such as by displaying the "tar" content of cigarettes or the lifetime cost of a high-interest loan.
