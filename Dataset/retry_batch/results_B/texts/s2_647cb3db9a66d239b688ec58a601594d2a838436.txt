### **Introduction**
The paper begins by tracing the origins of social facilitation research to Norman Triplett's 1898 study on cyclists, which found that the presence of others improved performance. Strauss introduces the central theme: a review of the theories and empirical findings related to how the presence of an audience or co-actors affects motor task performance. The review highlights the ongoing debate and often contradictory results within the field.

### **Theoretical Explanations**
Strauss outlines several major theoretical models proposed to explain social facilitation.

*   **Zajonc's Drive Theory (1965):** This foundational model posits that the "mere presence" of others increases an individual's arousal or "drive." This heightened arousal enhances the performance of simple, well-learned tasks (dominant responses) but impairs the performance of complex or novel tasks. Zajonc later suggested this arousal stems from an innate "alertness or preparedness for the unexpected" when others are present.
*   **Evaluation Apprehension Model (Cottrell, 1972):** This theory argues that it is not the mere presence of others but the fear of being evaluated that causes increased arousal. Social facilitation effects are therefore expected only when the performer believes their actions are being judged.
*   **Distraction-Conflict Theory (Baron, 1986 & Sanders, 1981):** This model suggests that the presence of others creates a conflict between attending to the task and attending to the audience. This conflict increases arousal, leading to the classic social facilitation effects. For simple tasks, the narrowed attention can be beneficial, but for complex tasks requiring broader attention, it is detrimental.
*   **Capacity Models (Manstead & Semin, 1980):** These theories focus on cognitive load. An audience is seen as an additional "attention-demanding factor." On tasks that are not cognitively demanding (automatic processes), the presence of others can focus attention and improve performance. However, on complex tasks that require significant cognitive control, the additional attentional demand from an audience overloads cognitive capacity and impairs performance.

### **Results of Research on Motor Tasks**
Strauss reviews the empirical evidence, categorizing motor tasks to analyze the findings more clearly. He notes that a comprehensive meta-analysis by Bond and Titus (1983) found the overall social facilitation effect to be "very small," explaining only 0.3–3% of performance variance.

The paper differentiates between three types of motor tasks:
*   **Coordination Tasks:** These are skill-based tasks requiring precision and control (e.g., balancing, pursuit rotor tasks). The research here is inconsistent. Findings show negative effects, particularly during learning or with less skilled participants, as well as positive or even no effects for well-learned tasks or skilled performers. Strauss suggests that theories based on cognitive capacity (like distraction-conflict or capacity models) are best suited to explain these varied results, as coordination tasks have high cognitive demands.
*   **Power and Stamina Tasks:** These are "condition-based" tasks that rely on strength and endurance (e.g., cycling, weightlifting). The review finds that performance on these tasks is generally facilitated by the presence of others. This aligns well with Zajonc's drive theory, where increased arousal energizes effort-based activities.
*   **Mixed Tasks:** Tasks that demand both coordination and conditioning simultaneously. The paper notes that existing models struggle to predict outcomes for these tasks.

### **Discussion and Conclusions**
Strauss concludes that no single theory can adequately explain all the findings in social facilitation research on motor tasks. The empirical results are often in direct contradiction to the predictions of major theoretical models. He argues for a more nuanced approach, suggesting that different types of motor tasks may require different theoretical explanations.

Specifically, performance on tasks high in conditioning demands seems to be facilitated by an audience, supporting activation-based theories like Zajonc's. In contrast, performance on tasks with high coordination demands is more likely to be inhibited, which is better explained by cognitive overload and capacity models.

Ultimately, Strauss concludes that the effect of the "mere presence" of others is generally weak. The decisive factor often appears to be whether an individual's performance can be identified and evaluated, lending strong support to the importance of "evaluation apprehension."
