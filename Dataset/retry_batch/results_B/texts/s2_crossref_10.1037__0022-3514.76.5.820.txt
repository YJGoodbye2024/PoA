The authors' review of the literature indicates that this 2-factor model captures robust structural properties of self-rated mood. Nevertheless, the evidence also indicates that the circumplex does not fit the data closely and needs to be refined. Most notably, the model's dimensions are not entirely independent; moreover, with the exception of Pleasantness-Unpleasantness, they are not completely bipolar. ## 2. Core Insights & Analysis

### **Introduction: The Affect Circumplex and Its Limitations**

The paper begins by revisiting the two-factor model of affect proposed by Watson and Tellegen in 1985, which itself was based on Russell's (1980) circumplex model. This model organizes affect around two primary dimensions: Pleasantness vs. Unpleasantness and Activation vs. Deactivation. The authors assert that while this two-factor structure is robust in research on self-rated mood, the circumplex model is an imperfect representation. Key problems are identified: the dimensions are not truly independent, and they are not perfectly bipolar (meaning the opposite of a term like "active" is not simply "inactive," but a more complex state). The data suggest a structure that lies somewhere between a simple two-factor model and a perfect circle (a circumplex).

The central aim of the paper is to refine this model by focusing on two specific, rotated dimensions within this structure: **Positive Activation (PA)** and **Negative Activation (NA)**. The authors propose that PA and NA are not just descriptive labels but are the subjective, experiential components of fundamental biobehavioral systems. Specifically, PA is linked to an "approach" system that motivates organisms to engage with their environment, while NA is tied to a "withdrawal" system that encourages disengagement from threats or aversive stimuli.

### **Structural and Evolutionary Evidence**

This section delves into the evidence supporting the PA/NA model. The authors re-analyze several datasets and review existing literature to demonstrate the structural integrity of the PA and NA dimensions. They argue that these two dimensions consistently emerge from factor analyses of mood terms.

From an evolutionary standpoint, the paper posits that these two systems are fundamental to survival. The "approach" system (linked to PA) facilitates engagement with rewarding stimuli such as food, social interaction, and opportunities for mating. It is characterized by feelings of energy, enthusiasm, and alertness. The "withdrawal" system (linked to NA) is a defensive system that responds to threats and punishment, characterized by feelings of distress, fear, and anger. The authors argue that having two distinct motivational systems is more adaptive than a single pleasure-pain dimension, as it allows for differentiated responses to the environment. An organism might need to suppress the withdrawal system to obtain a reward, or vice versa.

### **Psychobiological Evidence**

To bolster the claim that PA and NA are tied to distinct biological systems, the authors present psychobiological evidence. They connect the PA system to the **Behavioral Activation System (BAS)**, a concept from Jeffrey Gray's work. The BAS is associated with sensitivity to reward and is believed to be mediated by dopaminergic pathways in the brain. High PA is the subjective experience of an activated BAS.

Conversely, the NA system is linked to the **Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS)**, which is sensitive to punishment, novelty, and non-reward, leading to inhibition of behavior. This system is associated with feelings of anxiety, fear, and frustration. The paper suggests that the BIS provides the neurological substrate for the experience of Negative Activation. By linking PA and NA to these established neurobiological systems, the authors move their model from a purely descriptive one to one with explanatory power rooted in brain function.

### **Applications of the PA/NA Model**

In the final section, the authors demonstrate the utility of their model by applying it to several psychological and physiological phenomena:

*   **Circadian Rhythms:** The paper shows that PA and NA follow distinct daily patterns. PA generally rises throughout the morning, peaks in the early afternoon, and then declines into the evening. NA, in contrast, remains relatively low and stable for most of the day in healthy individuals, with a slight increase in the evening. This supports the idea that they are regulated by different underlying systems.
*   **Sleep:** The authors connect low PA to feelings of drowsiness and fatigue. They argue that the process of falling asleep is characterized by a significant drop in Positive Activation, while Negative Activation remains largely unchanged.
*   **Psychopathology:** The model is used to clarify the structure of mood disorders. Major Depressive Disorder is characterized primarily by low PA (anhedonia, loss of energy, lack of interest) and secondarily by high NA. In contrast, anxiety disorders are defined principally by high NA (fear, tension, worry), with PA being less affected. This differentiation helps explain the high comorbidity between anxiety and depression (as both can involve elevated NA) while also distinguishing their core features.

By applying the model to these diverse areas, the authors make a strong case for its broad utility in understanding human affect and its relationship to behavior and biology.
