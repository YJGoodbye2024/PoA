## 1. Official Abstract
The three most often-used performance measures in the cognitive and decision sciences are choice, response or decision time, and confidence. We develop a random walk/diffusion theory—2-stage dynamic signal detection (2DSD) theory—that accounts for all 3 measures using a common underlying process. The model uses a drift diffusion process to account for choice and decision time. To estimate confidence, we assume that evidence continues to accumulate after the choice. Judges then interrupt the process to categorize the accumulated evidence into a confidence rating. The model explains all known interrelationships between the 3 indices of performance. Furthermore, the model also accounts for the distributions of each variable in both a perceptual and general knowledge task. The dynamic nature of the model also reveals the moderating effects of time pressure on the accuracy of choice and confidence. Finally, the model specifies the optimal solution for giving the fastest choice and confidence rating for a given level of choice and confidence accuracy. Judges are found to act in a manner consistent with the optimal solution when making confidence judgments.

The 2010 paper, "Two-stage dynamic signal detection: a theory of choice, decision time, and confidence," by T. Pleskac and J. Busemeyer, introduces a unified theory to explain three fundamental components of decision-making: the choice itself, the time taken to make it, and the confidence in that choice. The authors propose the Two-Stage Dynamic Signal Detection (2DSD) model, which extends standard drift-diffusion and random walk models.

**Core Mechanism of the 2DSD Model**

The central innovation of the 2DSD model is its two-stage process for generating a confidence judgment.

1.  **Stage 1: Choice and Decision Time.** Like a standard drift-diffusion model, the 2DSD model posits that a decision-maker accumulates evidence over time until that evidence reaches a predefined threshold for a particular choice. This process accounts for both the final choice and the reaction time required to make it.

2.  **Stage 2: Post-Decisional Accumulation for Confidence.** Crucially, the model proposes that the evidence accumulation process does not stop once a choice is made. Instead, it continues for a further period of time. The state of the accumulated evidence at the end of this second stage is then used to determine the level of confidence in the initial choice. This post-decisional processing is the key feature that distinguishes 2DSD from prior models where choice and confidence were assumed to be based on the same information at the same time.

**Explanatory Power and Applications**

The 2DSD model provides a comprehensive framework that successfully accounts for established relationships between accuracy, response time, and confidence. For instance, it can explain why faster decisions are often associated with lower confidence and why confidence is generally higher for correct responses than for errors. The model's dynamic nature also allows it to predict how time pressure impacts both the accuracy of a choice and the subsequent confidence rating.

Pleskac and Busemeyer demonstrated the model's robustness by applying it to data from both perceptual tasks (e.g., discriminating line lengths) and cognitive tasks (e.g., judging which of two cities has a larger population). The theory also defines an optimal strategy for balancing speed and accuracy in both choice and confidence, and finds that human participants tend to behave in a manner consistent with this optimal solution when forming their confidence judgments.
