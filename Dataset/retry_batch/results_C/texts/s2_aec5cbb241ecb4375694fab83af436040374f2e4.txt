## 1. Official Abstract
>Can a person feel worse off as the options they face increase? The present studies suggest that some people—maximizers—can. Study 1 reported a Maximization Scale, which measures individual differences in the desire to maximize. Seven samples revealed negative correlations between maximization and happiness, optimism, self-esteem, and life satisfaction, and positive correlations between maximization and depression, perfectionism, and regret. Study 2 found maximizers less satisfied than nonmaximizers (satisficers) with consumer decisions, and more likely to engage in social comparison. Study 3 found maximizers more adversely affected by upward social comparison. Study 4 found maximizers more sensitive to regret and less satisfied in an ultimatum bargaining game. The interaction between maximizing and choice is discussed in terms of regret, adaptation, and self-blame.

### Introduction to Maximizers vs. Satisficers
The paper introduces a fundamental distinction between two types of decision-making styles: "maximizing" and "satisficing". Maximizers are individuals who strive to make the best possible choice by exhaustively searching through all options. In contrast, satisficers are those who search for an option that is simply "good enough" to meet their standards, without worrying that a better option might exist. The central thesis of the paper is that while maximizers might achieve objectively better outcomes, they tend to feel subjectively worse about their choices and have lower overall well-being.

### Core Findings from the Studies
The paper presents a series of four studies designed to develop a scale for measuring the tendency to maximize and to test the hypothesis that this tendency is linked to negative psychological outcomes.

*   **Study 1: Development of the Maximization Scale:** The authors developed and validated the "Maximization Scale," a self-report questionnaire to measure an individual's propensity for maximizing. Across seven different samples, they found that higher scores on this scale (indicating a maximizing tendency) were consistently and negatively correlated with measures of happiness, life satisfaction, optimism, and self-esteem. Conversely, maximization was positively correlated with depression, perfectionism, and, most notably, regret.

*   **Study 2: Consumer Decisions and Social Comparison:** This study investigated how maximizers and satisficers feel about consumer decisions. The results showed that maximizers were less satisfied with their choices compared to satisficers. A key reason for this dissatisfaction is that maximizers are more prone to engaging in social comparison, particularly upward social comparison where they compare their results to those who did better.

*   **Study 3: The Impact of Upward Social Comparison:** This study confirmed the findings of Study 2, demonstrating that maximizers are more negatively affected by the knowledge that someone else made a better choice. This sensitivity to upward comparison contributes to their diminished satisfaction, even when their own outcome is objectively good.

*   **Study 4: Regret in an Ultimatum Bargaining Game:** The final study used an ultimatum bargaining game to explore the role of regret. The authors found that maximizers were more sensitive to potential regret and, as a result, derived less satisfaction from the outcomes of the game. This suggests that the worry over future regret is a significant factor driving the behavior and emotional experiences of maximizers.

### Overall Analysis and Implications
The paper's primary contribution is the identification and validation of the "maximizer" as a psychological construct and the demonstration of its paradoxical effects. The research suggests that the effort to find the "best" option in a world of increasing choice can be psychologically costly. Maximizers' extensive search for the best possible option exposes them to higher opportunity costs and a greater potential for regret, self-blame, and dissatisfaction. In contrast, satisficers, by having a "good enough" threshold, are more easily satisfied and less burdened by the possibility of a better alternative.

Ultimately, the paper argues that happiness is, to some extent, a matter of choice—not the choice of what to buy or what job to take, but the choice of a decision-making strategy. The findings indicate that the maximizing strategy, while seemingly rational, is often maladaptive and negatively impacts subjective well-being.
