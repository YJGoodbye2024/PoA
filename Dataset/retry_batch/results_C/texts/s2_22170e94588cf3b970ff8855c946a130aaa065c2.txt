## 1. Official Abstract
"The authors review studies conducted by themselves and coauthors that document a 'self-serving' bias in judgments of fairness and demonstrate that the bias is an important cause of impasse in negotiations. They discuss experimental evidence showing that (1) the bias causes impasse; (2) it is possible to reduce impasses by debiasing bargainers; and (3) the bias results from selective evaluation of information. The authors also review results from a field study of negotiations between teachers' unions and school boards in Pennsylvania that both document the fairness bias in a naturalistic setting and demonstrates its impact on strikes."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. This synopsis is structured according to the paper's original sections.

### **Introduction**
The paper addresses the "major unsolved riddle" of why negotiations often result in impasse, despite the significant costs to all parties involved (e.g., litigation costs, labor strikes, domestic strife). Traditional economic models, which assume rational actors, struggle to explain this phenomenon, often attributing it to incomplete information. The authors argue that a key psychological factor, the **self-serving bias**, offers a more robust explanation. This bias is defined as the tendency for individuals to "conflate what is fair with what benefits oneself."

The authors propose three ways this bias leads to impasse:
1.  It can eliminate the "contract zone" by causing parties to have self-servingly optimistic views of their alternatives to negotiation.
2.  It causes parties to interpret the other side's reasonable offers as "cynical and exploitative," eroding trust and goodwill.
3.  Negotiators are highly averse to settling for less than what they perceive as a fair outcome, and the bias distorts this perception of fairness.

### **Experimental Evidence of the Self-Serving Bias**
This section details a series of laboratory experiments designed to isolate and demonstrate the causal link between the self-serving bias and bargaining impasse.

**A Motor-Cycle Accident Case:** In a key experiment, subjects were assigned the role of either plaintiff or defendant in a tort case based on a real Texas lawsuit. They were given the same 27 pages of case materials and asked to predict the judge's award and state what they considered a fair settlement amount.
*   **Findings:** Plaintiffs' predictions of the judge's award were, on average, significantly higher than defendants' predictions. Similarly, plaintiffs' ideas of a "fair" settlement were much higher than defendants'. The parties negotiated for 30 minutes, and the discrepancy between their fairness assessments was a strong predictor of whether they would fail to settle and incur legal fees.

**The Source of the Bias:** To determine the cause of the bias, the researchers ran another experiment where some participants were assigned their roles (plaintiff or defendant) *before* reading the case materials, while others were assigned their roles *after* reading them.
*   **Findings:** The bias was only present when participants knew their role beforehand. This suggests the bias doesn't stem from prior beliefs but from the "selective evaluation of information." Once a role is known, individuals tend to weigh arguments and facts that favor their own position more heavily.

**Debiasing Negotiators:** In a final experimental manipulation, some subjects were instructed to write an essay arguing the other side's case.
*   **Findings:** This "debiasing" technique was effective. Subjects who were asked to consider the weaknesses of their own case and the strengths of their opponent's showed a significantly smaller self-serving bias. They were also much more likely to reach a settlement in the subsequent negotiation.

### **Field Evidence: Teacher Contract Negotiations in Pennsylvania**
To test whether these findings apply in a real-world setting with experienced negotiators, the authors conducted a field study of public-school teacher contract negotiations in Pennsylvania. They surveyed union and school board presidents.
*   **Methodology:** Respondents were asked to identify the salaries in "comparable" districts that they felt were most relevant for their own negotiations. They were also asked for their final offer before a potential strike.
*   **Findings:** Both unions and school boards exhibited a clear self-serving bias. Unions tended to select comparable districts with higher-than-average salaries, while school boards selected those with lower-than-average salaries. The magnitude of this bias was the single best predictor of whether a negotiation would end in a strike. The study found that for every $1,000 increase in the discrepancy between the parties' views of the "comparable" wage, the likelihood of a strike increased by 49 percent.

### **Conclusion**
The authors conclude that the self-serving bias is a "pervasive and important cause of bargaining impasse." While standard economic models focus on structural factors like information asymmetry, this research demonstrates that psychological biases are a critical and often overlooked driver of conflict. The paper suggests that impasses are not always the result of rational strategic calculations but often stem from a genuine, if biased, belief by both parties that they are entitled to more than the other side is willing to offer. The research also offers a practical solution: encouraging negotiators to actively consider the other party's perspective can reduce the bias and promote settlement.
