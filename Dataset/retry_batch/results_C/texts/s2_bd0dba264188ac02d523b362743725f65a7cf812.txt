## 1. Official Abstract
"Our principal hypothesis is that reasoning and decision making are alike in that they both depend on the construction of mental models, and so they should both give rise to similar phenomena. In this paper, we consider one such phenomenon, which we refer to as "focussing": individuals are likely to restrict their thoughts to what is explicitly represented in their models. We show that focussing occurs in four domains. First, individuals fail to draw inferences in the modus tollens form: if p then q, not-q, therefore not-p, because they focus on their initial models of the conditional, which make explicit only the case where the antecedent (p) and consequent (q) occur. Second, in Wason's selection task, they similarly tend to select only those cards that are explicitly represented in their initial models of the conditional rule. Third, their requests for information in order to enable them to make a decision about whether or not to carry out a certain action are focussed on the action to the exclusion of alternatives to it. In each of these cases, we show how the focussing bias can be reduced by certain experimental manipulations. Finally, in counterfactual reasoning, focussing underlies individuals' attempts to imagine an alternative scenario that avoids an unfortunate ending to a story."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. The core argument of the paper is that both reasoning and decision-making are based on the construction of mental models. This shared foundation leads to a cognitive bias the authors term "focussing," where individuals concentrate only on the information explicitly represented in their initial mental models, neglecting alternatives or information that would require fleshing out these models.

### Introduction
The authors introduce their central hypothesis that reasoning and decision-making are governed by the same underlying mechanism: the use of mental models. They argue against the idea that human rationality depends on formal logic, suggesting instead that "the mental machinery underlying both deduction and induction is remarkably similar, and depends not on formal rules of inference but on the construction of mental models". This leads to the prediction that phenomena observed in reasoning should also appear in decision-making. The paper focuses on one such phenomenon, the "focussing effect," which they define as a consequence of the principle of truth in mental model theory: people tend to build initial models that represent only what is true and explicit, ignoring what is false or implicit.

### Focussing in Conditional Reasoning
This section explores how the focussing effect manifests in deductive reasoning, specifically with conditional statements ("if p then q"). The theory of mental models posits that individuals initially represent a conditional with a single explicit model: `p q`. The other possibilities (`not-p not-q` and `not-p q`) are left implicit. This explains why people easily make *modus ponens* inferences (given p, infer q) but struggle with *modus tollens* inferences (given not-q, infer not-p), as the latter requires fleshing out the implicit models to find the case of `not-p not-q`. The authors describe an experiment where a manipulation that helps individuals consider the `not-p` case significantly improves performance on *modus tollens* tasks, supporting the idea that focussing on the initial explicit model is the primary source of difficulty.

### Focussing in Wason's Selection Task
The paper extends the focussing argument to explain the classic errors in Wason's selection task, where participants must choose which cards to turn over to test a conditional rule (e.g., "If a card has a vowel on one side, then it has an even number on the other side"). The typical incorrect choices (selecting the 'vowel' card and the 'even number' card) correspond directly to the `p` and `q` explicitly represented in the initial mental model of the rule. The authors demonstrate that manipulations that make the alternative possibilities more salient can reduce this focussing bias and improve performance.

### Focussing in Decision Making
This section presents a novel set of experiments designed to show that the focussing effect extends to decision-making. In these studies, participants were asked to request information to help them decide whether to take a certain action (e.g., "to use a new sort of petrol"). The results showed a strong tendency for participants to request information only about the consequences of taking the action, ignoring the alternative (not taking the action). This is interpreted as focussing on the explicit model of the action under consideration. The authors conducted a follow-up experiment where the task was rephrased to force participants to choose between two explicit alternatives. This manipulation successfully reduced the focussing bias, causing participants to seek information about both options more evenly.

### Focussing in Counterfactual Thinking
The final domain explored is counterfactual reasoning—thinking about "what might have been." The authors argue that when people imagine an alternative to a past event, they tend to focus on a particular model of what happened and alter it minimally. This focussing explains why people's counterfactual thoughts are often constrained to specific possibilities. For instance, when asked how an unfortunate outcome could have been averted, individuals focus on the explicit events of the story and how they could be changed, rather than imagining entirely different scenarios.

### General Discussion
The authors conclude by reiterating their main thesis: reasoning and decision-making are unified by their reliance on mental models. The focussing effect is presented as a direct and predictable consequence of this shared mechanism. By demonstrating the effect across four different domains—conditional reasoning, the selection task, decision-making, and counterfactual thought—they provide strong evidence for the mental model theory as a unifying framework for a significant portion of higher-level cognition. The paper suggests that the key to debiasing human thought is to find methods that encourage individuals to "flesh out" their initial, simple models and consider the full range of possibilities.
