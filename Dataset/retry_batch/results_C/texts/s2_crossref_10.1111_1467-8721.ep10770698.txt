## 1. Official Abstract
"In the mid 1950s, the results of my doctoral dissertation were nearly ruined; it appeared that I might have treated my experimental subjects in such a way as to lead them to respond in accordance with my experimental hypothesis, or expectancy. All of this was quite unwitting, of course, but it did raise a sobering question about the possibility of interpersonal expectancy effects in the psychological laboratory. If it was my unintentional interpersonal expectancy effect or my 'unconscious experimenter bias' that had led to the puzzling and disconcerting results of my dissertation, then presumably my students and I could produce the phenomenon in our own laboratory, and with several experimenters rather than just one."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible and its core insights are summarized below, following the paper's original structure.

### Early Laboratory Experiments
The paper begins by recounting the author's initial encounter with interpersonal expectancy effects during his doctoral dissertation in the 1950s, which prompted a series of more controlled investigations. The first formal studies involved experimenters asking subjects to rate photographs of people. Half of the experimenters were led to believe the subjects would give high ratings, and the other half were led to expect low ratings. The results consistently showed that "experimenters expecting high ratings obtained substantially higher ratings than did experimenters expecting low ratings." To test the generality of this phenomenon, similar experiments were conducted with animal subjects. Experimenters were told their rats were either "maze-bright" or "maze-dull." In these studies, the rats believed to be brighter demonstrated superior learning, suggesting the expectancy effect was not limited to human interaction.

### The Pygmalion Effect
The research then moved from the laboratory to real-world settings, most famously to the classroom in a study that became known as the "Pygmalion in the Classroom" experiment. In an elementary school, all children were administered a nonverbal intelligence test, which was presented to teachers as a "Test of Inflected Acquisition" that could predict intellectual "blooming." Researchers randomly selected 20% of the students and told their teachers that these children were expected to show significant intellectual gains. The only difference between this experimental group and the control group was the expectation instilled in the teachers' minds. When retested eight months later, the "special" children showed significantly greater gains in IQ than the control group children. This demonstrated that teacher expectations could act as a self-fulfilling prophecy.

### Generality and Mechanisms of the Effect
Rosenthal presents a meta-analysis of 345 studies to illustrate the widespread nature of interpersonal expectancy effects across various domains, including reaction time, inkblot tests, and animal learning. The paper reports that the overall effect size is significant, comparable to the effect of aspirin in reducing heart attacks.

To explain how these expectancies are communicated, the paper introduces the "Four Factor Theory." This theory posits that people who are expected to perform well experience a different interpersonal environment, which can be broken down into four factors:
1.  **Climate:** Teachers create a warmer socio-emotional climate for students they have favorable expectations for, often communicated through nonverbal cues.
2.  **Input:** Teachers tend to teach more material, and more challenging material, to their "special" students.
3.  **Output:** These students are given greater opportunities to respond, including more time to answer questions.
4.  **Feedback:** Teachers offer more differentiated and positive feedback to students for whom they hold higher expectations.

The paper also notes that these expectancy effects have been studied in a variety of other contexts, such as in courtrooms, management, and nursing homes, with findings suggesting that unintended nonverbal behavior is a key mediator of the effect.
