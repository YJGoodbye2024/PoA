## 1. Official Abstract
Persistent, widespread harassment in schools can be understood as a product of collective school norms that deem harassment, and behavior allowing harassment to escalate, as typical and even desirable. Thus, one approach to reducing harassment is to change students' perceptions of these collective norms. Theory suggests that the public behavior of highly connected and chronically salient actors in a group, called social referents, may provide influential cues for individuals' perception of collective norms. Using repeated, complete social network surveys of a public high school, we demonstrate that changing the public behavior of a randomly assigned subset of student social referents changes their peers' perceptions of school collective norms and their harassment behavior. Social referents exert their influence over peers' perceptions of collective norms through the mechanism of everyday social interaction, particularly interaction that is frequent and personally motivated, in contrast to interaction shaped by institutional channels like shared classes. These findings clarify the development of collective social norms: They depend on certain patterns of and motivations for social interactions within groups across time, and are not static but constantly reshaped and reproduced through these interactions. Understanding this process creates opportunities for changing collective norms and behavior.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### **Introduction**
The paper begins by framing persistent student harassment as a problem of collective social norms. The authors argue that in many schools, norms exist that not only tolerate but may even encourage harassment. To change this behavior, one must first alter the students' perceptions of these norms. The introduction posits that certain influential students, termed "social referents," serve as powerful cues for what is considered typical and acceptable behavior within the school's social network. The central hypothesis is that by influencing the public behavior of these key students, it is possible to shift the broader student body's perception of collective norms and, consequently, reduce harassment. The authors define social referents as "highly connected and chronically salient actors in a group" whose public actions provide normative guidance.

### **Method**
The study was a year-long field experiment conducted in a public high school in the northeastern United States. The methodology involved several key components:
*   **Identifying Social Referents:** At the start of the school year, all students (N=1,338) were surveyed to map the school's social network. Students were asked to nominate up to 10 peers they "chose to spend time with in the past week." The 56 students who received the most nominations were identified as the "social referents."
*   **Randomization:** These 56 social referents were randomly assigned to either a treatment group (28 students) or a control group (28 students).
*   **Intervention:** The treatment group of social referents participated in an intervention where they were encouraged to publicly signal their disapproval of harassment. They were given orange wristbands with the slogan "Wrists for Respect" and asked to discuss online and in-person how to handle conflict constructively. Their public declarations against harassment were recorded and broadcast on a school-specific social media site. The control group of social referents did not participate in this intervention.
*   **Data Collection:** Data was collected through three main avenues:
    1.  **Network and Norm Surveys:** The entire student body was surveyed at the beginning and end of the school year to measure their social ties and their perceptions of school norms regarding conflict.
    2.  **Behavioral Observation:** Student "seed" reporters were trained to record instances of student conflict and harassment throughout the year.
    3.  **Disciplinary Records:** Official school records of disciplinary action for harassment were obtained.

### **Results**
The experiment yielded three main findings:
1.  **Change in Norm Perceptions:** Students who had more social ties to the *intervention* social referents showed a significant change in their perception of the school's collective norms. Specifically, they were less likely to believe that their peers viewed conflict as "normal" or desirable by the end of the year.
2.  **Change in Behavior:** Analysis of school disciplinary records revealed that students with more social connections to the intervention social referents received significantly fewer disciplinary actions for harassment over the course of the year compared to those more connected to control referents.
3.  **Mechanism of Influence:** The study found that the influence of social referents was transmitted through personal and frequent social interactions, not simply through shared institutional spaces like classrooms. The authors state, "Students with more exposure to intervention social referents through classroom assignment were disciplined less often for harassment behaviors; however, they were not more likely to act against harassment, either." This suggests that influence requires motivated, personal connection rather than passive co-existence. The most significant changes in norm perception occurred in students who actively chose to associate with the intervention referents.

### **Discussion**
The authors conclude that social referents are powerful agents for changing collective norms and behavior within a real-world social network. The study demonstrates that an intervention targeting a small, randomly selected group of influential individuals can create a ripple effect, altering school-wide perceptions and reducing actual harassment behaviors.

The findings underscore that social norms are not static but are actively "reshaped and reproduced" through specific patterns of social interaction. The key mechanism is not just visibility, but influence through repeated, personally motivated interactions. This provides a clear, evidence-based strategy for interventions aimed at reducing conflict in schools and other communities: identify and engage the social referents to lead the change.
