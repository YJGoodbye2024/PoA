## 1. Official Abstract
"This his volume represents an attempt to provide an integration of three different lines of research in social psychology. These three are reflected in our choice of title: “Perceived Variability, Entitativity, and Essentialism.” Each of these three terms emerged from distinct lines of work, much of it done by contributors to this volume. As we will see, these three terms, when applied to the perception of social groups, refer to related concepts. Our goal in launching this volume was to clarify their definitions, their relations, their causes, and their consequences."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on analyses from related academic works.

The chapter "Perceived Variability, Entitativity, and Essentialism" by Nick Haslam, Louis Rothschild, and Donald Ernst is situated within the research tradition on psychological essentialism—the tendency to believe that certain categories have an underlying reality or true nature that one cannot observe directly but that gives an object its identity. Their work aims to define the characteristics of essentialized groups.

The authors begin by tracing the historical origins of essentialism within linguistic and philosophical traditions before moving to their empirical work. Based on a series of studies, they argue that beliefs about essentialized groups are structured around two primary components:
1.  **Natural Kinds:** This dimension involves viewing social categories as if they were natural, biological categories. This perspective implies that such groups have clear-cut boundaries and that membership is fixed and immutable.
2.  **Entitativity:** This dimension relates to the perception of a group as a single, coherent entity. Haslam and his colleagues see entitativity as a component of essentialism, with perceived homogeneity (low variability) being one of the factors that contributes to the perception of entitativity.

A key argument presented in the chapter is that entitativity itself is a component of essentialism. They propose that essentialized groups are a specific subset of entitative groups, characterized by both high perceived similarity among members and a sense of agency or common goals. In this view, essentialism is a broader concept than just coherence; it is a theory about *why* group membership is meaningful and what it implies about the group's members. It carries attributional consequences that entitativity and perceived variability alone do not, suggesting an underlying essence is causally responsible for the group's shared characteristics.

The chapter, therefore, contributes to the book's overall goal of integrating the three distinct but related concepts of perceived variability, entitativity, and essentialism. It clarifies that while low perceived variability (homogeneity) contributes to entitativity (coherence), essentialism is a deeper belief system that treats groups as natural kinds, imbuing them with an explanatory essence.
