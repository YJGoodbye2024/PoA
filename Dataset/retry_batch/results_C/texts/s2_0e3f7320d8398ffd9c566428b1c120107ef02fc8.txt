## 1. Official Abstract
Theories of aggressive behavior and ethological observations in animals and children suggest the existence of distinct forms of reactive (hostile) and proactive (instrumental) aggression. Toward the validation of this distinction, groups of reactive aggressive, proactive aggressive, and nonaggressive children were identified (n = 624 9-12-year-olds). Social information-processing patterns were assessed in these groups by presenting hypothetical vignettes to subjects. 3 hypotheses were tested: (1) only the reactive-aggressive children would demonstrate hostile biases in their attributions of peers' intentions in provocation situations (because such biases are known to lead to reactive anger); (2) only proactive-aggressive children would evaluate aggression and its consequences in relatively positive ways (because proactive aggression is motivated by its expected external outcomes); and (3) proactive-aggressive children would select instrumental social goals rather than relational goals more often than nonaggressive children. All 3 hypotheses were at least partially supported.

### Introduction & Theoretical Framework
Crick and Dodge's 1996 paper builds upon the Social Information Processing (SIP) model, which posits that a child's behavioral response to a social situation is the result of a sequence of cognitive steps. The study's primary goal was to empirically validate the distinction between two theorized forms of aggression: **reactive aggression** (a "hot-tempered," angry, and defensive response to a perceived threat or provocation) and **proactive aggression** (a "cold-blooded," instrumental, and goal-oriented act to achieve a desired outcome). The authors hypothesized that these two types of aggression are driven by distinct cognitive distortions at different stages of the social information-processing flow.

### Methodology
The study involved 624 children between the ages of 9 and 12. These children were categorized into three groups: reactive aggressive, proactive aggressive, and nonaggressive. To assess the cognitive mechanisms underlying their behavior, the researchers presented the children with a series of hypothetical social vignettes depicting ambiguous provocations by peers. The children's responses to these stories were used to measure their cognitive patterns.

### Core Hypotheses and Findings
The study tested three central hypotheses, each linked to a specific step in the SIP model, and found at least partial support for all of them.

1.  **Hostile Attribution Bias in Reactive Aggression:** The first hypothesis predicted that reactively aggressive children would be uniquely prone to hostile attribution bias—the tendency to interpret ambiguous actions by peers as intentionally hostile. This aligns with the SIP model's early processing steps of encoding and interpretation. As predicted, the study found that only the reactive-aggressive group demonstrated this bias, which is thought to lead directly to the angry, retaliatory nature of their aggression. This finding has been consistently cited as a key differentiator between the two aggression types.

2.  **Positive Outcome Expectancies in Proactive Aggression:** The second hypothesis proposed that only proactively aggressive children would evaluate aggression and its outcomes in a relatively positive light. This relates to the later SIP steps of response evaluation and selection. The logic is that proactive aggression is not a reaction to provocation but a calculated means to an end; therefore, the aggressor must believe the aggressive act will lead to a desirable result (e.g., obtaining an object, asserting dominance). The study's findings supported this, showing that proactive aggressors were more likely to anticipate positive outcomes from their aggressive acts.

3.  **Instrumental Goal Selection in Proactive Aggression:** The third hypothesis stated that proactively aggressive children would be more likely to prioritize instrumental goals (e.g., getting what they want) over relational goals (e.g., maintaining friendships) compared to their nonaggressive peers. This cognitive step, goal clarification, helps direct the subsequent processing stages. The results supported this, indicating that the goal-oriented nature of proactive aggression is reflected in the fundamental priorities of the children who use it.

### Significance and Impact
Crick and Dodge's 1996 paper is a landmark study that provided crucial empirical evidence for the theoretical distinction between reactive and proactive aggression. It firmly linked these distinct behavioral patterns to different underlying social-cognitive mechanisms. By demonstrating that reactive aggressors are biased in their *interpretation* of social cues, while proactive aggressors are biased in their *evaluation* of aggressive responses, the study offered a more nuanced understanding of childhood aggression. This distinction has had a profound impact on the field, informing the development of more targeted clinical interventions and assessment tools, such as the Reactive-Proactive Aggression Questionnaire (RPQ).
