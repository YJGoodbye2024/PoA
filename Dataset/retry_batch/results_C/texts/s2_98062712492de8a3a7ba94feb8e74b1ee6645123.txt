## 1. Official Abstract
"The current research found that participants who had previously endured an emotionally distressing event (e.g., bullying) more harshly evaluated another person’s failure to endure a similar distressing event compared with participants with no experience enduring the event or those currently enduring the event. These effects emerged for naturally occurring (Studies 1, 3, and 4) and experimentally induced (Study 2) distressing events. This effect was driven by the tendency for those who previously endured the distressing event to view the event as less difficult to overcome (Study 3). Moreover, we demonstrate that the effect is specific to evaluations of perceived failure: Compared with those with no experience, people who previously endured a distressing event made less favorable evaluations of an individual failing to endure the event, but made more favorable evaluations of an individual managing to endure the event (Study 4). Finally, we found that people failed to anticipate this effect of enduring distress, instead believing that individuals who have previously endured emotionally distressing events would most favorably evaluate others’ failures to endure (Study 5). Taken together, these findings present a paradox such that, in the face of struggle or defeat, the people we seek for advice or comfort may be the least likely to provide it."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible.

### **Introduction**
The paper begins by challenging the common intuition that individuals who have overcome a difficult experience are uniquely qualified to offer compassionate advice to others facing similar struggles. The authors propose a counterintuitive hypothesis: having overcome a distressing event can, under certain circumstances, reduce compassion for those who fail to cope with a similar event. This phenomenon is termed the "empathy gap," where the memory of a past hardship is less intense than the present experience, leading to a misjudgment of the difficulty of the event for others. The authors suggest that individuals who have successfully navigated a challenge may reconstruct their memory of the event, viewing it as less difficult than it actually was. This cognitive distortion, in turn, leads them to judge those who are struggling more harshly. The introduction sets the stage for five studies designed to test this hypothesis across various contexts.

### **Study 1: The Polar Plunge**
*   **Methods:** The first study examined compassion among participants in a "Polar Plunge" event, where individuals jump into icy water. The researchers surveyed three groups: those who had already completed the plunge ("pasts"), those who were about to jump ("presents"), and spectators who had no intention of jumping ("no-experiences"). Participants read a scenario about another person who backed out of the plunge at the last minute and were asked to rate their compassion for this individual.
*   **Results:** The findings supported the central hypothesis. "Pasts" exhibited significantly less compassion for the individual who backed out compared to both "presents" and "no-experiences." This suggests that having successfully endured the challenge made individuals less empathetic toward someone who failed to do so.

### **Study 2: A Bullying Scenario**
*   **Methods:** To establish causality, the second study experimentally induced the experience of a distressing event. Participants were asked to write about a time they were either bullied (a distressing event) or when they engaged in a hobby (a neutral event). They were then presented with a story about a teenager who was being bullied and either coped successfully or failed to cope (by lashing out violently). Participants then rated their compassion for the teenager.
*   **Results:** The results showed that participants who had written about being bullied (the "pasts") were more compassionate toward the teenager who coped successfully. However, they were the least compassionate toward the teenager who failed to cope, compared to the control group. This provides experimental evidence that having a similar past experience can decrease compassion when the other person is perceived as failing.

### **Study 3: Perceived Difficulty**
*   **Methods:** This study aimed to understand the mechanism behind the reduced compassion. Participants were again divided into "pasts" (those who had been bullied) and "no-experiences." They read a scenario about a person being bullied and were asked to rate how difficult they perceived the situation to be for that person.
*   **Results:** As predicted, "pasts" rated the experience of being bullied as significantly less difficult to overcome than "no-experiences." This difference in perceived difficulty mediated the relationship between past experience and compassion, suggesting that those who have overcome a hardship tend to downplay its difficulty, which in turn leads to harsher judgments of others.

### **Study 4: Success vs. Failure**
*   **Methods:** The fourth study further explored the role of perceived success or failure. Participants with and without a history of being bullied evaluated a teenager who was either successfully or unsuccessfully coping with being bullied.
*   **Results:** The findings demonstrated that "pasts" were more favorable in their evaluations of the teenager who was successfully coping compared to the "no-experience" group. Conversely, they were more critical of the teenager who was failing to cope. This highlights that the effect is specific to how one evaluates another's failure to endure a distressing event.

### **Study 5: The Paradox of Seeking Advice**
*   **Methods:** The final study investigated whether people anticipate this lack of compassion from those who have "been there." Participants were asked from whom they would prefer to seek advice after a personal failure: someone who had successfully overcome a similar failure or someone with no such experience.
*   **Results:** The results revealed a significant lack of awareness of this phenomenon. Participants overwhelmingly believed that individuals who had previously endured a similar distressing event would be the most compassionate and would evaluate their failure most favorably. This indicates that people are likely to seek comfort from the very individuals who may be least likely to provide it.

### **General Discussion**
The authors conclude by summarizing the consistent findings across the five studies: having overcome a distressing event can lead to reduced compassion for others who are struggling with a similar challenge. This is driven by a tendency to underestimate the difficulty of the past event. The paper highlights a significant paradox: we often seek out those with shared experiences for support, yet they may be the most critical of our failures to cope. The authors suggest that this "empathy gap" has important implications for social support, mentorship, and advice-seeking in various domains of life. The research points to a cognitive bias where the successful resolution of a past hardship distorts the memory of its difficulty, leading to less empathy for those currently in the throes of a similar struggle.
