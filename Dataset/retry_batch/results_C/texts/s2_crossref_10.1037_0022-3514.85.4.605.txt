**Detailed Interpretation**

This study by Shelley E. Taylor and her colleagues directly addresses a central debate in social and personality psychology: whether self-enhancement—the tendency to hold overly positive views of oneself—is adaptive or maladaptive. The paper contrasts two dominant viewpoints: the "positive illusions" framework, which argues that self-enhancement fosters mental and physical health, and the "defensive neuroticism" position, which posits that it is a form of psychological defense that conceals underlying distress and carries physiological costs.

The core of the research was an empirical test using a laboratory-based stress-challenge paradigm. The researchers aimed to determine which of these two competing theories was supported by biological data. Participants identified as high self-enhancers were compared to others on several physiological stress markers.

The study's key findings provided strong support for the positive illusions model. Specifically, individuals with high levels of self-enhancing cognitions exhibited healthier biological profiles under stress. These profiles were characterized by:
*   **Lower cardiovascular responses to stress**: High self-enhancers showed less reactivity in their cardiovascular system when confronted with a stressor.
*   **More rapid cardiovascular recovery**: After the stress task was completed, their physiological systems returned to baseline more quickly.
*   **Lower baseline cortisol levels**: Cortisol is a primary hormone associated with the body's stress response, and lower baseline levels are indicative of a less taxed system.

These results ran counter to the predictions of the defensive neuroticism hypothesis, which would have anticipated that self-enhancers might show heightened physiological arousal due to the effort of maintaining a defensive facade.

Furthermore, the study included a second set of analyses that replicated the "illusory mental health paradigm" from a seminal, critical paper by Shedler, Mayman, and Manis (1993). Even using this alternative framework, the authors found no support for the defensive neuroticism hypothesis.

An important nuance in the findings was the identification of a potential mediator. The researchers discovered that the relationship between self-enhancement and lower cortisol was mediated by "psychosocial resources." This suggests that self-enhancers may possess greater psychological resources, such as optimism or a sense of personal control, which in turn helps to buffer them against biological stress responses. The study concluded by emphasizing the significant stress-buffering effects of holding self-enhancing beliefs.
