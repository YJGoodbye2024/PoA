## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the book was accessible for analysis. It is organized into five parts and 17 chapters. The following is a synopsis structured according to the book's original sections.

### Introduction: Freedom, Control, and Reactance Theory
The book begins by establishing the central premise of psychological reactance theory. It outlines the motivational state that occurs when a person's freedoms are threatened or eliminated. The theory posits that "a threat to or loss of a freedom motivates the individual to restore that freedom." This introductory section frames the book's primary goal: to explore the psychological consequences that arise from a threat to or loss of control or freedom. The authors also state their ambition to generate future research on the topic.

### Part I: The Theory
This section delves into the foundational concepts of reactance theory. It defines what constitutes a "freedom," which is the belief that one can engage in a particular behavior. It then explains how freedoms can be threatened or eliminated. The core principles are laid out, establishing that reactance is a motivational state aimed at reclaiming a specific freedom that has been compromised. Key determinants of the magnitude of reactance are identified:
*   **Importance of the Freedom:** The more significant a freedom is to an individual, the greater the reactance aroused by a threat to it.
*   **Nature of the Threat:** The magnitude of reactance increases with the size of the threat.
*   **Number of Freedoms Threatened:** The more freedoms that are threatened, the greater the resulting reactance.

### Part II: Empirical Issues
This part of the book reviews the body of experimental evidence supporting the theory. It covers various ways individuals attempt to restore their freedom once reactance is aroused. These can include directly engaging in the threatened behavior (behavioral restoration), increasing one's liking for the threatened option (attitudinal restoration), or acting aggressively toward the source of the threat. The authors synthesize findings from numerous laboratory-based studies that demonstrate these effects.

### Part III: Applications of the Theory
Brehm and Brehm expand the theory beyond the laboratory, applying it to real-world scenarios. This section is notable for broadening the theory's applicability.
*   **Clinical Psychology:** The book was among the first to apply reactance theory to the therapeutic relationship, explaining why patients might resist a therapist's recommendations. It discusses how understanding reactance can improve client-therapist interactions.
*   **Societal Problems and Power Relations:** The analysis extends to power dynamics and social issues, providing a framework for understanding resistance to mandates and social influence.
*   **Developmental Aspects:** The authors also consider the developmental trajectory of reactance throughout a person's life.

### Part IV: Control
In this section, the book situates reactance theory within the broader psychological literature on control. It compares and contrasts reactance with other theoretical models where the concept of control is central, such as learned helplessness. This part clarifies that while related, reactance is a distinct response to a loss of *specific* freedoms, rather than a general sense of helplessness.

### Part V: Conclusion
The final part of the book summarizes the core arguments and discusses unresolved theoretical issues. A significant contribution highlighted in this section and by later analyses is the introduction of "trait reactance," the idea that some individuals have a dispositional tendency to be more sensitive to threats to their freedom. The authors conclude by reiterating their hope that the volume will stimulate further research into reactance and related psychological processes.
