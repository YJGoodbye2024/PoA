## 1. Official Abstract
>Four-studies examined stereotype change using 3 models: the bookkeeping model, in which each instance of stereotype-relevant information is used to modify the stereotype gradually; the conversion model, in which stereotypes change radically in response to dramatic or salient instances; and the subtyping model, in which new stereotypic structures are developed to accommodate instances not easily assimilated by existing stereotypes. The models predict different response patterns as a function of variations in the pattern of stereotype-inconsistent evidence and the number of instances encountered. In Exps I and II, a total of 126 undergraduates were given information about either a small or a large sample of group members in which stereotype-inconsistent evidence was dispersed across many members or concentrated within a few members. Results generally support the subtyping model when evidence was concentrated and the bookkeeping model when evidence was dispersed. Exps III and IV, with 30 and 40 undergraduates, respectively, suggested that development of subtypes occurs because dramatically inconsistent individuals are seen as unrepresentative of the group as a whole. Conditions under which the conversion model may operate are considered.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on descriptive summaries and abstracts from academic databases.

This highly influential 1983 paper by Renee Weber and Jennifer Crocker, published in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, investigates the cognitive mechanisms that govern how people change their stereotypes when faced with evidence that contradicts them. The authors proposed and tested three distinct models of stereotype revision.

**The Three Models of Stereotype Change:**

1.  **The Bookkeeping Model:** This model posits that stereotype change is a gradual and cumulative process. Each piece of information that is inconsistent with the stereotype acts like an entry in a ledger, slowly and incrementally adjusting the prevailing belief.
2.  **The Conversion Model:** In contrast, this model suggests that stereotypes can change suddenly and dramatically. This "conversion" happens in response to a particularly salient, powerful, or dramatic piece of counter-stereotypic evidence.
3.  **The Subtyping Model:** This model proposes that when faced with individuals who do not fit a stereotype, people often "fence them off" by creating a new, more specific sub-category for them. This allows the overarching stereotype to remain intact while accommodating the exceptions. For example, a person might subtype a "hard-partying accountant" as an "unusual accountant," thus preserving the general stereotype of accountants as being staid and reserved.

**Experimental Design and Findings:**

The authors conducted a series of four experiments with undergraduate students to test these models. The core of the methodology involved presenting participants with information about members of stereotyped occupational groups, such as corporate lawyers and librarians. This information contained a mix of stereotype-consistent and stereotype-inconsistent traits. The key manipulation was how the inconsistent information was presented:

*   **Dispersed Condition:** The stereotype-inconsistent traits were scattered across many different members of the group. For instance, many different lawyers would each display one trait that went against the stereotype.
*   **Concentrated Condition:** The same number of inconsistent traits were concentrated in just a few individuals. For example, only a couple of lawyers would display multiple traits that went against the stereotype.

The study's main findings were:

*   When disconfirming evidence was **dispersed** across many individuals, participants tended to modify their stereotypes gradually. This finding lends support to the **bookkeeping model**.
*   When disconfirming evidence was **concentrated** in a few highly atypical individuals, participants were more likely to create subtypes for these exceptions. This result strongly supports the **subtyping model**. The dramatically inconsistent individuals were seen as unrepresentative of the group as a whole, making it easier to isolate them into a new category rather than change the overall stereotype.

The research found less support for the conversion model, suggesting that radical, sudden stereotype change is not the typical response to disconfirming evidence. The authors concluded that subtyping is a common strategy that explains why stereotypes are so resistant to change; it allows the holder of the belief to acknowledge exceptions without having to discard the overarching, and often simpler, general stereotype.
