## 1. Official Abstract
Two field studies explored the relationship between self-awareness and transgressive behavior. In the first study, 363 Halloween trick-or-treaters were instructed to only take one candy. Self-awareness induced by the presence of a mirror placed behind the candy bowl decreased transgression rates for children who had been individuated by asking them their name and address, but did not affect the behavior of children left anonymous. Self-awareness influenced older but not younger children. Naturally occurring standards instituted by the behavior of the first child to approach the candy bowl in each group were shown to interact with the experimenter's verbally stated standard. The behavior of 349 subjects in the second study replicated the findings in the first study. Additionally, when no standard was stated by the experimenter, children took more candy when not self-aware than when self-aware.

The 1979 paper by Beaman, Klentz, Diener, and Svanum presents two field studies designed to test the hypothesis that heightened self-awareness reduces transgressive (rule-breaking) behavior in children. The core of the research is built on the theory of objective self-awareness, which posits that when attention is focused inward, individuals are more likely to compare their current behavior against salient social and personal standards.

### **Study 1: Halloween Experiment**

The first study was a cleverly designed field experiment conducted on Halloween night with 363 trick-or-treaters. The researchers set up a situation where children would arrive at a house and be instructed by an experimenter to take only one piece of candy from a bowl. To manipulate self-awareness, a large mirror was placed behind the candy bowl for some children, while for others, there was no mirror. The mirror acted as the stimulus to induce a state of objective self-awareness, reminding the children of their own identity and the standard of behavior just given to them.

A key finding was that the mirror's effect was not uniform across all children. It significantly decreased the rate of transgression (taking more than one candy) only for those children who had been "individuated" by the experimenter asking for their name and address. For children who remained anonymous, the presence of the mirror had no significant effect on their behavior. Furthermore, the self-awareness manipulation was effective on older children but not younger ones. The study also noted an interesting social interaction: the behavior of the first child in a group to take candy could set a "naturally occurring standard" that influenced the rest of the group. Overall, children in front of a mirror were significantly less likely to take extra candy (14.4%) compared to those without a mirror (28.5%).

### **Study 2: Replication and Extension**

The second study involved 349 children and was designed to replicate and extend the findings of the first. It successfully reproduced the main results from the initial experiment. A crucial addition in this study was a condition where the experimenter gave no explicit standard for how much candy to take. In this ambiguous situation, children who were not made self-aware (no mirror) took more candy than those who were in the self-aware (mirror) condition. This suggests that in the absence of a clear external rule, self-awareness can activate internalized standards of fairness or moderation.

### **Conclusion and Impact**

Together, these two studies provided strong evidence from a naturalistic setting that self-awareness is a powerful factor in promoting self-regulation and adherence to social standards. By making a person's attention focus inward, stimuli like a mirror can make them more conscious of their own actions and how those actions align with established norms, thereby reducing dishonest or disobedient behavior. The research demonstrated that this effect is moderated by factors such as age and individuation, highlighting the complex interplay between the situation, the individual, and their developmental stage.
