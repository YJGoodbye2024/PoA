## 1. Official Abstract
"A cognitive-ecological approach to judgment biases is presented and substantiated by recent empirical evidence. Latent properties of the environment are not amenable to direct assessment but have to be inferred from empirical samples that provide the interface between cognition and the environment. The sampling process may draw on the external world or on internal memories. For systematic reasons (proximity, salience, and focus of attention), the resulting samples tend to be biased (selective, skewed, or conditional on information search strategies). Because people lack the metacognitive ability to understand and control for sampling constraints (predictor sampling, criterion sampling, selective-outcome sampling, etc.), the sampling biases carry over to subsequent judgments. Within this framework, alternative accounts are offered for a number of judgment biases, such as base-rate neglect, confirmation bias, illusory correlation, pseudo-contingency, Simpson's paradox, outgroup devaluation, and pragmatic-confusion effects."

### **Core Thesis: Judgment Biases as a Function of Biased Information Samples**

Klaus Fiedler's 2000 paper, "Beware of samples!", introduces a cognitive-ecological framework that reframes the origin of many judgment biases. Instead of attributing biases primarily to flawed mental processing or motivational factors, Fiedler argues that they often arise from a perfectly rational cognitive system processing biased data. The core idea is that our judgments about the world are not based on the "latent properties of the environment" but on the limited and often unrepresentative samples of information we gather from it, either through direct experience or from memory.

The theory posits that the information we sample is systematically biased for reasons like salience, accessibility, and the strategies we use to search for information. A crucial part of the thesis is that humans generally lack the metacognitive awareness to recognize and correct for these sampling constraints. This "metacognitive deficit" or "blindness" means that the biases inherent in the sample are directly carried over into our final judgments. This perspective treats the decision-maker as an "intuitive statistician" whose accuracy is hindered by the quality of the input data.

### **Key Concepts from Third-Party Analysis**

*   **The Sampling Process:** Fiedler's model emphasizes that the environment itself can impose unequal samples of information. For instance, in the context of inter-group relations, even if a person processes all information equally, their judgment can become biased if their information environment simply provides more exposure to one group over another. This can be influenced by motivations, such as an individual's identity guiding them to sample more information about their own group (ingroup) than another (outgroup).
*   **Sources of Sampling Bias:** The biases in samples can be endogenous (generated internally) or influenced by external factors. For example, initial negative information about something might lead a person to avoid further sampling, cementing a negative attitude that is based on a very small and unrepresentative sample. This highlights how initial experiences can skew the entire dataset upon which future judgments are based.
*   **Application to Judgment Biases:** The framework provides alternative explanations for a wide range of well-documented biases:
    *   **Illusory Correlations & Stereotypes:** False associations between groups and traits may not stem from faulty reasoning but from biased environmental inputs. If the information sample itself is unrepresentative, even correct cognitive processing will lead to a biased conclusion.
    *   **Inter-Group Bias:** Biased judgments about groups can originate in "perfectly adaptive behavior" within a biased information environment, rather than necessarily from motivational biases or conflict. The size of the sample is critical; smaller samples, which are more likely for outgroups, are prone to more extreme and homogenous judgments.
    *   **Confirmation Bias:** The model explains confirmation bias through outcome-selective sampling, where a lack of disconfirming feedback can lead to unjustified confidence in a decision, discouraging further information gathering.
*   **Distinction from Traditional Accounts:** The cognitive-ecological approach is distinct from traditional theories that focus on the internal limitations of the mind (bounded rationality). Fiedler's work shifts the focus to the interplay between the mind and the structure of its information environment. It suggests that to understand cognitive processes, one must first understand the biases and distributions built into the environmental information that feeds those processes. This perspective challenges researchers to consider that observed framing effects or other biases might be driven by the nature of the information sample rather than assuming they are evidence of biased motivational processes within the individual.

In summary, Fiedler's paper presents a paradigm where many cognitive biases are not necessarily errors of a faulty mind, but rather logical inferences based on faulty—because they are biased and incomplete—samples of reality. The critical failure is metacognitive: the inability to see and correct for the distorted lens through which we sample the world.
