## 1. Official Abstract
Feelings of familiarity are not direct products of memory. Although prior experience of a stimulus can produce a feeling of familiarity, that feeling can also be aroused in the absence of prior experience if perceptual processing of the stimulus is fluent (e.g., Whittlesea, Jacoby, & Girard, 1990). This suggests that feelings of familiarity arise through an unconscious inference about the source of processing fluency. The present experiments extend that conclusion. First, they show that a wide variety of feelings about the past are controlled by a fluency heuristic, including feelings about the meaning, pleasantness, duration, and recency of past events. Second, they show that the feeling of familiarity can be produced by conceptual as well as perceptual fluency. Third, they show that the feeling of familiarity is not an automatic consequence of fluent processing: It occurs only when fluency is attributed to the past.

### Core Thesis: Familiarity as a Heuristic Attribution
The central argument of Whittlesea (1993) is that the feeling of familiarity is not a direct readout from memory but rather an "illusion" created through a cognitive heuristic. This feeling arises when cognitive processing is unexpectedly easy or "fluent." Instead of being a direct product of memory, familiarity is the result of an unconscious inference or attribution about why a particular stimulus was so easy to process. If the fluency is attributed to a prior encounter, the stimulus is experienced as familiar.

### Key Experiments and Findings
The paper presents a series of experiments to demonstrate that this "fluency heuristic" can be triggered by various factors, leading to illusions of memory and other feelings about the past.

*   **Conceptual Fluency:** A key contribution of the paper was demonstrating that illusions of familiarity could be generated by *conceptual fluency*, not just perceptual fluency. In one experiment, target words were presented in sentences that were either highly predictive of the target (e.g., "The stormy seas tossed the tiny ____ [BOAT]") or neutral. Words presented in a predictive context were processed more fluently, which led subjects to incorrectly identify them as "old" (i.e., seen before) more often than words in a neutral context. This showed that when a word's meaning is primed and easy to process, that ease can be misattributed as familiarity.

*   **Manipulation of Feelings:** The research extended this principle beyond simple familiarity. Whittlesea showed that a variety of judgments—such as the pleasantness, duration, and recency of past events—are also controlled by this fluency heuristic. For instance, in one experiment, words that appeared in a predictive semantic context were not only pronounced faster (indicating fluent processing) but were also judged as more pleasant.

*   **Attribution is Not Automatic:** A critical finding is that the link between fluency and familiarity is not automatic. The feeling of familiarity only arises when the fluency is specifically attributed to the past. Later research, building on this work, established the "discrepancy-attribution hypothesis," which posits that it is not fluency alone but *unexpectedly* fluent processing that triggers the attributional process leading to a feeling of familiarity. If a person is aware of the manipulation causing the fluency (e.g., they know the text is clear or the context is predictive), the illusion of familiarity is reduced or eliminated.

### Impact and Critical Analysis
Whittlesea's 1993 paper is a highly influential work in the study of memory and cognition, cited over 900 times. It solidified the idea that subjective experience is not a direct reflection of reality but is constructed and inferential. The core concept is that people act as intuitive scientists, trying to explain their own cognitive experiences; the feeling of fluency is an internal signal that they then attempt to explain. When fluency is high, and there is no other obvious cause, a prior experience is inferred, creating an "illusion of familiarity."

This work helped shift the understanding of familiarity from a simple memory trace to a metacognitive judgment. It laid the groundwork for subsequent theories, such as the discrepancy-attribution hypothesis, which further refined the conditions under which fluency leads to feelings of familiarity, emphasizing the role of surprise and expectation.
