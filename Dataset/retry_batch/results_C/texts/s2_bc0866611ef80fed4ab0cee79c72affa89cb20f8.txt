## 1. Official Abstract
The authors conducted an empirical study to test McGuire's (1984) distinctiveness theory within an advertising context. First, following the distinctiveness theory postulate, they found that members of minority groups were more likely than majority groups to have their ethnicity salient. Furthermore, in applying distinctiveness theory to persuasion, they found that members of minority (versus majority) groups find an ad spokesperson from their own ethnic group to be more trustworthy and that increased trustworthiness led to more positive attitudes toward the brand being advertised. The authors draw implications for both advertising to ethnic/minority groups as well as for further research applications of distinctiveness theory.

### Detailed Interpretation

**Introduction & Theoretical Framework**
"A Tale of Two Cities: Distinctiveness Theory and Advertising Effectiveness" by Rohit Deshpandé and Douglas M. Stayman is a foundational study that applies psychological principles to the field of ethnic advertising. The paper's central framework is McGuire's distinctiveness theory, which posits that an individual's awareness of a particular personal trait increases when that trait is uncommon or distinctive within their immediate social environment. The authors were the first to apply this theory to an advertising context, seeking to understand how a consumer's sense of ethnic identity, when made salient, affects their response to targeted advertising. The core idea is that members of a minority group are more likely to have their ethnicity be a salient part of their self-concept in a given situation, which in turn influences their perception of advertisements.

**Methodology**
The study was an empirical experiment conducted in "two cities" to create different social contexts for the participants. The research involved Hispanic and Anglo (White) consumers who were placed in situations where they were either in the numerical majority or minority. This design allowed the researchers to manipulate the "distinctiveness" of a participant's ethnicity. The study then exposed participants to advertisements featuring spokespeople of different ethnic backgrounds to measure the effects on persuasion. Key variables measured included the salience of ethnic self-identity, the perceived trustworthiness of the ad spokesperson, and the subsequent attitude toward the advertised brand.

**Key Findings**
The study produced two main findings:
1.  **Salience of Ethnic Identity:** Consistent with distinctiveness theory, the study found that the situational context directly influenced the salience of ethnicity. Members of an ethnic group (both Hispanic and White) were more likely to be spontaneously aware of their own ethnicity when they were in a numerical minority.
2.  **Advertising Effectiveness:** The heightened ethnic salience in minority contexts had a direct impact on advertising effectiveness. When their ethnicity was distinctive, consumers found a spokesperson from their own ethnic group to be significantly more trustworthy. This increased perception of trustworthiness led to more positive attitudes toward the brand being advertised.

In essence, the research demonstrated that targeted advertising featuring a spokesperson of the same ethnicity as the viewer is more effective for consumers who are in a numerical minority, because their ethnic identity is more prominent in that context.

**Implications and Significance**
The paper's conclusions have significant implications for marketing strategy, particularly in multicultural markets. It provides a theoretical explanation for why and when ethnically targeted advertising works. The findings suggest that advertisers can increase persuasion among minority consumers by using models and cues that align with the target group's ethnicity, as this enhances source trustworthiness. The study is frequently cited in subsequent research on ethnic marketing, consumer identity, and advertising persuasion, forming a basis for later investigations into how social status, cultural cues, and other identity dimensions interact with advertising messages.
