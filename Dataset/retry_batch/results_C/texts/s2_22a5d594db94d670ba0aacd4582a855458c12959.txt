## 1. Official Abstract
"Telling people that a consumer claim is false can make them misremember it as true. In two experiments, older adults were especially susceptible to this “illusion of truth” effect. Repeatedly identifying a claim as false helped older adults remember it as false in the short term but paradoxically made them more likely to remember it as true after a 3 day delay. This unintended effect of repetition comes from increased familiarity with the claim itself but decreased recollection of the claim's original context. Findings provide insight into susceptibility over time to memory distortions and exploitation via repetition of claims in media and advertising."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. The synopsis below is structured according to the paper's original sections.

### Introduction: Memory for Truth
The authors introduce the "illusion of truth" effect, where individuals are more likely to believe a statement is true simply because they have been exposed to it before. This phenomenon occurs even when a claim is explicitly identified as false upon initial presentation. The paper's central argument is that judgments of truth rely on two distinct memory processes: the feeling of **familiarity** with the content of a claim and the specific **recollection** of the context in which it was presented (e.g., being told it was "true" or "false"). When recollection of the context fades over time while the sense of familiarity remains, people may misattribute this familiarity as a sign of truth. The authors propose that this dissociation is particularly pronounced in older adults, whose memory for context is more susceptible to decline than their sense of familiarity.

### Age, Repetition, and Delay
Building on the core concepts, the authors hypothesize how age, repetition, and time delays interact. They predict that while repetition should initially strengthen memory for both the claim and its context (i.e., that it is false), a paradoxical effect will emerge over time. Specifically for older adults, they propose that after a delay, the detailed memory of the "false" context will fade more rapidly than the feeling of familiarity with the claim itself. "Once the needed contextual details are lost," the authors argue, "the remaining feeling of familiarity fosters the paradoxical acceptance of false claims as true, rendering older adults particularly susceptible to this bias."

### Experiment 1
This experiment was designed to test the effects of repetition and delay on memory for false claims in both younger and older adults.

*   **Method:** The study involved younger (ages 18-25) and older (ages 71-86) participants. They were shown a series of health-related statements, each explicitly marked as either "true" or "false." False statements were presented either once or three times. Participants' memory was tested twice: once after a 30-minute delay and again after a 3-day delay.
*   **Results:** After the short 30-minute delay, repetition worked as intended for older adults; they were *more* accurate at identifying the repeatedly shown false claims as false. However, after the 3-day delay, the effect reversed dramatically. The more times older adults had been warned that a claim was false, the more likely they were to misremember it as true. For false claims shown three times, older adults incorrectly identified them as true 40% of the time, compared to 28% for claims shown only once. This "backfire" effect was not observed in the younger participants.

### Experiment 2
The second experiment investigated whether making a claim familiar *before* issuing a warning would increase its later acceptance as true.

*   **Method:** Older adults (ages 69-83) were first exposed to a list of claims without any indication of their truthfulness (the "familiarization" phase). In a subsequent "study" phase, they were shown a mix of familiar and new claims, all of which were now identified as false.
*   **Results:** The findings showed that simply having been exposed to a claim beforehand—even without any initial judgment of its truth—made it more likely to be accepted as true later on, despite being explicitly warned that it was false. When tested after a 3-day delay, older adults were more likely to misremember the familiarized false claims as true compared to new false claims. This confirmed that prior familiarity with a claim can undermine subsequent warnings about its falsity.

### General Discussion
The authors conclude that warnings about false information can paradoxically increase belief in that very information over time, a phenomenon they term the "illusion of truth." This effect is driven by a dissociation between familiarity and recollection. While repetition makes a claim feel more familiar, the memory for the specific context of the warning (i.e., "this is false") fades, particularly for older adults. Over time, the strong sense of familiarity is misinterpreted as a signal of truth.

The practical implications are significant for consumer protection and public health. The authors state, "our findings suggest that the most common strategy for debunking false information—namely, to reiterate the false claim and pair it with a warning that it is false—is likely to be ineffective and may even backfire for the most vulnerable consumers." Instead of repeating false claims to debunk them, the research suggests that communication strategies should focus on affirming and repeating what *is* true.
