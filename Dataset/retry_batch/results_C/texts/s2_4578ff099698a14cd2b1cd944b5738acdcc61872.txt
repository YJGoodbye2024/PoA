## 1. Official Abstract
Psychopathy is a personality disorder characterized by impulsive antisocial deviance in the context of emotional and interpersonal detachment. A factor analysis of the subscales of the Psychopathic Personality Inventory (PPI) yielded evidence for 2 factors. One factor showed relations with external criteria mirroring those of the emotional-interpersonal facet of psychopathy, including high dominance, low anxiety, and venturesomeness. The other factor showed relations paralleling those of the social deviance facet of psychopathy, including positive correlations with antisocial behavior and substance abuse, negative correlations with socioeconomic status and verbal ability, and personality characteristics including high negative emotionally and low behavioral constraint. Findings support using the PPI to assess these facets of psychopathy in community samples and to explore their behavioral correlates and genetic-neurobiological underpinnings.

### **Introduction & Background**
The 2003 paper by Benning and colleagues sought to investigate the underlying factor structure of the Psychopathic Personality Inventory (PPI), a self-report instrument designed to assess the personality traits associated with psychopathy in the general population. At the time, the dominant model for assessing psychopathy, particularly in forensic settings, was the clinician-rated Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R), which had a well-established two-factor structure: Factor 1 representing interpersonal/affective traits (e.g., callousness, grandiosity) and Factor 2 representing an impulsive/antisocial lifestyle. The authors aimed to determine if a similar and valid factor structure could be derived from the PPI's subscales, which would support its use for clinical assessment and research in community samples.

### **Methods**
The study conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on the eight subscales of the Psychopathic Personality Inventory. The sample consisted of 353 male twins from a community, not a clinical or incarcerated, population. The analysis initially yielded three factors, but because the third factor was defined solely by the "Coldheartedness" subscale, the researchers opted to extract and focus on a two-factor solution.

### **Results: The Two-Factor Model**
The factor analysis revealed two distinct, higher-order factors, which were later given specific labels in subsequent research.

*   **PPI-I (Fearless Dominance):** This factor was primarily composed of the PPI subscales for **Stress Immunity, Social Potency,** and **Fearlessness**. It was found to be associated with high dominance, venturesomeness, and low anxiety. Subsequent analyses by other researchers noted that this factor also had small positive correlations with academic performance, socioeconomic status, and verbal intelligence. This factor was interpreted as mirroring the interpersonal and affective features of psychopathy, but with an emphasis on adaptive traits like social dominance and resilience to stress rather than the more pathological traits like lack of empathy seen in the PCL-R.

*   **PPI-II (Impulsive Antisociality / Self-Centered Impulsivity):** This factor was defined by the PPI subscales for **Impulsive Nonconformity, Blame Externalization, Machiavellian Egocentricity,** and **Carefree Nonplanfulness**. This factor demonstrated correlations that paralleled the social deviance facet of psychopathy. Specifically, it was positively correlated with antisocial behavior and substance abuse, and negatively correlated with socioeconomic status and verbal ability. It was also associated with high negative emotionality and low behavioral constraint.

A key finding was that the "Coldheartedness" subscale did not load significantly onto either of these two factors in the final model.

### **Discussion & Implications**
Benning et al. concluded that the two-factor structure they identified in the PPI was conceptually parallel to the influential two-factor model of the PCL-R. They argued that their findings supported the validity of using the PPI to assess these distinct facets of psychopathy in non-forensic populations. The study proposed that the PPI could serve as a valuable tool for exploring the behavioral correlates and the genetic and neurobiological foundations of psychopathy in the general community.

However, later research has shown that this two-factor model has limitations. Studies attempting to replicate the structure in incarcerated samples have found that the model provides a poor fit, suggesting the factor structure may differ significantly between community and offender populations. The unique nature of PPI-I ("Fearless Dominance"), which captures adaptive traits, has also been highlighted as a key difference from the PCL-R's first factor, which is more centered on pathological affective deficits like callousness. This has led to an ongoing academic discussion about the true latent structure of the PPI and psychopathy itself.
