## 1. Official Abstract
This article reviews existing empirical research on the peak-and-end rule. This rule states that people's global evaluations of past affective episodes can be well predicted by the affect experienced during just two moments: the moment of peak affect intensity and the ending. One consequence of the peak-and-end rule is that the duration of affective episodes is largely neglected. Evidence supporting the peak-and-end rule is robust, but qualified. New directions for future work in this emerging area of study are outlined. In particular, the personal meanings associated with specific moments and with specific emotions should be assessed.

### Introduction
The paper begins by highlighting a paradox in human behavior: people sometimes make choices that appear to violate hedonistic principles, such as preferring more pain over less or limiting opportunities for pleasure. The author posits that these choices reveal important lessons about how individuals "extract meaning from past affective experiences to make choices about the future." The central challenge addressed is how people construct a single, global evaluation of an affective experience that changes in intensity or quality over time. The paper sets out three primary goals: to review the existing empirical research on this process, to propose new directions for future research, and to discuss the prescriptive implications for optimizing happiness.

### Research Review: Evaluation by Moments
This section synthesizes the empirical evidence for the "peak-and-end rule." This rule proposes that people's retrospective evaluations of an emotional episode are not an average of all moments, but are instead disproportionately influenced by two key moments: the moment of peak emotional intensity (the "peak") and the final moments of the experience (the "end"). A significant consequence of this heuristic is "duration neglect," where the total length of the affective experience has very little impact on its subsequent evaluation. The paper reviews robust evidence supporting this rule, drawn from studies involving both pleasant and aversive experiences, such as watching films or undergoing painful medical procedures.

### New Directions: Evaluation by Meaning
Fredrickson argues for moving beyond simply identifying these heuristic moments to understanding *why* they are so important. The paper suggests that peaks and ends serve as primary "carriers of meaning" for an entire emotional episode.

*   **Peaks and Ends as Carriers of Meaning:** This subsection proposes that the meaning derived from an experience is concentrated in its most intense and final moments. The peak moment may signify the extent of the challenge or pleasure, while the end moment may inform a person's outlook for the future.

*   **Specific Emotions as Carriers of Meaning:** The analysis extends beyond the simple valence (positive/negative) of an experience to consider the role of specific, discrete emotions. Fredrickson argues that different emotions, even of the same valence, can lead to different long-term outcomes and meanings. For instance, certain negative emotions might be more corrosive to personal growth and social relationships than others. This part of the paper connects to the author's broader "broaden-and-build" theory, which posits that experiences of positive emotions can build lasting personal resources.

### Prescriptive Implications: Optimizing Happiness
Building on the peak-and-end rule, this section explores how individuals might consciously structure their experiences to create more favorable memories. If retrospective evaluations depend heavily on peaks and ends, it might be possible to "optimize" happiness by ensuring experiences conclude on a positive or at least less-negative note. This concept is highlighted by studies where participants preferred a longer period of pain that ended with diminishing intensity over a shorter period that ended abruptly at its peak.

### Conclusions
The paper concludes by reiterating that when people evaluate past affective episodes to make future decisions, they often use the peak and end moments as proxies for the entire experience, while largely neglecting its duration. It calls for future research to focus on the personal meanings associated with these key moments and the specific emotions felt during them, moving the field from a simple "evaluation by moments" to a more nuanced "evaluation by meaning."
