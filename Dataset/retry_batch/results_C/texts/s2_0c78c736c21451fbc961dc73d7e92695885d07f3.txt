## 1. Official Abstract
"how do consumers decide how many units to buy? Whereas prior research on individual consumers' purchases has focused primarily on purchase incidence and brand choice, the authors focus on the psychological process behind the purchase quantity decision. The authors propose that a simple anchoring and adjustment model describes how consumers make purchase quantity decisions and suggests how point-of-purchase promotions can increase sales. Two field experiments and two lab studies show that anchor-based promotions—presented as multiple-unit prices, purchase quantity limits, and suggestive selling—can increase purchase quantities. The final study shows that consumers who retrieve internal anchors can counter these anchor-based promotions effectively. Firms might receive net benefits from anchor-based promotions depending on whether increases in unit sales reflect increased category consumption, brand switching, variety switching, store switching, or stockpiling."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible. The following is a synopsis structured according to the paper's original sections.

### Introduction
The paper addresses the question of how consumers decide on the quantity of a product to purchase. The authors argue that while previous research has concentrated on purchase incidence (whether to buy) and brand choice, the purchase quantity decision has been overlooked. They propose that consumers use a simple "anchoring and adjustment" heuristic. This model suggests that consumers often start with an initial number (an anchor) presented to them at the point of purchase and then adjust from there. However, this adjustment is often insufficient, meaning the initial anchor heavily influences the final quantity. The paper outlines a series of four studies designed to test how external anchors—such as multiple-unit pricing, quantity limits, and suggestive slogans—influence purchase quantities.

### Study 1: The Effect of Multiple-Unit Pricing Anchors
This study investigated whether multiple-unit pricing (e.g., "2 for $2") acts as an anchor that increases sales compared to single-unit pricing (e.g., "1 for $1"). Conducted as a series of 13 field experiments in an 86-store supermarket chain, the study found that "multiple-unit promotion pricing led to a 32% increase in sales compared with single-unit pricing." This supports the hypothesis that the number in the multi-unit price serves as a purchase quantity anchor, influencing consumers to buy more than they would with a single-unit price, even when the per-unit cost is identical.

### Study 2: The Effect of Purchase Limit Anchors
Study 2 explored the impact of explicit purchase quantity limits (e.g., "Limit 12 per customer") on sales. This field experiment was conducted in supermarkets using displays of Campbell's soup cans. The results showed a powerful anchoring effect. When no limit was imposed, shoppers purchased an average of 3.3 cans. However, when a sign stated, "Limit of 12 per person," the average purchase quantity rose to 7 cans. The study demonstrated a "monotonically increasing impact on sales" as the limit number increased, confirming that such limits act as strong external anchors that suggest a reasonable or desirable purchase quantity.

### Study 3: Will Suggestive Selling Anchors Be Effective Without Discounts?
This laboratory study aimed to determine if anchoring effects persist even without a price discount. The researchers tested the influence of "suggestive selling anchors," such as the slogan, "Buy 18 Snickers bars for your freezer." The findings indicated that these suggestive slogans could significantly influence intended purchase quantities, even in the absence of any sale or discount. This isolates the psychological effect of the anchor itself, showing that its power is not solely dependent on being associated with a promotion. It works by making a higher number more accessible and plausible in the consumer's mind.

### Study 4: The Moderating Role of Internal Anchors
The final study investigated a method for mitigating the effect of external anchors. The authors hypothesized that if consumers generate their own "internal anchor" by first considering their personal needs, the influence of external anchors will be reduced. To test this, participants in a lab setting were asked to think about their own usage needs for a product *before* being exposed to an external anchor. The results confirmed that "consumers who retrieve internal anchors can counter these anchor-based promotions effectively." When consumers had their own number in mind, they were less susceptible to the influence of suggestive selling slogans or other point-of-purchase anchors.

### General Discussion
The authors conclude that the anchoring and adjustment model provides a robust explanation for how consumers make purchase quantity decisions. Across four studies, they demonstrated that external anchors presented at the point of purchase—through multiple-unit prices, quantity limits, and suggestive slogans—consistently increase the number of units sold. This occurs because these anchors suggest a plausible purchase amount, and consumers tend to adjust insufficiently from this initial value. However, the effect can be neutralized if consumers are prompted to consider their actual needs, thereby creating a self-generated internal anchor. The paper notes that for businesses, the net benefit of using these promotions depends on whether the increased sales come from higher consumption, brand switching, or simply stockpiling by existing customers.
