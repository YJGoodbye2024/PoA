## 1. Official Abstract
"three sets of studies provide evidence for an illusion of transparency, or a tendency for people to overestimate the extent to which others can discern their internal states. People often mistakenly believe that their internal states "leak out" more than they really do. The authors attribute this bias to a tendency for people to adjust insufficiently from the "anchor" of their own phenomenological experience when attempting to take another's perspective. Evidence for this illusion is provided by showing that liars overestimate the detectability of their lies (Studies 1a, 1b, and 1c) and that people believe their feelings of disgust are more apparent than they actually are (Studies 2a and 2b). A final pair of experiments (Studies 3a and 3b) explores the implications of the illusion of transparency for people's reluctance to intervene in emergencies. All 3 sets of studies also provide evidence consistent with the proposed anchoring and adjustment interpretation."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible and its core insights are summarized below, following the paper's original structure.

### **Introduction**
The authors introduce the "illusion of transparency" as the tendency for individuals to overestimate how much their internal states are noticed by others. This illusion stems from a well-established cognitive bias known as "anchoring and adjustment," where people are overly influenced by their own intense, firsthand experience of their emotions and thoughts (the anchor) and fail to adequately adjust for the fact that others do not have the same access to this internal information. The paper notes that this is related to the "spotlight effect," which is the overestimation of how much others notice one's external appearance and actions. The authors propose to test this illusion across three different scenarios: lie detection, the experience of disgust, and bystander intervention in emergencies.

### **Studies 1a, 1b, and 1c: The Overestimation of Lie-Detection**
These studies were designed to test whether liars overestimate how detectable their lies are to observers.

*   **Methods:** Participants were divided into groups and took turns either lying or telling the truth about a topic (e.g., what was depicted on a card). The liars were then asked to estimate how many observers would be able to correctly identify them as the liar. The actual success rate of the observers was then compared to the liars' predictions.
*   **Results:** Across all three studies, the results consistently showed that liars significantly overestimated the detectability of their lies. For instance, in one study, liars predicted that about 49% of observers would spot their lie, when in reality, only 26% did. This gap demonstrates the illusion of transparency: the liars felt their deception was obvious, but it was not nearly as apparent to others. The researchers also found that informing participants about the illusion (in Study 1c) could reduce this bias, suggesting that awareness can help mitigate the effect.

### **Studies 2a and 2b: The Perceived Transparency of Disgust**
These experiments investigated whether people believe a strong internal emotion like disgust is more apparent to others than it actually is.

*   **Methods:** Participants ("tasters") sampled five different beverages, one of which was intensely foul-tasting (e.g., a vinegar-based drink). They were instructed to maintain a neutral facial expression throughout. After the task, the tasters estimated how many of a group of observers would be able to identify which drink was the disgusting one simply by watching a video of their reaction.
*   **Results:** Similar to the lie-detection studies, the tasters consistently overestimated how many observers could detect their disgust. Observers were correct far less often than the tasters predicted, indicating that the tasters' internal experience of disgust felt more externally visible than it was in reality. This provides further evidence for the illusion of transparency, rooted in the tasters' anchoring on their own potent sensory experience.

### **Studies 3a and 3b: Implications for Bystander Intervention**
This final set of studies explored a real-world consequence of the illusion of transparency: its role in the "bystander effect," where individuals are less likely to help in an emergency when others are present.

*   **Methods:** Participants were placed in a group setting and exposed to a simulated emergency (e.g., hearing sounds of a person choking or falling from a bookshelf in an adjacent room). The key was to see if individuals who were concerned would believe their alarm was obvious to the other, non-reacting group members (who were actually confederates of the experimenter). Participants later estimated how apparent their level of concern was to the others.
*   **Results:** The studies found that participants who were concerned about the potential emergency believed their alarm was far more visible to others than it actually was. This illusion contributes to pluralistic ignorance, a key driver of the bystander effect. Because an individual believes their own concern is obvious, they interpret the inaction of others as a sign that the situation must not be an emergency. They fail to realize that the other bystanders may also be internally alarmed but externally calm, each one mistakenly assuming their own feelings are transparent.

### **General Discussion**
The authors conclude that the three sets of studies provide robust evidence for the illusion of transparency across various contexts. The phenomenon is explained as a consequence of an egocentric bias in perspective-taking, specifically the anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic. People are anchored in their own rich, internal phenomenological world and cannot fully appreciate that it is largely opaque to others. The paper discusses the broad implications of this bias, from simple miscommunications in daily life to critical situations like emergency interventions. It is closely linked to the "spotlight effect," as both biases stem from the difficulty of moving beyond one's own immediate experience to accurately gauge how one is perceived by others.
