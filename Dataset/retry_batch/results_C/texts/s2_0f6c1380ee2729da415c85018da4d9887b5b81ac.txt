## 1. Official Abstract
Researchers attempting to understand how citizens process political information have advanced motivated reasoning to explain the joint role of affect and cognition. The prominence of affect suggests that all social information processing is affectively charged and prone to biases. This article makes use of a unique data set collected using a dynamic information board experiment to test important effects of motivated reasoning. In particular, affective biases should cause citizens to take longer processing information incongruent with their existing affect and such biases should also direct search for new information about candidates. Somewhat perversely, motivated reasoners may actually increase their support of a positively evaluated candidate upon learning new negatively evaluated information. Findings are reported that support all of these expectations. Additional analysis shows that these affective biases may easily lead to lower quality decision making, leading to a direct challenge to the notion of voters as rational Bayesian updaters.

### Introduction
The paper positions itself within the political psychology literature, aiming to test the theory of "hot cognition" or motivated reasoning in the context of political decision-making. This theory posits that all information processing is emotionally charged ("affectively charged") and therefore subject to bias. The author, David P. Redlawsk, challenges the model of voters as "cool" or rational Bayesian updaters who logically adjust their beliefs based on new evidence. Instead, the study explores how voters' existing feelings (affect) towards a candidate can bias the processing of new information, especially when that information is inconsistent with their prior beliefs. The core idea is that people are motivated to defend their existing preferences, a process that is not entirely conscious.

### Methods
To test this theory, the study employed a unique experimental design using a "dynamic information board". This method allows researchers to track how individuals seek out and process information during a simulated political campaign. In the experiment, subjects were presented with information about political candidates and their choices were monitored. This setup allows for the measurement of variables such as the time spent processing information that is either congruent or incongruent with a subject's existing attitudes toward a candidate. The design compares subjects who are processing information "on-line" (the default, affect-driven state) with those motivated toward accuracy, which helps isolate the effects of motivated reasoning.

### Results & Hypotheses
The study reported findings that strongly support the motivated reasoning framework. The key hypotheses and results include:

*   **Hypothesis 1 (H1):** Voters using on-line processing will take longer to process information that is affectively incongruent with their existing evaluation of a candidate compared to congruent information. The findings supported this expectation.
*   **Information Search Bias:** The study found that affective biases direct the search for new information about candidates, suggesting that voters are not impartial information-seekers.
*   **Attitude Strengthening / Backfire Effect:** Perhaps the most striking finding is that motivated reasoners may paradoxically *increase* their support for a favored candidate after being exposed to negative information about them. This counterintuitive result shows that encountering challenging information can trigger a defensive reaction that reinforces the original, positive attitude.
*   **Hypothesis 4 (H4):** This attitude strengthening in the face of negative information leads to lower-quality decisions. The analysis confirmed that these affective biases can impair decision-making, directly challenging the notion that voters act as rational updaters.

### Discussion & Conclusion
The paper concludes that affect plays a crucial role in political decision-making. The "hot cognition" model, where emotions guide and bias cognitive processes, appears to be a more accurate description of voter behavior than a "cool consideration" model. The evidence for biased processing, particularly the finding that negative information can strengthen prior beliefs, demonstrates a significant departure from rational models of voting. Redlawsk argues that these affective biases can easily lead to "lower quality" decisions, undermining the classic ideal of a well-reasoned democratic choice. The study serves as a direct challenge to the concept of voters as rational Bayesian updaters and underscores the power of pre-existing feelings in shaping how political information is processed and used.
