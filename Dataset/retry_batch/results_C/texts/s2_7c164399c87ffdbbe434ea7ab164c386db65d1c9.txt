## 1. Official Abstract
"THE COMMON EMPHASIS ON THE IMPORTANCE OF EVALUATION IN JUDGMENT WAS CRITICALLY EXAMINED IN A STUDY OF INFERENCES BETWEEN TRAITS. SETS OF TRAITS WERE SELECTED TO REMOVE THE USUAL CONFOUNDING BETWEEN EVALUATIVE AND DESCRIPTIVE ASPECTS OF JUDGMENT. SS MADE INFERENCES FROM 90 TRAITS TO 40 SCALES DEFINED BY 2 OPPOSING TRAITS. RESULTS REVEAL THE SECONDARY IMPORTANCE OF EVALUATION. ON 70 ITEMS WHERE THEY WERE DIRECTLY OPPOSED, THE DESCRIPTIVE ASPECTS WERE ALWAYS DECISIVE OVER EVALUATION. FACTOR ANALYSIS SHOWED THAT NONE OF THE FACTORS WAS EVALUATIVE. GENERAL DESCRIPTIVE DIMENSIONS SUGGESTED BY FACTOR ANALYSIS COULD ACCOUNT FOR THE EVALUATIVE CONSISTENCY OF INFERENCES IN GENERAL. EVIDENCE SUGGESTS THAT EVALUATION IS TYPICALLY BASED ON A DESCRIPTIVE JUDGMENT OF THE DEGREE OF EXTREMENESS. A MODEL IS PROPOSED FOR THE SYSTEMATIC RELATION OF EVALUATION TO DESCRIPTIVE JUDGMENT. (22 REF.)"

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was not accessible. The following is a detailed interpretation based on an analysis of secondary academic sources.

Dean Peabody's 1967 paper, "Trait inferences: Evaluative and descriptive aspects," is a foundational work in personality psychology that critically challenges the emphasis on evaluation (i.e., judging something as good or bad) in social judgment. The central thesis is that most personality trait terms confound two distinct components: a descriptive aspect, which is factual, and an evaluative aspect, which passes judgment. For example, calling someone "conscientious" is not only a description of their behavior but also a positive evaluation. Peabody argued that to understand how people make inferences about personality, these two components must be separated.

**Methodology and Key Innovation**

To disentangle these components, Peabody developed an ingenious method known as "sets of four". He created quartets of trait adjectives that systematically contrasted the descriptive and evaluative elements. A classic example used by later scholars to illustrate Peabody's model includes the traits *generous, thrifty, extravagant,* and *stingy*. In this set:
*   **Descriptive Contrast:** *Generous* and *extravagant* both describe spending a lot of money, while *thrifty* and *stingy* both describe spending little money.
*   **Evaluative Contrast:** *Generous* and *thrifty* are generally seen as desirable (positive evaluation), while *extravagant* and *stingy* are seen as undesirable (negative evaluation).

By using these carefully constructed sets in trait inference tasks, Peabody could create situations where the descriptive meaning of a trait was in direct opposition to its evaluative implication. Subjects were asked to make inferences from a list of traits to a series of scales, each defined by two opposing traits.

**Core Findings and Impact**

The study's results were decisive and influential. Peabody found that when descriptive and evaluative aspects were pitted against each other, the descriptive component consistently dominated people's inferences. The abstract states that in 70 items designed to directly oppose these aspects, "the descriptive aspects were always decisive over evaluation". Furthermore, a factor analysis of the inference data revealed general descriptive dimensions but no purely evaluative factor.

This led to the paper's main conclusion: evaluation is of secondary importance in trait inference. Instead of being a primary driver of judgment, evaluation often appears to be a byproduct of a descriptive judgment. Specifically, the evidence suggested that the extremity of a descriptive judgment often determines its evaluative tone. For instance, the same underlying behavior can be labeled positively (e.g., "confident") or negatively (e.g., "conceited") depending on its perceived intensity and context.

Peabody's work demonstrated that the strong evaluative consistency often found in personality ratings was largely an artifact of the "usual confounding" between description and evaluation in the natural language of traits. By controlling for this, he revealed that the underlying structure of trait inference is primarily descriptive. This insight has had a lasting impact, influencing subsequent research on personality structure, the lexical hypothesis (the idea that important personality characteristics are encoded in language), and methods for reducing social desirability bias in personality assessments.
