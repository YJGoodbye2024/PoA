## 1. Official Abstract
> Psychological reactance (Brehm, 1966; Brehm & Brehm, 1981) has been a long-standing topic of interest among scholars studying the design and effects of persuasive messages and campaigns. Yet, until recently, reactance was considered to be a motivational state that could not be measured. Dillard and Shen (2005) argued that reactance can be conceptualized as cognition and affect and made amenable to direct measurement. This article revisits Dillard and Shen's (2005) questions about the nature of psychological reactance and reports a test designed to identify the best fitting model of reactance. A meta-analytic review of reactance research was conducted (K = 20, N = 4,942) and the results were used to test path models representing competing conceptualizations of reactance. The results offer evidence that the intertwined model—in which reactance is modeled as a latent factor with anger and counterarguments serving as indicators—best fit the data.

### Detailed Interpretation

**Background: The Challenge of Measuring Reactance**
For decades after its introduction, psychological reactance was considered a hypothetical, intervening variable—a motivational state that could not be directly observed or measured. This posed a significant challenge for researchers, who could only infer its presence based on outcomes like defiance or attitude change away from a persuasive message. A major shift occurred when Dillard and Shen (2005) proposed that reactance could be operationalized and measured by capturing its key components: negative cognitions (i.e., counterarguments) and negative affect (i.e., anger). They put forth several competing models to explain the relationship between these components.

**Objective of the Meta-Analysis**
The primary goal of Stephen A. Rains' 2013 paper was to bring clarity to this debate by systematically testing the competing models proposed by Dillard and Shen. Rather than conducting a new individual experiment, Rains performed a meta-analysis, synthesizing the data from 20 different studies that involved 4,942 participants. This large-scale analysis aimed to determine which conceptualization of reactance was most strongly supported by the existing body of evidence.

**The Competing Models**
The meta-analysis evaluated several distinct models of reactance:
*   **Single-Process Models:** These models conceptualized reactance as either a purely cognitive phenomenon (counterarguing) or a purely affective one (anger).
*   **Dual-Process Model:** This model treated anger and counterarguing as separate and unique factors that independently contribute to reactance effects.
*   **The Intertwined Model:** This model, which Dillard and Shen's original work supported, posits that reactance is a single, latent construct composed of both anger and counterarguments. In this view, affect and cognition are an "amalgam" that cannot be cleanly separated.

**Core Finding and Conclusion**
The central finding of the meta-analysis was that the **intertwined model provided the best fit for the data**. This result offered powerful, large-scale confirmation of Dillard and Shen's (2005) initial findings. By aggregating the results of numerous studies, Rains demonstrated that psychological reactance is most accurately understood and measured as a latent variable that manifests through a combination of anger and negative cognitions.

**Significance**
This paper is considered a landmark review in the field of communication and persuasion. It solidified the "intertwined model" as the dominant and most empirically supported framework for measuring state reactance. This has allowed researchers to more reliably measure reactance in experimental settings and better understand its role as a mediator in response to freedom-threatening messages, particularly in areas like health communication. The study affirmed that when a persuasive message threatens an individual's freedom, the resulting reactance is an integrated experience of both feeling angry and actively generating counterarguments.
