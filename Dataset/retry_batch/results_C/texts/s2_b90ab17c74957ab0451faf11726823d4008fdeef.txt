## 1. Official Abstract
>In a series of two studies, the authors examined autobiographical memories for the presence of two distinct growth orientations that were hypothesized to correspond differentially to maturity and well-being, which are considered key aspects of "the good life" (L. A. King, 2001). Mature participants tended to emphasize integrative memories, which involve conceptual integration and learning, whereas happy participants focused on intrinsic memories, which are related to humanistic concerns. Both types of growth memories showed a stronger correlation with eudaimonic measures of well-being than with hedonic ones. These growth memories were largely independent of the Big Five personality traits in their relationship with maturity and well-being. Additionally, older participants demonstrated marginally greater maturity and higher well-being, a finding partially explained by their increased tendency to have growth memories. The discussion explores the function of growth memories in the deliberate cultivation of a good life.

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
Full text was accessible for analysis.

### Introduction
The paper introduces the concept of "the good life" as a combination of happiness (social-emotional well-being) and meaning or maturity (social-cognitive maturity). The authors propose that the way people narrate their life stories, specifically their memories of growth, can reveal how they pursue these two different, though related, paths. The study aims to identify two types of "growth memories"—**integrative memories** (focused on learning and understanding complexity) and **intrinsic memories** (focused on humanistic and fulfilling concerns)—and to investigate how they relate to maturity and happiness, respectively.

### Methodology
The research comprised two separate studies:
*   **Study 1:** Involved 125 undergraduate students at Northwestern University.
*   **Study 2:** Involved 51 adults from the Evanston, Illinois area, with a mean age of 51.7 years.

In both studies, participants were asked to narrate significant autobiographical memories, including high points, low points, and turning points in their lives. These narratives were then coded for the presence of two key themes:
1.  **Integrative Themes:** These narratives focused on learning, self-understanding, and making sense of complex life events. The researchers expected this theme to correlate with maturity, as measured by ego development.
2.  **Intrinsic Themes:** These narratives emphasized personal growth, meaningful relationships, and contributing to society. This theme was expected to correlate with well-being and happiness.

Alongside the narrative analysis, participants completed several non-narrative measures, including assessments for ego development (a measure of maturity), subjective well-being (both hedonic and eudaimonic), and the Big Five personality traits.

### Results and Discussion
The findings from the two studies supported the authors' primary hypotheses:
*   **Maturity and Integrative Memories:** Participants with higher levels of ego development (maturity) were more likely to frame their life memories in terms of integration, learning, and conceptual understanding.
*   **Happiness and Intrinsic Memories:** Participants who reported higher levels of happiness and well-being were more likely to tell stories emphasizing intrinsic, humanistic themes of personal growth and connection.
*   **Eudaimonic vs. Hedonic Well-Being:** Both integrative and intrinsic growth memories were more strongly linked to *eudaimonic* well-being (a sense of purpose and meaning) than to *hedonic* well-being (simple happiness or life satisfaction).
*   **Independence from Personality Traits:** The relationship between growth memories and the "good life" (maturity and well-being) was found to be largely independent of the Big Five personality traits. This suggests that life narratives offer a unique level of insight into personality beyond what standard traits can capture.
*   **Age and Growth Memories:** Older participants in the study tended to have higher levels of both maturity and well-being. The research showed that this age-related difference was partly explained by the fact that older adults were more likely to interpret their life experiences through the lens of growth memories.

In conclusion, the paper argues that the way individuals interpret and narrate their past is a key component of living a "good life." Mature and happy people actively construct stories of growth from their experiences, and these narrative practices may play a crucial role in the intentional cultivation of personal development and well-being.
