## 1. Official Abstract
"The distinction between intuitive and analytic thinking is common in psychology. However, while often being quite clear on the characteristics of the two processes ('Type 1' processes are fast, autonomous, intuitive, etc. and 'Type 2' processes are slow, deliberative, analytic, etc.), dual-process theorists have been heavily criticized for being unclear on the factors that determine when an individual will think analytically or rely on their intuition. We address this issue by introducing a three-stage model that elucidates the bottom-up factors that cause individuals to engage Type 2 processing. According to the model, multiple Type 1 processes may be cued by a stimulus (Stage 1), leading to the potential for conflict detection (Stage 2). If successful, conflict detection leads to Type 2 processing (Stage 3), which may take the form of rationalization (i.e., the Type 1 output is verified post hoc) or decoupling (i.e., the Type 1 output is falsified). We tested key aspects of the model using a novel base-rate task where stereotypes and base-rate probabilities cued the same (non-conflict problems) or different (conflict problems) responses about group membership. Our results support two key predictions derived from the model: (1) conflict detection and decoupling are dissociable sources of Type 2 processing and (2) conflict detection sometimes fails. We argue that considering the potential stages of reasoning allows us to distinguish early (conflict detection) and late (decoupling) sources of analytic thought. Errors may occur at both stages and, as a consequence, bias arises from both conflict monitoring and decoupling failures."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis.

### Introduction
The paper addresses a central criticism of dual-process theories: they are often unclear about what triggers analytic (Type 2) thinking to override an initial intuitive (Type 1) response. The authors aim to create a model that specifies the factors determining when an individual will engage in deeper, analytic thought. They propose that a key trigger is the detection of conflict between two or more competing intuitive (Type 1) outputs. The paper sets out to test this by dissociating the cognitive processes involved in detecting a conflict from the subsequent, more effortful process of overriding an intuitive response.

### A Three-Stage Dual-Process Model
The core of the paper is its proposed model, which refines traditional dual-process accounts by breaking down the reasoning event into three stages:

*   **Stage 1: Autonomous Type 1 Processing:** A stimulus or problem cues multiple, autonomous Type 1 processes. These can generate several potential "intuitive" responses, which may come to mind with varying speeds and fluency. The most salient one is termed the Initial Response 1 (IR1).
*   **Stage 2: Conflict Detection:** The key innovation of the model is this monitoring stage. If a secondary intuitive response (IR2) conflicts with the primary one (IR1), the individual may detect this conflict. This detection is considered a "bottom-up" trigger for analytic thought. However, this stage can fail; if no conflict is detected, the individual will likely proceed with their initial intuition without further analysis.
*   **Stage 3: Type 2 Processing (Decoupling or Rationalization):** Successful conflict detection in Stage 2 initiates deliberate, Type 2 processing. This analytic engagement can take two forms:
    *   **Rationalization:** The reasoner focuses on justifying or elaborating on their initial intuitive response (IR1), without seriously considering the conflicting alternative.
    *   **Decoupling:** This is a more effortful process where the reasoner mentally "decouples" from their initial intuition to entertain the alternative response, potentially overriding the initial biased output in favor of a more logical or normative one.

The model posits that errors in reasoning can occur at two distinct points: a failure to detect conflict in Stage 2 (a monitoring failure) or a failure to sufficiently decouple and override the initial intuition in Stage 3 (a decoupling failure).

### Methodology
To test their model, the researchers employed a novel base-rate task. Participants were presented with problems describing a person randomly drawn from a sample of 1000 people. The sample consisted of two groups, for example, 995 nurses and 5 doctors.

*   **Conflict vs. Non-Conflict Problems:** In **conflict problems**, the provided stereotype (e.g., a description of a person that sounds like a typical doctor) was inconsistent with the base-rate probabilities (e.g., 995 nurses vs. 5 doctors). In **non-conflict problems**, the stereotype and base-rates pointed to the same conclusion.
*   **Response Time (RT) Measurement:** The time taken to respond was a key dependent variable. The model predicts that conflict problems should take longer to process than non-conflict problems, even when participants give the intuitive (stereotype-based) answer, because the conflict itself requires additional processing time (Stage 2 activity). The longest response times are expected for correct answers on conflict trials, as this indicates successful conflict detection (Stage 2) followed by successful decoupling (Stage 3).

### Results
The experimental findings supported the key predictions of the three-stage model:

1.  **Evidence for Conflict Detection:** Participants took significantly longer to answer conflict problems compared to non-conflict problems, regardless of whether their final answer was correct or incorrect. This supports the existence of a conflict detection stage (Stage 2) that occurs even when the initial, biased response is not ultimately overridden.
2.  **Dissociation of Conflict Detection and Decoupling:** The results showed that the increase in response time for incorrect answers in conflict trials (indicating conflict detection followed by rationalization or failed decoupling) was different from the much larger increase in response time for correct answers in conflict trials (indicating successful conflict detection and successful decoupling). This provides evidence that conflict detection and decoupling are distinct and dissociable processes.
3.  **Evidence for Conflict Monitoring Failure:** The model allows for the possibility that conflict detection can fail. The data suggested that on some trials, participants responded quickly and intuitively, consistent with a failure to detect the conflict between the stereotype and the base-rate information.

### General Discussion & Conclusion
The authors conclude that their three-stage model provides a more nuanced understanding of when and why people engage in analytic thinking. By separating conflict detection from the act of overriding intuition, the model can account for different types of reasoning errors. Bias can arise not just from a failure to engage in effortful thinking (a decoupling failure), but also from a more fundamental failure to even notice that there is a problem to be solved (a conflict monitoring failure).

The paper argues that this staged approach helps reconcile different perspectives in dual-process theory and provides a clearer framework for predicting when Type 2 processing will be engaged. It highlights that the trigger for "thinking" is often an automatically detected conflict between competing intuitions.
