## 1. Official Abstract
"Research in organizational psychology has shown that commitment to the organization correlates with different criteria of work effectiveness. This paper argues that social psychology and, particularly, the Social Identity Approach to intergroup relations extend the concept of commitment theoretically. Above that, it provides a broader conceptual framework for the understanding of underlying processes in the relation between organizational identification and job-related attitudes and behaviours. This theoretical analysis is completed with a review of empirical findings in different fields of application (group performance, work-related attitudes, group norms). An integrated model of social psychological and organizational psychological terms and processes is proposed and implications for future research and for the management of organizational identification are discussed."

The 2001 paper, "Identification in Organizational Contexts: Linking Theory and Research from Social and Organizational Psychology," by Rolf van Dick, serves as a theoretical bridge between two fields of psychology to better understand an employee's connection to their organization. The central thesis is that the concept of "organizational commitment," traditionally studied in organizational psychology, is enhanced and more deeply understood when integrated with the "Social Identity Approach," a cornerstone of social psychology.

**Key Arguments and Contributions:**

*   **Theoretical Enhancement of Commitment:** The paper argues that while organizational commitment is a useful concept, the Social Identity Approach offers a richer theoretical foundation. Social identity theory posits that a person's sense of self is partly derived from their membership in social groups. Van Dick applies this to the workplace, suggesting that "organizational identification" is a specific form of social identification where an employee's belief about their organization becomes a defining part of their own identity.

*   **Distinguishing Identification from Commitment:** The paper contributes to the differentiation between organizational identification and organizational commitment. While commitment is often seen as an attitude or a bond (e.g., affective, continuance), identification is presented as a more cognitive process of self-definition where the individual and the organization merge in the person's self-concept. It involves integrating the organization's perceived values and goals into one's own identity.

*   **An Integrated Model:** A core contribution of the paper is the proposal of an integrated model that combines terms and processes from both social and organizational psychology. This model aims to explain the underlying mechanisms that link a strong sense of organizational identification to important work-related outcomes.

*   **Review of Empirical Evidence:** The paper supports its theoretical arguments by reviewing empirical findings across several domains:
    *   **Work-Related Attitudes & Behaviors:** It connects identification to outcomes like job satisfaction and a reduced intention to leave the company (turnover). Employees who strongly identify with their organization are more likely to exhibit behaviors that go beyond their formal job requirements, contributing positively to the company's success.
    *   **Group Performance and Norms:** The analysis extends to how identification influences group dynamics, performance, and adherence to group norms.

*   **Components of Identification:** Citing van Dick's work, subsequent research notes that organizational identification is a multi-faceted construct. It includes a cognitive component (the awareness of being a member), an affective component (emotional attachment), an evaluative component (viewing the group positively, like pride), and a behavioral component (acting on behalf of the group).

In summary, van Dick's paper advocates for using the social identity perspective as a powerful lens to understand the relationship between an employee and their organization. It reframes the conversation from simple commitment to a deeper sense of oneness, providing a more robust framework for explaining why employees who identify with their organization are often more satisfied, more motivated, and more willing to contribute to its success.
