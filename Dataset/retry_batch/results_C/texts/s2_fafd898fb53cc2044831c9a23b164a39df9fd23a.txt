### **Synopsis of Full Text**

#### **Introduction and Historical Context**

The paper begins by recounting the origins of the power bases framework, which started at the Research Center for Group Dynamics in the early 1950s. The initial goal was to understand interpersonal influence and the dynamics of group effects on individual behavior. This led to the seminal 1959 paper by John R. P. French Jr. and Bertram Raven, "The Bases of Social Power," which introduced the original five bases of power: Coercive, Reward, Legitimate, Expert, and Referent power. The 2008 paper serves as a capstone, summarizing the evolution of this model over several decades.

#### **The Six Bases of Power**

Raven details the expansion and refinement of the original five bases, most notably with the addition of a sixth, Informational Power. The framework presented in this paper includes:
*   **Coercive Power:** Based on the target's perception that the influencing agent can mediate punishments.
*   **Reward Power:** Based on the perception that the agent can mediate rewards.
*   **Legitimate Power:** Influence stemming from a target's feeling of obligation or responsibility to accept an agent's influence due to their role or position. Raven further breaks this down into subtypes, including legitimacy of position, reciprocity, equity, and dependence.
*   **Expert Power:** Influence based on the target's belief that the agent possesses superior knowledge or ability in a specific area.
*   **Referent Power:** Based on the target's identification with, or desire to be like, the influencing agent.
*   **Informational Power:** Influence based on the content of the communication and the information presented by the agent. This was distinguished from Expert Power, as the influence rests in the logic and evidence of the message itself, rather than the perceived expertise of the source.

The paper emphasizes that these bases are not mutually exclusive and often operate in combination. It also discusses the distinction between personal power bases (Expert and Referent) and position power bases (Coercive, Reward, Legitimate).

#### **The Power/Interaction Model of Interpersonal Influence**

A significant portion of the paper is dedicated to explaining the Power/Interaction Model, which provides a more dynamic view of social influence. This model moves beyond a static list of power bases to describe the entire influence process from the agent's perspective. Key stages of the model include:
1.  **Motivation for Influence:** The agent's reasons for attempting to influence the target.
2.  **Assessment of Available Power Bases:** The agent evaluates which power bases are available and most appropriate for the situation and the target. An unsuccessful influence attempt can result from misjudging the effective power bases available.
3.  **Preparation for and Employment of Influence:** The agent chooses and deploys specific influence tactics based on the chosen power base. The model acknowledges that the agent's choice is influenced by factors like their own motivations (e.g., a desire to be liked might lead to using referent power).
4.  **Effects of the Influence Attempt:** The model considers the outcomes, including not just compliance but also potential resistance or changes in the relationship between agent and target.

#### **Elaboration through Questions and Answers**

The latter part of the paper is a self-interview where Raven addresses a series of probing questions about the model. This section reveals deeper insights:
*   **On the ubiquity of social power:** Raven notes seeing the principles of social power everywhere, from interactions with salespeople and police to medical consultations and advertising.
*   **On differentiating the power bases:** He clarifies the nuances between the bases, for example, distinguishing the "negative" forms of expert and referent power, where a target might do the opposite of what an expert or a disliked role model suggests.
*   **On the choice of power bases:** The model suggests that the selection of a power tactic is a deliberate choice, influenced by the agent's goals and the perceived costs and benefits of using each base. For instance, coercive power might be used under specific conditions, while reward power is more frequently employed to engage people.

This section provides a rich commentary on the decades of research and clarifies the practical applications and theoretical complexities of the power/interaction framework.
