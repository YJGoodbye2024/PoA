## 1. Official Abstract
The official abstract for the working paper version of this research is quoted verbatim below. The published version in *Econometrica* integrates the abstract into the introduction.

"In this paper we apply the theory of decision making with expected utility and non-additive priors to the choice of optimal portfolio. This theory describes the behavior of a rational agent who is averse to pure 'uncertainty' (as well as, possibly, to 'risk'). We study the agent's optimal allocation of wealth between a safe and an uncertain asset. We show that there is a range of prices at which the agent neither buys not sells short the uncertain asset. In contrast the standard theory of expected utility predicts that there is exactly one such price. We also provide a definition of an increase in uncertainty aversion and show that it causes the range of prices to increase."

## 2. Core Insights & Analysis
The full text of the paper was accessible for analysis. Below is a synopsis organized by the paper's original sections.

### 1. Introduction
The paper explores the implications of a decision-making model that generalizes standard expected utility theory by incorporating non-additive probabilities, which allows for a distinction between "risk" (quantifiable) and "uncertainty" (unknown). The authors apply this model, based on the work of Schmeidler and Gilboa, to the problem of optimal investment decisions. Under standard expected utility, an investor will buy a risky asset if its price is below its expected value and sell it if the price is above. In contrast, this paper demonstrates that an uncertainty-averse agent will have a range of prices for a risky asset at which they will choose to hold zero of that asset, neither buying nor selling it. This range is determined by the agent's degree of uncertainty aversion.

### 2. An Example
To illustrate the core concept, the paper presents an example of a risk-neutral agent considering an asset with two potential future values: High (H) or Low (L). The agent's beliefs are represented by a non-additive probability measure where the sum of the probabilities for the two outcomes is less than one, signifying uncertainty aversion. The decision to buy or sell is based on two reservation prices: a buying price (pb) and a selling price (ps). The agent will only buy the asset if the market price *p* is less than *pb* and will only sell it short if *p* is greater than *ps*. Crucially, if the market price falls between *pb* and *ps*, the agent will not trade the asset. This interval of inaction is a direct result of uncertainty aversion and contrasts with standard models where *pb* equals *ps*.

### 3. The General Case
This section formalizes the findings from the example for a general case involving an agent who may be risk-averse (not just risk-neutral) and an asset with a random future value. The authors establish that even for a risk-averse agent, there exists a range of prices where the optimal holding of the uncertain asset is zero. They define two reservation prices, a buying price and a selling price, which create a "price band of inaction." The paper shows that these reservation prices depend on the agent's beliefs and aversion to uncertainty, but not on their attitude towards risk (i.e., the curvature of the utility function) for small investments. This result is described as the "nonadditive analog of the local risk-neutrality result." An increase in uncertainty aversion is shown to widen the gap between the buying and selling reservation prices, leading to a larger range of prices where the agent remains inactive.

### 4. Conclusion
The conclusion summarizes the paper's main finding: incorporating uncertainty aversion into standard portfolio choice models explains why an investor might refuse to trade an asset over a certain range of prices. This behavior, where an agent neither buys nor sells, is a direct consequence of the distinction between risk and uncertainty. The model shows that there is a bid-ask spread for the agent's reservation prices, and this spread widens as uncertainty aversion increases. The authors note that this framework could be applied to other economic problems where uncertainty, as distinct from risk, plays a critical role.
